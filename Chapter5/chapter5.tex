\chapter{A Compact Model of the Dual-Phase Transient}\label{ch:model}

\noindent This chapter turns the current transient characterised in Chapter~\ref{ch:transients} into a compact, circuit-level primitive that the later spiking-network chapters can simulate. The two transient phases are first decoupled experimentally. Substituting the inert gold top electrode with indium tin oxide (\abbrevdef{ITO}) removes the slow conductance decay while leaving the fast rise intact, which localises the decay to the top metal--insulator interface. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (\abbrev{XPS}) then identifies it with proton-driven electrochemical reduction of the \abbrev{ITO} contact. With the mechanism fixed, an empirical \abbrev{SPICE} model of the gold-contacted device is extended with a dispersive trap cascade that reproduces the sigmoidal rise and with explicit leakage (relaxation) elements. Applied to the \abbrev{ITO} devices, the same framework exposes the bare trap-discharge time constant $\tau_{leak}$ directly. The result is a semi-physical model whose single coefficient set generalises across the operating range. The electrode substitution exposes its retention $\tau_{leak}$ for direct measurement, supplying the slow synaptic dynamics that the reward-modulated learning of Chapter~\ref{ch:learning} uses as its eligibility trace. \\

\noindent The empirical \abbrev{SPICE} model that this chapter extends comes from earlier co-authored work on a single gold-contacted device \cite{mannion2023compact}, detailed in Section~\ref{sec:5p-model}. The indium-tin-oxide devices were fabricated within the UCL Electronic Materials and Devices group, where the spectroscopic ellipsometry of the pristine \abbrev{ITO} contact was also performed; the author interpreted the resulting optical constants here. The \abbrev{XPS} depth profiling was performed by collaborators at the University of Oxford. The author interpreted it, designed and carried out the electrode-substitution experiment, developed the dispersive trap-cascade-plus-leakage extension reported here, and fitted the \abbrev{ITO} relaxations from which the eligibility-trace retention $\tau_{leak}$ is obtained.

\section[Electrode Substitution and the Two Phases]{Electrode Substitution and the Two Phases}\label{sec:5p1}

\noindent This section establishes experimentally that the transient is a composite of two distinct processes. Substituting the top electrode isolates the slow decay to the top interface, and the proton-drift hypothesis it motivates is then set out and, in the following section, tested spectroscopically.

\subsection[Top-Electrode Substitution with ITO]{Top-Electrode Substitution with ITO}

\noindent Devices with an indium tin oxide (\abbrev{ITO}) top contact in place of gold showed distinctive stress responses. The bottom contact and silicon oxide (SiOx) layer were unchanged from previous devices. Under electrical stress, the \abbrev{ITO}-contacted device behaved markedly differently from the gold--titanium device. Rather than a smooth, gradual rise in conductance, the response was erratic and chaotic. Aluminium-contacted devices, by contrast, consistently showed no current transients after stress. Any stress-induced conductance increase was more volatile, decaying back to a high-resistance state within a couple of hours.\\

\noindent The \abbrev{ITO}-contacted devices did, however, show partial current transients. A typical transient (Figure \ref{fig:5a}) began with an increase in conductance but did not then decay. Instead the device displayed chaotic, spiking-like behaviour, and prolonged observation typically ended in a switch to a low-resistance state.\\

\noindent Because \abbrev{ITO} is transparent across the visible range, replacing the opaque gold contact with \abbrev{ITO} also makes the active oxide optically accessible. This adds a means of modulating the device that the gold-contacted stack lacks. A preliminary illumination experiment confirms the devices are photoactive. Figure \ref{fig:5b} shows current--voltage sweeps of a representative device from a batch of 25 nominally identical \abbrev{ITO} samples, measured in the dark and under illumination. Under light the current rises by roughly an order of magnitude across most of the range, reaching the largest enhancements ($\sim$15--45$\times$) near 14--16\,V. This enhancement is consistent with photo-excitation of carriers from the same sub-stoichiometric-oxide defect states that mediate dark conduction, and it points to a route towards optically gated memristive synapses.\\

\noindent This optical sensitivity is promising but preliminary. The three sweeps were recorded in close succession, with the current rising from one high-voltage sweep to the next, so the measurement conflates the optical response with a concurrent stress-induced conductance increase. Wavelength and power were also not calibrated. A dedicated study would separate the two and quantify the spectral response, interleaving illuminated and dark references on the same device across calibrated wavelength and intensity. The transparent-electrode platform provides a ready basis for it. The remainder of this chapter concentrates on the electrically driven transient, whose two phases the \abbrev{ITO} substitution helps to separate.\\

\subsection[Optical Constants of the Pristine ITO Contact]{Optical Constants of the Pristine ITO Contact}\label{subsec:ellipsometry}

\noindent Before the device is stressed, the optical state of the as-deposited \abbrev{ITO} contact is worth fixing quantitatively, both to confirm the film is a well-formed transparent conductor and to set a reference against which the later electrochemical reduction is read. The optical constants were measured by spectroscopic ellipsometry on a pristine stack (Semilab SEA analyser, $79^{\circ}$ angle of incidence, over the $450$--$760$\,nm visible range). The measured ellipsometric angles ($\Psi$, $\Delta$) were fitted with a layered optical model in which the \abbrev{ITO} dielectric function combines a Drude free-carrier term with a Lorentz oscillator, returning the wavelength-dependent refractive index $n$ and extinction coefficient $k$.\\

\begin{figure}[htbp!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter5/Figs/ito_optical_dispersion.png}
    \caption[Optical dispersion of the pristine ITO contact.]{Optical dispersion of the pristine \abbrev{ITO} top contact from spectroscopic ellipsometry. The refractive index $n$ (teal, left axis) and extinction coefficient $k$ (navy, right axis) are shown across the measured visible range ($450$--$760$\,nm); the dotted line marks the $632.8$\,nm reference ($n = 2.14$, $k = 0.10$). The transparent, weakly absorbing response is characteristic of a well-formed transparent conducting oxide and defines the pristine reference state of the electrode.}
    \label{fig:5ellip}
\end{figure}

\noindent The model reproduced the measured response with high fidelity (regression $R^2 = 0.999$). The fitted optical constants (Figure~\ref{fig:5ellip}) show the as-deposited \abbrev{ITO} to be transparent and weakly absorbing across the visible range: the refractive index falls from $n \approx 2.29$ at $450$\,nm to $n \approx 2.08$ at $760$\,nm, and the extinction coefficient from $k \approx 0.17$ to $k \approx 0.085$ over the same range ($n = 2.14$, $k = 0.10$ at the $632.8$\,nm HeNe line). These values are characteristic of a well-formed transparent conducting oxide, and the free-carrier (Drude) contribution confirms the degenerate, conductive nature of the pristine film, giving a carrier concentration of order $10^{20}$\,cm$^{-3}$ and a sheet resistance of $\approx260$\,$\Omega$/sq. This optically transparent, low-loss starting state is the baseline against which the chemical and structural changes induced by electrical stress are interpreted: the reduction examined in the next section darkens the film as it loses oxygen, the optical counterpart of departing from this pristine state.\\

\noindent The partial transient in \abbrev{ITO}-contacted devices, with the initial conductance increase but no subsequent slow decay, is a telling result. It shows the full transient to be a composite of at least two distinct, simultaneous physical changes rather than a single continuous process. The decay is exclusive to gold-contacted devices, so the top metal--insulator interface governs it. The initial increase occurs with both electrodes, so it is less sensitive to that interface and likely arises in the bulk oxide or at the bottom contact. That specific interfaces can selectively shape the transient is further supported by tantalum-oxide devices, in which an $Al_2O_3$ buffer layer between the tantalum-oxide bulk and the titanium-nitride electrodes markedly altered the prominence of the transient \cite{tuller2011point}.

\subsection[Proton Drift Hypothesis]{Proton Drift Hypothesis}

\noindent Locating where in the stack the conductance reduction originates is the first step in understanding these mechanisms. The decay disappears precisely when the top electrode is changed, strongly suggesting that its cause lies at the interface between the top electrode and the amorphous silicon dioxide. Given the slow dynamics, the drift of a mobile defect is the likely cause.\\


\noindent Silicon dioxide films are well known to be susceptible to mobile alkali ions such as positively charged sodium and lithium \cite{snow1965ion, yon1966sodium}. As these charges drift and accumulate they shift potential drops at metal--oxide interfaces and modulate barrier heights. Attributing the observed positive space charge to alkali metals is nonetheless improbable. There is no evidence that the samples contain a significant concentration of such ions, which typically occur only as environmental contaminants, and their migration generally requires temperatures above $100^\circ C$ \cite{deal1974current}. As all transients here were observed at room temperature, the mobile space charge must be some other species, both naturally present in the oxide and mobile under ambient conditions.\\

\noindent Temperature modelling of $TaO_x$ devices indicates localised increases of up to $100^\circ C$ near the conductive filament from Joule heating at applied voltages of -0.7V to -1.8V \cite{shen2021experimentally}. Comparable heating during the current transient could facilitate alkali-defect migration, but only within those confined regions.\\

\noindent The proton \cite{hofstein1967proton} is a more likely positive ion mobile at room temperature, an interpretation now supported by extensive work on proton-conducting neuromorphic materials \cite{yuan2024proton}. Ionised hydrogen in silicon dioxide films is repeatedly observed to be stable \cite{vanheusden1998chemical}. Protons trapped within the oxide influence the electronic properties of capacitor devices \cite{vanheusden1999non}, and their long-term stability is shown by repeated cycles of migration between electrodes \cite{warren1997protonic}. They are commonly introduced as hydrogen during oxide growth \cite{vanheusden1998thermally}, with their concentration rising on annealing above $200^\circ C$ \cite{lifshitz1989detection}, and can also enter electronically via electrolysis of water within the device and through radiation \cite{winokur1977field}. Most relevant here, they migrate repeatedly at room temperature \cite{hofstein1967proton, vanheusden1999non}.\\

\noindent The decisive support is on this very platform. A dedicated study of these \abbrev{SiOx} stacks identifies mobile hydrogen as a determinant of the conductance, diffusing to and from the electrode interfaces under the operating field, and notes that concentrations as low as $\sim$2\,at.\% (the level reported for comparable SiO$_2$ films) already modify the oxide's electronic properties measurably \cite{cox2024hydrogen}. Oxygen vacancies are a coupled second species, supplied by the reactive Ti and Mo electrodes that getter oxygen from the oxide and able to be passivated or generated by the mobile hydrogen, so the two cannot be cleanly separated \cite{cox2024hydrogen, mehonic2017intrinsic}. The current itself remains electronic throughout, this mobile charge acting only by screening the interfacial field.\\

\noindent Why, then, do protons accumulate in gold-contacted devices but not in \abbrev{ITO}? Gold is inert and unlikely to be reduced by protons, so their accumulation at the gold interface is expected. \abbrev{ITO}, in contrast, is reduced in the presence of protons, as substantial evidence indicates. Although \abbrev{ITO} contacts are often treated as inert, their reduction depends strongly on electrolyte pH, and electrode reduction has been observed many times in acidic electrolytes \cite{ciocci2021differentiating, senthilkumar2008electrochemical}.\\

\noindent The reduction of \abbrev{ITO} by protons has been demonstrated both in room-temperature electrochemical experiments \cite{wang2003optical} and in \abbrev{ITO} exposed to a hydrogen plasma \cite{banerjee1987degradation}. Applying negative voltages to an \abbrev{ITO} electrode immersed in hydrochloric acid formed metallic-appearing spherical structures at the grain boundaries of the \abbrev{ITO} film \cite{huang2003electrochemical}. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (\abbrevdef{EDS}) \cite{huang2003electrochemical} and X-ray diffraction (\abbrevdef{XRD}) \cite{liu2015important} showed these regions to be depleted of oxygen or to exhibit only indium and tin peaks, strong evidence of their metallic nature. Similar structures appeared in \abbrev{ITO} films exposed to a hydrogen plasma, where Auger spectroscopy again showed lower oxygen concentrations \cite{banerjee1987degradation}.\\

\noindent Reduction of \abbrev{ITO} by protons explains the absence of space-charge accumulation in \abbrev{ITO}-contacted devices. The reduction proceeds by a cathodic half-reaction that consumes the migrating protons,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{In_2O_3 + 6\,H^+ + 6\,e^- \rightarrow 2\,In + 3\,H_2O}, \label{eq:5.redox}
\end{equation}
with the analogous reaction reducing the tin oxide. The standard potential for In$^{3+}$/In is only $-0.34$\,V versus the standard hydrogen electrode \cite{liu2015important}. The strong cathodic bias on the top contact therefore drives the electrode potential well below it and makes the reduction thermodynamically accessible.\\

\noindent The consequence for the transient is direct. Because the protons are consumed as water rather than accumulating, no positive space charge builds up, and the slow decay branch is removed. The accompanying structural changes in the electrode may also explain the more erratic current--time response of \abbrev{ITO} devices.\\

\noindent Because structural changes can occur in both the oxide and the metal contacts, the specific role of each in modulating conductance is hard to determine. Fabricating and characterising devices with a range of contact materials would help. Any difference in behaviour between them could be ascribed to the metal or the metal--insulator interface, while any effect common to all could be attributed to the oxide. Varying the oxide thickness would test the oxide's role further, since a thickness dependence would support it. Caution is needed, however, because changing the thickness also alters the current density and so may affect the interfaces. Fabricating devices with different oxide thicknesses and a range of metal contacts remains a promising direction.\\


\noindent If the transient indeed results from two separate changes, engineering a device to exhibit only one in isolation would be strong evidence that the mechanisms are separable. Changing the top contact to conductive \abbrev{ITO} achieves exactly this. The device shows the initial increase but, in place of the slow recovery, grows progressively noisier until breakdown (Figure \ref{fig:5c}).\\

    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter5/Figs/ito_current_time.png}
    \caption[The current-time response for a device with a conductive ITO top electrode.]{Current--time response of a conductive \abbrev{ITO}-contacted device under step potentials on the top contact (bias amplitude $|V|$ by colour). Unlike the gold-contacted devices, the \abbrev{ITO} device shows the initial conductance change without the slow recovery, and grows progressively noisier at higher bias as it approaches electrical instability.     \label{fig:5c}

\noindent The \abbrev{ITO} device was stressed much as the gold devices were. Initially the devices show only capacitive currents and a very high resistance, and a constant-current stressing procedure then makes them more conductive. After a relaxation period a repeatable transient appears, provided the voltage is held briefly enough to avoid breakdown. A comparable phenomenon is documented in a range of oxides \cite{moon2019rram} and is often used to emulate short-term synaptic potentiation \cite{zhang2017emulating, chang2011short}. This slower dynamic may also corroborate earlier work \cite{meyer2005oxygen} attributing slow dynamics to oxygen vacancies, since the \abbrev{ITO} contact may exchange oxygen with the vacancy more readily than the inert gold contact.\\

\noindent An alternative hypothesis is that the Au contact diffuses through the oxide while the \abbrev{ITO} contact does not. Gold can form conductive bridges between contacts through a thin film of ZnO \cite{peng2012resistive} and is known to diffuse in silicon dioxide films \cite{madams1974migration}, so this is conceivable. However, Transmission Electron Microscopy (\abbrev{TEM}) of the devices studied here shows no observable gold filaments, casting doubt on the hypothesis \cite{mehonic2017intrinsic}.\\


\noindent A further explanation for the difference between Au and \abbrev{ITO} contacts is a difference in work function. Although not measured on these samples, gold's work function is reported between 4.9 and 5.2 electronvolts (eV) \cite{tran2019anisotropic}, whereas thin films of indium tin oxide (\abbrev{ITO}) measure between 4.25 and 4.28 eV \cite{schlaf2001work}, a difference of about 1 eV. Such a gap would offset the band alignments at the contact and oxide and could change which oxide traps electrons are injected into. As discussed, the change in conductance dynamics with the top electrode strongly suggests that proton migration drives the slower decay in device current.

\section[XPS Evidence for Electrode Reduction]{XPS Evidence for Electrode Reduction}\label{sec:5p2}

\noindent X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (\abbrevdef{XPS}), also known as electron spectroscopy for chemical analysis (\abbrevdef{ESCA}), is a widely used surface-analysis technique in materials science \cite{fadley2010x}. It reveals the composition and electronic structure of matter \cite{oswald2006x}, and yields further chemical information such as binding energy and oxidation states. \\

\subsection[Principles of XPS Depth Profiling]{Principles of XPS Depth Profiling}


\noindent Photoelectron spectroscopy rests on the photoelectric effect, in which a flux of photons ejects electrons from a material \cite{margaritondo1988100}. X-rays irradiate the sample and eject electrons whose energies are collected to infer its composition and electronic structure. Because the exact core-level binding energy depends on the chemical environment of the emitting atom, elements are identified from electron energy and chemical bonding from energy shifts. The analysis is conducted in ultra-high vacuum, both to satisfy the technique and to lengthen the photoelectron mean free path and minimise contamination. Depth profiling is achieved by ion sputtering \cite{oswald2006x}.\\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
\centering    
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Chapter5/Figs/xps_depth_profile_setup.png}
\caption[Schematic of the XPS depth-profiling experiment.]{Schematic of the \abbrev{XPS} depth-profiling experiment on the layered \abbrev{ITO} (50\,nm) / SiO$_x$ (25\,nm) / Mo (150\,nm) stack inside the ultra-high-vacuum chamber. X-ray radiation ($h\nu$) ejects photoelectrons ($e^-$), which are focused by the lens system onto a hemispherical energy analyser and dispersed by kinetic energy onto an electron multiplier to build up the \abbrev{XPS} spectrum (counts versus binding energy). An Ar$^+$ ion beam sputter-etches successive layers, so the analysis proceeds with increasing depth into the stack. Layer thicknesses are drawn for clarity and are not to scale.}
\label{fig:5e}
\end{figure}

\noindent Figure \ref{fig:5e} shows the system schematically: an X-ray source irradiates the sample in vacuum, and electronic lenses feed the ejected photoelectrons to a hemispherical energy analyser that disperses them by kinetic energy to build up the spectrum. To eject a core electron the incident radiation must supply at least its binding energy $E_b$, so the measured kinetic energy $E_k$ follows from the X-ray energy $h\nu$ (with $h$ Planck's constant and $\nu$ the photon frequency), the sample work function $\phi$, and the binding energy:
\begin{align}
E_k = h\nu - E_b - \phi \label{eq:5.1}
\end{align}

\noindent Since $h\nu$ is known, $E_b$ is read from $E_k$, and because $E_b$ varies with element and chemical state, distinct peaks characterise the sample composition. The technique is surface-sensitive. X-rays penetrate deeply, but only electrons within the short escape depth (0.5--2\,nm) leave without heavy scattering \cite{oswald2006x}. Depth profiling extends this into the stack by adding an ion-beam source that mills the sample sequentially as the composition is characterised \cite{geng2002xps}, though the energetic ions can themselves alter the composition with depth.


\subsection[Validating ITO Reduction Under Stress]{Validating ITO Reduction Under Stress}

\noindent To test the hypothesis that the indium tin oxide (\abbrev{ITO}) top electrode, unlike the inert gold contacts, is chemically unstable and undergoes electrochemical reduction under electrical stress, X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (\abbrev{XPS}) depth profiling was performed. The analysis compared a pristine device stack with a stressed one biased under the conditions known to induce erratic conducting behaviour. The stack consisted of \abbrev{ITO} (50 nm) / SiOx (25 nm) / Mo (150 nm).\\

The pristine sample (Figure \ref{fig:5i}a) shows a gradual transition between the metal-oxide (M-O) and silicon-oxide (SiOx) species, but the stressed sample (Figure \ref{fig:5i}b) is markedly different, its species cross-over points occurring earlier in the etch sequence. Since the initial \abbrev{ITO} thickness was identical (50 nm), this faster removal of the top layer is puzzling if the material properties are assumed constant. It fits the reduction hypothesis, however: reducing the \abbrev{ITO} ceramic to a metallic-rich, sub-stoichiometric layer softens it, raising its susceptibility to ion milling and its sputter rate.\\

Figure \ref{fig:5j} shows the BE shift relative to the surface state. As the profile reaches the interface, the Indium (In $3d_{5/2}$) peak in the stressed sample deviates markedly from the pristine control, whereas the Silicon (Si $2p$) peak shifts little or not at all. The indium shift indicates a change in chemical state, specifically an oxidation state or oxygen stoichiometry consistent with reduction.\\

\noindent Taken together, the \abbrev{XPS} data spectroscopically validate the proposed physical model. The intermediate oxygen states, the binding-energy shifts in indium, and the accelerated etch rate of the top electrode all confirm that the \abbrev{ITO} layer is not inert: it undergoes electrochemical reduction, likely by protons accumulated under negative bias, producing the structural instabilities and chaotic electrical behaviour seen in the device characterisation.

\section[Compact SPICE Model]{Compact SPICE Model}\label{sec:5p-model}

\noindent This section develops empirical models that track the rates of conductance increase and decrease separately. Their aim is to quantify the rate of change in conductance, leaving a full physical model to later discussion. The framework follows the canonical criteria for compact memristor models: well-posedness, simulation capability, qualitative asymptotic similarity, predictive ability, and structural stability \cite{woo2024localized}.\\

\noindent The dual-mechanism circuit that follows was first introduced as a black-box \abbrev{SPICE} model fitted to a single gold-contacted device over 0.7--1.0\,V, in which the mobile species was deliberately left as a ``generic'' space charge of unidentified origin \cite{mannion2023compact}. That work was explicit about three limitations: the physical mechanism was unverified, the ion species was unidentified, and the model handled only single step potentials. The present chapter takes that empirical core as its starting point and addresses each limitation in turn.\\

\noindent The electrode-substitution experiment of Section~\ref{sec:5p1} and the \abbrev{XPS} evidence of Section~\ref{sec:5p2} identify the mobile species as a proton and attribute the slow decay to its electrochemical reduction of the top contact, replacing the generic space charge with a physically grounded mechanism. The model is then extended with a diode-based interface, a sequential trap cascade that captures the sigmoidal conductance rise, and explicit relaxation (leakage) elements, so that it responds to arbitrary pulse trains rather than single steps and generalises across 0.8--1.5\,V from a single coefficient set.\\

\noindent The empirical formulation of the earlier model is therefore retained and credited; the physical interpretation, the input generality, and the extended operating range are the contributions developed here.

\subsection[Dual-Mechanism Empirical Formulation]{Dual-Mechanism Empirical Formulation}

\noindent The relaxation transients are captured empirically as the product of two time-dependent terms, a charging term $f_{inc}(t)$ that rises from 0 to 1 and a decay term $f_{dec}(t)$ that defines the eventual current envelope \cite{mannion2023compact}:
\begin{align}
    I(t) = f_{inc}(t)\, f_{dec}(t), \quad f_{inc}(t) \in [0, 1], \quad f_{dec}(t) \in \mathbb{R} \label{eq:5.2}
\end{align}
The two factors are realised by the \abbrev{SPICE} circuit of Figure \ref{fig:5k}: a fixed channel resistance $R_0$ in series with two time-varying voltage sources, $V_n(t)$ (red sub-circuit) and $V_{sc}(t)$ (blue sub-circuit). Three observations justify the split. The conductance rise is much faster than the decay, it is far more volatile (millisecond versus minute-to-hour reset), and the decay can be eliminated by changing the top electrode material \cite{mannion2022current}, indicating two physically distinct mechanisms acting in parallel.\\

\noindent Fits are evaluated by inspecting residuals (centred at zero with no systematic trend) and by the coefficient of determination $R^2 = 1 - SS_{res}/SS_{tot}$, with $SS_{tot} = \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2$ and $SS_{res} = \sum_i (y_i - f_i)^2$. Because nominally good fits can hide large parameter uncertainty, 90\% confidence bounds on each fitted parameter are also reported, and models that yield tighter bounds are preferred when the underlying parameter excursions are small.\\

\begin{figure}[htbp!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[height=0.50\textheight]{Chapter5/Figs/spice_empirical_model.png}
    \caption[Empirical SPICE model.]{Empirical \abbrev{SPICE} model. (A) The composite circuit: a channel resistance $R_0$ in series with two time-varying voltage sources, $V_n(t)$ (red) and $V_{sc}(t)$ (blue), driven by the input $V(t)$. (B) The charge-trapping sub-circuit generating $V_n(t)$, which falls from $V_{n0}$ over time, increasing the device current. (C) The space-charge sub-circuit generating $V_{sc}(t)$, which rises from zero over time, reducing the current.}
    \label{fig:5k}
\end{figure}

\noindent The fast rise in conductance is attributed to charge trapping at a Schottky-like interface barrier \cite{cowley1965surface,sze2021physics}. The devices are not always rectifying, but the silicon-rich filament forms an effective metal--semiconductor junction with the contact, and analogous barriers are reported in oxide memristors \cite{hansen2015double}. The barrier is represented by the time-dependent voltage $V_n(t)$ across capacitor $C_n$, initialised to $V_{n0}$ and discharged toward an equilibrium $V_{n\infty}$ by the voltage-controlled current source
\begin{align}
I_n(t) = \alpha \left[ V_n(t) - V_{n\infty} \right], \label{eq:5.3}
\end{align}
where $\alpha$ has units of conductance and represents the carrier-trapping probability.\\

\noindent The slow decay (tens to hundreds of seconds, fully reversible on bias reversal) is too slow to be electronic trapping and is instead attributed to drift of positively charged defects \cite{wang2006oxygen,vanka2022hydrogen}. The \abbrev{XPS} evidence in the previous section, together with the ambient-temperature mobility, identifies these as protons rather than oxygen vacancies \cite{cox2024hydrogen,hofstein1967proton,vanheusden1999non}. Conduction itself remains filamentary through the silicon-rich percolation paths visible in C-\abbrev{AFM} \cite{buckwell2015conductance}, with negligible ionic current \cite{meyer2005oxygen}, so the protons act only as a modulating space charge. Under bias they accumulate at an inert gold electrode, screen the applied potential, and reduce the field across the filament. Replacing gold with \abbrev{ITO} suppresses the decay because protons electrochemically reduce the \abbrev{ITO} instead of piling up against it \cite{mannion2022current}. This screening is captured by the voltage $V_{sc}(t)$ across the blue sub-circuit, charged by
\begin{align}
I_{sc}(t) = \mu \left[ V_{scmax} - V_{sc}(t) \right] \left[ V(t) - V_{sc}(t) \right], \label{eq:5.4}
\end{align}
where the driving term is the effective potential $V(t) - V_{sc}(t)$, the bracketed factor $V_{scmax} - V_{sc}(t)$ enforces saturation at the steady-state screening voltage $V_{scmax}$, and $\mu$ (units $A/V^2$) absorbs the proton mobility together with the geometric and material constants. The two sub-circuits act in series on the channel resistance $R_0$ to give the composite model of Figure \ref{fig:5k}. \\

\noindent In each state equation the node capacitance enters only through the products that fix the time constants ($R_{leak}C$ for the leakage, and the charging rate divided by $C$). $C$ and the resistances are therefore not separately identifiable from a current transient, only their products are. The capacitances $C_n$ and $C_{sc}$ are accordingly normalised to unity, a units convention rather than a physical plate capacitance, so that each fitted resistance is read directly as its node time constant in seconds. The resistance and voltage-bias parameters are obtained by fitting the experimental transients, and because the channel is non-Ohmic, $R_0$ is treated as voltage-dependent at the meta-parameter stage below.\\


\noindent The base model carries six parameters, all fitted to current transients recorded on a single device from 0.7\,V to 1.1\,V. Each trial was first fitted individually using MATLAB's \texttt{fminsearch} (Nelder--Mead simplex \cite{lagarias1998convergence}) against a mean-squared-error objective. Re-optimising per voltage drove the mean error across the transient to 1.9\%, with absolute residuals below 5\,nA in every case. The worst case (13\% peak error at the 0.7\,V onset) was dominated by noise on the small measured currents. A persistent low-amplitude oscillation in the residuals shows that the present functional form misses some transient dynamics, but the per-voltage fit is only a diagnostic step. The end goal is a generalised model whose single coefficient set spans the full voltage range. One limitation should be noted. The gold-device coefficient set and the field-acceleration laws derived from it (Tables~\ref{table:5b}--\ref{table:5c}) are calibrated on this single device across voltage, so they capture its bias dependence but not the device-to-device spread. The across-run statistics reported here are confined to the \abbrev{ITO} retention study below: 50 of 70 held-bias workbooks pass the primary $R^2\geq0.80$ threshold, while a relaxed $R^2\geq0.70$ sensitivity reproduces the historical count of 53. Device identity is unavailable, so workbook/run, not device, is the experimental unit.\\

\noindent To generalise, each per-trial parameter is replaced by a closed-form function of the applied voltage $V$. These fitted expressions, the meta-parameters, are reported in the upper block of Table \ref{table:5b}. Of the six, only $V_{n\infty}$ shows no clear voltage dependence and is held constant at the across-trials mean of 0.35\,V. Substituting these expressions into the \abbrev{SPICE} circuit yields a black-box model that reproduces the 0.7--1.0\,V transients from one coefficient set. The generalised fit is slightly looser than per-voltage refits but needs no re-fitting at each bias point.\\

\noindent At 1.1\,V the generalised model breaks down, under-predicting the device current at the transient onset. This is attributed to electrical stress in the oxide at higher voltages and currents, previously shown to increase device conductivity \cite{mannion2023unipolar}. The discharge current of equation (\ref{eq:5.3}) is therefore augmented with a current-dependent term representing the dynamic creation of additional trap sites at the Schottky interface:
\begin{align}
    I_n(t) = \alpha \left[ V_n(t) - V_{n\infty} \right] - \sigma I(t). \label{eq:5.5}
\end{align}
Here $\sigma$ is a fitted coefficient weighting the stress-driven trap-creation contribution; it scales the measured device current $I(t)$ (in amperes) into the node-charging current $I_n$ of the unit-capacitance state equation (the $C_n=1$ normalisation introduced above, under which the source terms set the node time constants directly), and so carries that conversion factor rather than being dimensionless, which is why its fitted value is numerically large ($\sigma = 5.61 \times 10^5$). Although the term markedly improves the fit to the first half of the 1.1\,V transient, it breaks the generalisation because trap creation is negligible at lower voltages. It is therefore retained only as a diagnostic for the high-bias regime and dropped from the generalised model below.\\

\noindent The base model therefore generalises across 0.7--1.0\,V, where trap creation is slow enough to neglect over the transient. At 1.1\,V and above, the higher field and current density accelerate trap generation, requiring the stressing term of Equation~(\ref{eq:5.5}). This term is not validated well beyond 1.1\,V, and further mechanisms such as Joule heating or avalanche breakdown would likely be needed at higher bias still.\\

\subsection[Dispersive Trap-Cascade Extension and Relaxation]{Dispersive Trap-Cascade Extension and Relaxation}\label{sec:5p3}

\noindent The empirical model of Section~\ref{sec:5p-model} captures the transient with a single charge-trapping state, but two limitations remain. A single trapping time constant produces a purely concave (saturating-exponential) rise, whereas the measured transients rise \emph{sigmoidally} at low bias. The model also responds only to single step potentials. Both are addressed here by (i) replacing the single trapping state with a short \emph{cascade} of sequential trap states, and (ii) adding leakage (relaxation) elements so the device responds to arbitrary pulse trains. The resulting circuit is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:5l}.\\

\noindent The cascade is motivated by the shape of the rise itself. Fitting the measured gold-device rise to a stretched/compressed-exponential (Kohlrausch--Williams--Watts) form, $\Phi_r(t) = 1 - \exp[-(t/\tau_r)^{\beta_{fill}}]$, returns a compression exponent $\beta_{fill} = 1.95 \pm 0.30$ across all six voltages and both repeat trials. $\beta_{fill}$ is thus \emph{bias-independent}, with $\beta_{fill}>1$. This compressed ($\beta_{fill}>1$, sigmoidal) form is the opposite of the $\beta<1$ stretched form associated with classical dispersive band-tail transport \cite{scher1975anomalous,kakalios1987stretched}, and a bias-independent shape exponent of this kind is the property exploited by compact trap-based models of amorphous-semiconductor devices \cite{libsch1993bias,kim2022compact}.\\

\noindent A compressed-exponential rise with $\beta_{fill}\approx2$ is consistent with a short effective cascade of first-order filling states. In an idealised Erlang representation the effective exponent rises from approximately 1.86 at $k=3$ to 2.21 at $k=4$ \cite{vankampen2007stochastic}; the historical averaged-trace estimate therefore lies in a $k\approx3$--4 representation band. This mapping does \emph{not} uniquely observe three or four microscopic trap stages. The full grouped fit used for predictive model comparison gives a different compression estimate ($\beta_{fill}=2.64$), illustrating its sensitivity to preprocessing, fit window and objective. The cascade depth is consequently treated as a compact state-space representation, consistent with but not proof of the trap-assisted tunnelling picture of Chapter~\ref{ch:transients} \cite{jimenez2001physical}.\\

    \centering

(A) The applied bias fills a short cascade of independent trap states $v_{n1}\!\to\!v_{n2}\!\to\!\cdots\!\to\!v_{nk}$ at a field-accelerated rate $\alpha(V)$, each leaked to ground through its own $R_{leak}$; the final stage $v_{nk}$ sets the branch conductance, producing the compressed-exponential ($\beta_{fill}\approx2$, $k\approx3$) sigmoidal rise. (B) The space-charge branch drives the slower current decay, with $R_{leak}$ providing relaxation when the input is removed.}
    \label{fig:5l}
\end{figure}

\noindent The trapping branch is therefore modelled as a chain of $k$ sequential states, governed by a coupled system of first-order ordinary differential equations (\abbrevdef{ODE}s). The applied bias drives the first state at a field-accelerated rate $\alpha(V)$, each subsequent state is charged by the one before it, and every state leaks toward zero through $R_{leak}$:
\begin{align}
C\,\frac{d v_{n1}}{dt} &= \alpha(V)\,\big[ V_{nmax} - v_{n1} \big] - \frac{v_{n1}}{R_{leak}}, \label{eq:5.6} \\
C\,\frac{d v_{ni}}{dt} &= \alpha(V)\, \frac{v_{n,i-1}}{V_{nmax}}\big[ V_{nmax} - v_{ni} \big] - \frac{v_{ni}}{R_{leak}}, \quad i = 2,\dots,k, \label{eq:5.7}
\end{align}
where the final stage $v_{nk}$ sets the conductance of the trapping branch and $V_{nmax}$ is the saturated trap occupancy. The charging rate rises with bias as the field-accelerated trap emission/capture rate, physically of the Poole--Frenkel form $\alpha(V) \propto \exp(\gamma\sqrt{V})$ \cite{frenkel1938pre}. Over the narrow 0.8--1.5\,V operating window this field acceleration is captured empirically by the exponential-in-$V$ laws fitted below (Equations~\ref{eq:5.8}--\ref{eq:5.9}), so the bias dependence is carried entirely by $\alpha(V)$ and the relaxation time constants, with no separate diode-injection term. The space-charge branch is retained from the empirical model (Equation~\ref{eq:5.4}) with an added $R_{leak}$ relaxation term. Equivalently and more compactly, the whole trapping rise is the stretched-exponential kernel $\Phi_r(t)=1-\exp[-(t/\tau_r)^{\beta_{fill}}]$ used for the generalised fit below.\\

\noindent Beyond reproducing the single-step transients it was fitted to, the extended model is now driven by structured spike inputs to demonstrate two functional behaviours: rate habituation (Figure~\ref{fig:5n}) and short-term memory (Figure~\ref{fig:5o}). These are \emph{demonstrations} of the fitted model, not fits to additional measurements. The time-constant laws $\tau_r$ and $\tau_d$ are fitted to the gold-device transients above; $k=3$ and $\beta_{fill}=2$ are frozen compact-representation choices, with $k=4$ retained as a later sensitivity rather than a microscopic identification. The demonstration otherwise sets only the input protocol and the regime-selecting rates (state capacitances held at $C=1$, currents normalised).\\

\noindent The first behaviour validates the model against the homeostatic response measured in Chapter~\ref{ch:transients} (Figure~\ref{fig:4l}). Driven by the same $1\,\text{Hz}\to10\,\text{Hz}\to1\,\text{Hz}$ rate protocol, the model reproduces it (Figure~\ref{fig:5n}). The current settles to a suppressed steady state under sustained high-rate input and recovers when the rate falls. The mechanism is the fitted timescale separation $\tau_r \ll \tau_d$, not an added ingredient. The fast trap cascade sets a rate-independent baseline while the slow space-charge branch integrates the spike rate, suppressing the current between closely spaced spikes and bleeding off when they are sparse. Because the device constants (seconds to minutes) far exceed the inter-spike interval, the spike train enters as its mean rate, the quantity that drives habituation. The demonstration keeps the fitted $k=3$, $\beta_{fill}\approx2$ cascade and a trap leak of $\approx8$\,s. The only tuned settings are a large trap-charging gain ($3.0$) for the saturating baseline, a slow space-charge rate ($0.05$) and relaxation ($0.06$) for the integrating depression branch, and a below-unity suppression depth ($0.85$) matching the partial suppression measured in Figure~\ref{fig:4l}.\\

\noindent This habituation is the depression-dominated face of the model, set by the slow space-charge branch. Its complementary, potentiation-dominated face is a short-term memory set by the trap cascade and its leakage $\tau_{leak} = R_{leak}C$. That face is taken up in Section~\ref{sec:5p-ito}, once the leakage has been measured directly on the \abbrev{ITO} devices, since $\tau_{leak}$ is the very parameter the short-term memory sweeps.

\subsection[Extension to ITO Devices]{Extension to ITO Devices}\label{sec:5p-ito}

\noindent The model developed above is parameterised on the gold-contacted device. Applying it to the \abbrev{ITO}-contacted devices tests how far the framework transfers across the electrode substitution of Section~\ref{sec:5p1}. The model carries three well-separated timescales, and the \abbrev{ITO} relaxation must be matched to the right one. The slowest is the space-charge (proton) decay $\tau_d$, of order hundreds of seconds (Equation~\ref{eq:5.9}). Next is the trap-\emph{filling} rise $\tau_r$ (Equation~\ref{eq:5.8}), of order ten seconds. The fastest is the leakage time constant $\tau_{leak}=R_{leak}C$ introduced above, which governs how quickly each trap state \emph{discharges} once the bias is removed. \\

\noindent As established in Section~\ref{sec:5p1}, the \abbrev{ITO} contact removes the slow proton decay, and consistent with this, none of the measured \abbrev{ITO} relaxations fall on the $\tau_d$ timescale. What remains is a much faster relaxation, of order one second (primary-QC median $1.390$\,s). This is neither the proton decay nor the trap-filling rise (both an order of magnitude or more slower) but the leakage time constant $\tau_{leak}$. It is the trap states discharging through $R_{leak}$ once the stress is removed, exactly the relaxation that the gold device's short-term-memory behaviour (Figure~\ref{fig:5o}) also exercises. Identifying the \abbrev{ITO} relaxation with $\tau_{leak}$ rather than a new mechanism keeps the extension within the existing two-branch model. \abbrev{ITO} exposes a fast residual relaxation once the proton branch is absent; the present data do not by themselves demonstrate electrode tuning of its retention.\\

\noindent This fast \abbrev{ITO} relaxation is fitted to a stretched exponential,
\begin{equation}
    I(t) = A\,\exp\!\big[-(t/\tau_{leak})^{\beta_{leak}}\big] + I_0, \qquad \beta_{leak} < 1, \label{eq:5.10}
\end{equation}
the discharge-face counterpart of the compressed-exponential rise. An auditable refit of the frozen January 2025 cohort retains 50 of 70 held-bias records at $R^2\geq0.80$. The stretched form describes them markedly better than a single exponential (median $R^2=0.982$ versus $0.855$), with $\beta_{leak}=0.470\pm0.090$ and median $\tau_{leak}=1.390$\,s. Lowering only the QC threshold to 0.70 retains 53 records but leaves these summaries essentially unchanged. A stretched exponent $\beta_{leak}<1$ is the canonical signature of \emph{dispersive} relaxation over a distribution of release times \cite{scher1975anomalous,kakalios1987stretched}. Because device identity is unavailable, these are workbook/run summaries and should not be described as a 50-device population. \\

\noindent The fitted mean is close to the room-temperature exponent $\beta = 0.45 \pm 0.05$ reported for stretched-exponential relaxation in hydrogenated amorphous silicon, where $\beta=T/T_0$ with $T_0\approx600$\,K \cite{kakalios1987stretched}. Under the corresponding multiple-trapping interpretation, $\beta_{leak}=k_BT/E_0$, the primary-QC mean implies a band-tail scale $E_0\approx55$\,meV at room temperature. This is a physical consistency argument, not a unique identification of the release mechanism. \\

\noindent These relaxations are all recorded under a \emph{held} subthreshold bias, so the median is not a field-free discharge constant. The frozen refit gives the descriptive field law
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{leak}(V) \approx 2.55\,e^{-0.665\sqrt{V}}\ \text{s}. \label{eq:5.11}
\end{equation}
Its zero-bias intercept is $2.55$\,s with a workbook-bootstrap 95\% interval $[1.73,3.85]$\,s. This is a held-bias extrapolation, not a direct zero-field measurement and not a lower bound guaranteed to bracket the operating retention. The stretched-exponential scale is reported as fitted; no conversion to a mean lifetime or offset-based kinetic correction is applied. \\

\noindent A field-broadened release-rate distribution is compatible with the observed dispersion, but the present fit cannot separate it from refilling under the sensing bias. Nor does it establish how $\beta_{leak}$ behaves exactly at zero field. The two separately acquired 1\,mV reads below are consequently reported as supplementary direct-retention evidence rather than used to fit or validate a field law. \\

\noindent The $\sqrt{V}$ form is the field signature of barrier-lowered trap emission, the process that empties a trap back to the band, as opposed to the trap-assisted tunnelling that carries the steady-state current between traps (Chapter~\ref{ch:fab}). The two are not cleanly separable, since the field-lowered barrier is plausibly the same one a field-assisted trap-assisted hop crosses. As noted in Chapter~\ref{ch:transients}, $\sqrt{V}$ alone does not separate Poole--Frenkel from Schottky emission, and on a filament the unknown high-field length precludes the dielectric-constant test that would, so the assignment is provisional and a low-field temperature (Arrhenius) sweep would settle it. The identification of the trace does not depend on it. The stretched form with a field-tunable constant holds whichever barrier-lowering mechanism applies. \\

\noindent The two electrodes therefore expose complementary faces of the same dispersive trap physics (Figure~\ref{fig:5r}). The gold device reveals the compressed trap-filling rise ($\tau_r$, $\beta_{fill}\approx2$), and the \abbrev{ITO} device the stretched trap-discharge relaxation ($\tau_{leak}$, held-bias $\beta_{leak}\approx0.5$). With the rate captured by the measured $\sqrt{V}$ law of Equation~\ref{eq:5.11}, the \abbrev{ITO} extension supplies both faces of the trap physics with a parameterised rate law rather than a qualitative confirmation.\\

\noindent An earlier analysis treated the fitted offset $I_0$ as a calibrated occupancy floor and used it to rescale held-bias lifetimes. That inference is withdrawn: the offset also contains background and readout contributions, so it cannot identify filling and emission rates without an independent calibration. Synthetic floor-independence checks now require the fitted stretched-exponential scale to remain unchanged when the offset changes. The fill--emission balance remains useful as a model,
\begin{align}
\frac{dv}{dt} &= f\,(V_{max}-v) - g\,v, \qquad f = \alpha\,d(V),\quad g = 1/\tau_{leak}, \label{eq:5.12}
\end{align}
with $f$ the field-driven filling rate and $g$ the emission rate. Its idealised steady state is
\begin{align}
\rho \equiv \frac{v_{ss}}{V_{max}} = \frac{f}{f+g}. \label{eq:5.13}
\end{align}
It motivates the homeostatic transfer below, but $\rho$ is a latent state fraction and is not equated to the fitted current offset. No second field-free-retention estimate is claimed from these held-bias traces.\\

\noindent This same balance has a rate dependence that the later chapters draw on directly. Under a sustained pre-synaptic spike train the device constants (seconds) exceed the inter-spike interval, so the input enters as its mean rate $r$ (as for the habituation of Figure~\ref{fig:5n}): the field-driven filling rate is modelled as $f=\kappa r$, with per-spike charging increment $\kappa$, while $g=1/\tau_{leak}$. The steady-state occupancy of Equation~\ref{eq:5.12} is $v_{ss}/V_{max}=f/(f+g)=\rho$. Because the accumulated charge screens the conduction (Section~\ref{sec:5p-model}), the available steady-state conductance reads the unscreened complement,
\begin{align}
G_{ss}(r) = G_{max}\left(1 - \frac{v_{ss}}{V_{max}}\right) = \frac{G_{max}}{1 + r/r_{th}}, \qquad r_{th} = \frac{1}{\kappa\,\tau_{leak}}, \label{eq:5.15}
\end{align}
a rate-divisive gain that falls once the input rate exceeds $r_{th}$. A single first-order screening state gives the unit-exponent sigmoid $1/(1+r/r_{th})$; additional states would change the response. The threshold depends jointly on retention and the unmeasured charging gain $\kappa$, so it is device-inspired rather than parameter-free from the present data. This steady-state construction motivates the hardware-aware homeostatic regulariser of Chapter~\ref{ch:bio}. The same balance, linearised about a set point, motivates the activity-stabilising rule of Chapter~\ref{ch:learning}; neither simulation is an on-device implementation.\\

\noindent A direct \emph{drive-off} measurement at exactly zero bias would isolate emission, but the conduction readout weakens as the bias falls. Two separately acquired $|V|=1$\,mV records therefore provide supplementary near-zero evidence (Figure~\ref{fig:5u}). Their fitted constants are $4.31$ and $9.20$\,s with $\beta_{leak}=0.836$ and 0.814. They are much less dispersive than the held-bias cohort, but two workbooks cannot establish a population or an exact zero-field law. A temperature sweep or contactless charge probe would be needed to separate thermal and field contributions and determine the true $\tau_{leak}(0)$ without a sensing bias.\\

\noindent The same relaxation underlies a complementary capability: a short-term memory of recent input (Figure~\ref{fig:5o}). Across spike bursts the trapped charge builds during a burst and decays between bursts at a rate set by $\tau_{leak}=R_{leak}C$, so a short $\tau_{leak}$ forgets all but the most recent activity while a long one integrates across bursts. The $2$ and $10$\,s curves are illustrative model settings, chosen with the frozen $k=3$, $\beta_{fill}=2$ representation. They span the held-bias median, the $2.55$\,s zero-bias extrapolation and the two supplementary near-zero reads rather than constituting interpolated device settings. The retention rides the electronic trap-discharge branch, not the slow ionic space-charge branch; experimentally transferring a selected value into a fabricated learning circuit remains future work.\\

\noindent Taken together, the gold and \abbrev{ITO} results establish the model as a compact, semi-physical description of the transient, well suited to circuit-level Spiking Neural Network (\abbrev{SNN}) simulation in tools such as Brian2 or NEST without the computational overhead of atomistic models. Its two-branch structure makes electrode choice an explicit design handle. The gold contact retains the slow proton decay and so supplies the depression and rate-habituation behaviour, while the \abbrev{ITO} contact removes it and exposes the bare trap-release dynamics. A circuit designer can therefore select which relaxation branch dominates the observed response by material rather than by re-tuning parameters.\\


\section[Summary]{Summary}


\noindent This chapter presented a modelling framework for current transients in silicon oxide memristors. Replacing the inert gold top electrode with indium tin oxide (\abbrev{ITO}) eliminated the slow conductance decay, leaving only the rapid initial increase. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (\abbrev{XPS}) depth profiling showed that the \abbrev{ITO} electrode undergoes electrochemical reduction under stress, likely driven by proton accumulation, while the silicon oxide bulk remains chemically stable. The transient therefore stems from two independent processes: rapid charge trapping at a Schottky-like barrier, driving the initial increase, and mobile-ion accumulation at the top metal--insulator interface, driving the subsequent decay.\\

\noindent These two phases were captured in an extended \abbrev{SPICE} model that combines a dispersive trap cascade with a space-charge relaxation branch on a shared resistive path, generalising across a 0.8--1.5\,V range from a single coefficient set. The trap cascade reproduces the sigmoidal, compressed-exponential conductance rise, while the leakage terms let the model follow arbitrary pulse trains, reproduce the measured rate habituation of Chapter~\ref{ch:transients}, and provide a parameterised short-term memory in simulation. Applied to the \abbrev{ITO} records, the same framework describes a complementary dispersive trap-release relaxation and a field-dependent retention scale. The evidence supports electrode-dependent visibility of relaxation branches, but not a demonstrated knob for programming retention. The compact model supplies the device-facing primitives used in the following chapters; their learning and homeostatic implementations remain simulations.
