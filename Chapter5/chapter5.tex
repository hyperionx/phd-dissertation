\chapter{Dispersive Memristive Transients}\label{ch:model}

\noindent The Ti/Au-contacted transient of Chapter~\ref{ch:transients} contains a rapid current rise followed by a
much slower decay under bias. A single transient cannot determine whether these two phases share one physical
origin. This chapter compares Ti/Au- and \abbrev{ITO}-contacted SiO$_x$ device families,
characterises the pristine and stressed \abbrev{ITO} contact, and represents the measured dynamics in a circuit
model. Because the two families differ in oxide and molybdenum thickness as well as in the top contact, their
electrical comparison supports an association with device family and not a controlled electrode substitution.\\

\noindent The circuit analysis starts from a published \abbrevdef{SPICE} model of one Ti/Au-contacted device
\cite{mannion2023compact}. The author performed the electrical comparison, interpreted the optical and
\abbrev{XPS} measurements, developed the trap-cascade and leakage extension, and
fitted the \abbrev{ITO} relaxations. Fabrication of the \abbrev{ITO} devices and the spectroscopic ellipsometry
were carried out by the UCL Electronic Materials and Devices group, and collaborators at the University of Oxford
performed the \abbrev{XPS} depth profiling. Within the circuit model, the rapid rise, slow decay under bias, and
post-drive relaxation are separated without identifying the microscopic traps or mobile species.

\section[Device Families and Transient Separation]{Device Families and Transient Separation}\label{sec:5p1}

\noindent The family comparison tests whether the rapid rise and slow decay under bias remain coupled when the
device stack changes. Spectroscopic ellipsometry then defines the pristine optical state of the alternative
\abbrev{ITO} contact. These two measurements distinguish the phases of the Ti/Au transient and fix the optical
reference against which the stressed contact is later read, so the chemical evidence enters the chapter with a
known starting state.

\subsection[Electrical Separation of the Transient]{Electrical Separation of the Transient}

\noindent Devices with the \abbrev{ITO} top contact used a 50\,nm \abbrev{ITO} / 25\,nm SiO$_x$ / 150\,nm Mo stack.
Their Ti/Au counterparts used 35\,nm SiO$_x$ on 280--300\,nm Mo beneath a titanium adhesion layer and gold pad.
Twenty-one \abbrev{ITO} devices underwent the constant-current conditioning of Section~\ref{sec:3cond}, followed by
the same held-voltage protocol used for the Ti/Au devices. Applying one preparation and measurement sequence to both
sets isolates a difference between the two device families and not between their conditioning or drive
histories.\\

\noindent Conditioned \abbrev{ITO} devices do not reproduce the smooth rise followed by decay measured in the Ti/Au
devices. During constant-current conditioning, the device voltage follows a different trajectory in each device
(Figure~\ref{fig:5a}). Under a held voltage, the current initially rises but does not then decay
(Figure~\ref{fig:5c}). Bias amplitude sets the shape of the held-bias response: the lowest-amplitude transients
remain smooth and monotonic, whereas larger biases produce step-like current changes. At the highest amplitudes,
the current fluctuates by about a factor of two before the device enters a low-resistance state
(Figure~\ref{fig:5d}).

\noindent Of the two decay measurements that the comparison separates, the slow decay under applied bias develops
over tens to hundreds of seconds while the voltage remains on, and it is absent from the held-bias \abbrev{ITO}
transients. Post-drive relaxation begins after removal of the drive, is about an order of magnitude faster, and
occurs in both device families. Keeping the two measurements distinct
prevents the exposed post-drive relaxation from being mistaken for the slow phase missing from the \abbrev{ITO}
waveform.\\

\noindent Both stacks show the rapid rise, and only the Ti/Au devices show the slow decay under bias. The Ti/Au
transient therefore contains at least two contributions, and the absence of a decay in the \abbrev{ITO} devices
associates the slower one with the gold-contacted stack. Because the rise persists across both stacks, the comparison
does not associate it with either contact, and it remains consistent with the trap-filling account of
Chapter~\ref{ch:transients}.

\subsection[Optical Access and Pristine ITO Reference]{Optical Access and Pristine \abbrev{ITO} Reference}

\noindent Optical characterisation of the pristine \abbrev{ITO} is possible because the top contact transmits
visible light. Fitting the layered ellipsometry model of Section~\ref{sec:3optical} reproduces the measured
response with $R^2=0.999$. Across $450$--$760$\,nm, the fitted refractive index falls from $2.29$ to $2.08$, and
the extinction coefficient falls from $0.17$ to $0.085$ (Figure~\ref{fig:5opt}a); at $632.8$\,nm, $n=2.14$ and
$k=0.10$. From the Drude contribution,
the fit gives a carrier concentration of order $10^{20}$\,cm$^{-3}$ and a sheet resistance of approximately
$260\,\Omega/\mathrm{sq}$, consistent with a degenerate, transparent conducting film.\\

\noindent Consecutive dark--light--dark sweeps provide a qualitative check of optical access. The current increases
with sweep order, and the final dark sweep carries the largest current (Figure~\ref{fig:5opt}b). Because the source is
uncalibrated and the device stresses progressively under repeated sweeps, this increase cannot be assigned to
illumination, so the pristine optical reference rests on the ellipsometry alone and the stressed-state chemistry
requires separate evidence.

\section[Chemical Evidence at the ITO Contact]{Chemical Evidence at the ITO Contact}\label{sec:5p2}

\noindent X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy compares the stressed \abbrev{ITO} contact with an unstressed reference.
Depth profiles of the pair can show differences in the \abbrev{ITO}-related chemical signals, but two features of
the experiment prevent those differences from being assigned to electrical stress alone. Argon sputtering removes
oxygen preferentially from In$_2$O$_3$ during profiling \cite{oswald2006x}, which makes the unstressed profile a
beam-affected reference and not an unmodified depth profile. Only one sample was profiled in each state, which leaves the natural
variation between nominally identical stacks unmeasured. These two contributions, sputter-induced oxygen loss and
sample-to-sample variation, accompany every difference reported below.

\subsection[XPS Profiling and Observations]{XPS Profiling and Observations}

\noindent Two nominal \abbrev{ITO} (50\,nm) / SiO$_x$ (25\,nm) / Mo (150\,nm) stacks supplied the depth profiles, in
the arrangement of Section~\ref{sec:3optical} and Figure~\ref{fig:3v6}. The stressed sample underwent the electrical
biasing that produces the unstable conduction of Section~\ref{sec:5p1}; a separate unstressed stack supplied the
reference. All spectra were acquired \emph{ex situ} after removal of the bias. Within this design the comparison
reports differences between the two profiled samples and assesses the working interpretation from their consistency
across several chemical signals.\\

\noindent In the stressed etch sequence the O $1s$ signal crosses from metal oxide to silicon oxide earlier,

calibrated depth, so this observation does not measure a thinner physical layer. Oxygen-deficient material could

these causes.\\

\noindent Core-level binding energy depends on oxidation state and local chemical coordination, so a change in the
In $3d_{5/2}$ position reports a change in the indium environment. These trajectories differ between the two samples
(Figure~\ref{fig:5j}): the unstressed profile rises steadily to a plateau near $0.9$\,eV, while the stressed profile
reaches about $0.7$\,eV before falling. Measured against the surface state, the larger excursion therefore occurs in
the unstressed sample.\\

\noindent Progressive removal of a graded oxygen-deficient region could produce the rise and fall in the stressed
profile, but interface roughening or sputter-induced mixing could produce a similar trajectory. Neither In $3d$
envelope contains a resolved second component, so the spectra do not resolve metallic indium or tin. Between them
the oxygen crossover and the indium trajectory establish a difference in the \abbrev{ITO}-related signals, while the
silicon signal remains stable within the measurement sensitivity.

\subsection[Interfacial Reduction and the Mobile Species]{Interfacial Reduction and the Mobile Species}

\noindent An oxygen-deficient stressed \abbrev{ITO} layer is consistent with both the altered indium trajectory and
the earlier oxygen-state crossover. No resolved metallic-indium component accompanies either observation
\cite{huang2003electrochemical, liu2015important}, and the profiles do not separate the sputter contribution from an
electrical one \cite{oswald2006x}, so reduction remains a working interpretation of the combined signals. Proton
mediation is one proposed route, but hydrogen was not measured, and oxygen-vacancy redistribution could produce the
same spectroscopic changes \cite{lee2019charge, kenyon2019interplay}.\\

\noindent Under negative bias the \abbrev{ITO} top contact is the cathode, so a mobile positive species would drift
towards the \abbrev{ITO}/SiO$_x$ interface, where the indium trajectory changes while the silicon line remains stable
through the oxide. Reduction at this interface could consume the arriving species instead of allowing it to
accumulate as screening charge. This depth-profile evidence supports an interfacial change, but its resolution does
not exclude a contribution from the adjacent SiO$_x$.\\

\noindent Ionised hydrogen is stable and mobile in silicon dioxide under ambient conditions
\cite{hofstein1967proton, vanheusden1999non, vanheusden1998thermally}. Measurements on this material platform also
show that hydrogen concentrations near $2$\,at.\% can affect conductance \cite{cox2024hydrogen}. Oxygen vacancies form
a coupled second candidate because electrode oxygen exchange creates them, and mobile hydrogen can passivate or
generate them \cite{cox2024hydrogen, mehonic2017intrinsic}. The available measurements cannot separate these species,
so in either interpretation electronic carriers provide the terminal current while mobile charge changes the
interfacial field.\\

\noindent This working interpretation assigns different interfacial outcomes to the two contacts: charge reaching a
Ti/Au interface can accumulate because gold is not reduced by protons. By contrast, cathodic \abbrev{ITO} reduction
has been reported in acidic electrolytes, room-temperature electrochemical cells, and films exposed to hydrogen
plasma \cite{ciocci2021differentiating, senthilkumar2008electrochemical, wang2003optical, banerjee1987degradation,
huang2003electrochemical, liu2015important}. In the limiting case of complete reduction, the cathodic half-reaction
would consume the migrating protons,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{In_2O_3 + 6\,H^+ + 6\,e^- \rightarrow 2\,In + 3\,H_2O}, \label{eq:5.redox}
\end{equation}
with an analogous reaction for tin oxide. Consumption at the contact would prevent the species from accumulating and
would suppress the slow screening contribution.\\

\noindent Reduction at the \abbrev{ITO} interface and proton-mediated screening in the Ti/Au devices are both
compatible with the electrical and spectroscopic measurements. A matched series of
contact materials at fixed oxide and molybdenum thickness would be needed to isolate the contact contribution. These
spectroscopic results therefore support the circuit interpretation developed below while leaving the mobile species
and the microscopic reaction unresolved.

\section[Dispersive Filling and Discharge]{Dispersive Filling and Discharge}\label{sec:5p-model}

\noindent A circuit model can represent the measured transient even though its chemical mechanism remains
provisional. Two time-dependent voltage sources acting on a conductive channel describe the step-voltage transients
measured on one Ti/Au-contacted device, the first producing the rapid current increase and the second the slower
decay \cite{mannion2023compact}. Coefficients of that starting model were generalised over $0.7$--$1.0$\,V, with an
additional stress term required at $1.1$\,V.\\

\noindent The extension developed here replaces the single filling state with a short cascade and adds leakage paths
that allow the internal states to recover after the drive changes. All coefficients are refitted to the
$0.8$--$1.5$\,V Ti/Au measurements, so published values are not extrapolated beyond their fitted range. Within that
refit, the Ti/Au measurements determine the filling and slow-screening branches, while the normalised
\abbrev{ITO} relaxation determines only the discharge profile assigned to leakage \cite{woo2024localized}; the model
keeps their absolute currents and complete parameter sets separate. This working interpretation, drawn in
Figure~\ref{fig:5r}, does not assign the circuit states to identified traps or mobile species.

\subsection[Trap-Cascade Extension of the Compact Model]{Trap-Cascade Extension of the Compact
Model}\label{sec:5p3}

\noindent Both model sources act on one channel resistance, $R_0$. In the trapping branch, current increases as
charge occupies states near a Schottky-like barrier between the silicon-rich filament and the contact
\cite{cowley1965surface, sze2021physics, hansen2015double}. A time-dependent node voltage $V_n(t)$ represents that
occupancy, initialised at $V_{n0}$ and driven towards the equilibrium value $V_{n\infty}$ by
\begin{align}
I_n(t) = \alpha \left[ V_n(t) - V_{n\infty} \right], \label{eq:5.3}
\end{align}
where $\alpha$ is an effective trapping coefficient with units of conductance. The trap cascade introduced below
replaces this single-state branch.\\

\noindent Space charge forms the second branch, produced as positively charged defects drift towards the top contact
and screen the applied potential \cite{wang2006oxygen, vanka2022hydrogen}. This slow branch is absent
from the \abbrev{ITO} transients of Section~\ref{sec:5p1}. Electronic conduction remains filamentary along the
silicon-rich paths observed by conductive atomic force microscopy (\abbrevdef{CAFM})
\cite{buckwell2015conductance}, while the ionic current is negligible \cite{meyer2005oxygen}. The model therefore
treats the mobile species as space charge that modifies the channel field, represented by a voltage $V_{sc}(t)$
charged according to
\begin{align}
I_{sc}(t) = \mu \left[ V_{scmax} - V_{sc}(t) \right] \left[ V(t) - V_{sc}(t) \right], \label{eq:5.4}
\end{align}
where $V(t)-V_{sc}(t)$ is the effective potential. The factor $V_{scmax}-V_{sc}(t)$ limits the state to the
steady-screening value $V_{scmax}$, and $\mu$ combines the species mobility with the geometric and material
constants.\\

\noindent Node voltages $V_n$, $V_{sc}$, and $v_{ni}$ are circuit-state proxies for trap occupancy and
accumulated screening charge, not measured internal voltages. Current transients identify only the products
of capacitance and resistance that set the time constants, so the two quantities cannot be fitted independently. The
capacitances are therefore normalised to unity, following the published model \cite{mannion2023compact}, which makes
each fitted resistance numerically equal to a time constant in seconds instead of a physical resistor value. Channel
resistance enters the model as a voltage-dependent quantity because the measured conduction is non-Ohmic
(Section~\ref{subsec:transport}).\\

\noindent A single trapping state cannot reproduce two features of the measured transients. Its first-order response
rises as a concave saturating exponential, while the low-bias transients rise sigmoidally. It also provides no path
for the filled state to empty after the drive changes, which limits the circuit to a single voltage step. The
extension therefore uses a short cascade of sequential states to reproduce the rise, and leakage restores each state
after the drive, as drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:5l}.\\

\noindent The measured rise fixes the required cascade shape. A compressed-exponential
Kohlrausch--Williams--Watts form describes how the cascade fills,
\begin{align}
    \Phi_r(t) = 1 - \exp\!\left[ -\left( \frac{t}{\tau_r} \right)^{\!\beta_{fill}} \right], \label{eq:5.fill}
\end{align}

\noindent where $\Phi_r(t)$ is the fraction filled at time $t$, $\tau_r$ the filling time, and $\beta_{fill}$ the
rise exponent. Fitting Equation~\ref{eq:5.fill} gives
$\beta_{fill}=2.60$ with a $95\%$ interval of $[2.44,2.78]$ from $2000$ repeat-stratified refits of the $18$
transients. One exponent is shared across six biases and their repeat trials, and its interval excludes the
single-exponential value of unity. This compressed, sigmoidal rise differs from the $\beta<1$ stretched form of
classical dispersive band-tail transport \cite{scher1975anomalous,kakalios1987stretched}. Compact trap-based models
of amorphous semiconductors likewise use a common shape with bias-dependent timescales
\cite{libsch1993bias,kim2022compact}.\\

\noindent A compressed rise requires more than one first-order filling stage
\cite{vankampen2007stochastic}. Cascade depths from one to five were compared by withholding each bias in turn and
evaluating the model on the omitted bias. Three stages give the lowest normalised root-mean-square error,
$0.0548$, although every depth above one lies within a few per cent. This three-stage cascade is therefore an
economical representation of the measured shape, not an identified number of microscopic transitions. Its form is
compatible with the trap-assisted tunnelling interpretation of Chapter~\ref{ch:transients}, but does not provide
independent evidence for that mechanism \cite{jimenez2001physical}.\\

\noindent The trapping branch contains $k$ sequential states governed by coupled first-order ordinary differential
equations (\abbrevdef{ODE}s). Under applied bias, the first state fills at the field-accelerated rate $\alpha(V)$,
each state fills the next, and all states discharge towards zero through $R_{leak}$:
\begin{align}
C\,\frac{d v_{n1}}{dt} &= \alpha(V)\,\big[ V_{nmax} - v_{n1} \big] - \frac{v_{n1}}{R_{leak}}, \label{eq:5.6} \\
C\,\frac{d v_{ni}}{dt} &= \alpha(V)\, \frac{v_{n,i-1}}{V_{nmax}}\big[ V_{nmax} - v_{ni} \big] - \frac{v_{ni}}{R_{leak}}, \quad i = 2,\dots,k, \label{eq:5.7}
\end{align}
where $V_{nmax}$ is the saturated occupancy proxy and the final state, $v_{nk}$, sets the trapping-branch
conductance.\\

\noindent Over $0.8$--$1.5$\,V, the filling rate and screening rate follow the empirical exponential-in-voltage
laws fitted below. Voltage dependence enters through $\alpha(V)$ and the filling and screening times. An exponential
dependence on $V$ differs from the $\sqrt{V}$ dependence expected for
Poole--Frenkel capture, so the fitted laws do not identify a capture mechanism. Equation~\ref{eq:5.4} still describes
the space-charge branch, which gains its own $R_{leak}$ recovery path.\\

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.64\textwidth]{Chapter5/Figs/spice_trap_cascade_model.png}
    \caption[The extended SPICE model with a sequential trap cascade.]{Extended \abbrev{SPICE} model.
    \textbf{(A)} The applied bias fills the cascade
    $v_{n1}\!\to\!v_{n2}\!\to\!\cdots\!\to\!v_{nk}$ at rate $\alpha(V)$; each state discharges through
    $R_{leak}$, and $v_{nk}$ sets the trapping-branch conductance. \textbf{(B)} The space-charge branch produces the

\noindent The fitted parameters separate into one shape shared across bias and two field-accelerated time constants.
Cascade depth remains fixed at three, while the filling and screening times vary with voltage:
\begin{align}
\tau_r(V) &= 1.45 \times 10^{2}\, e^{-2.9V}\ \mathrm{s}, \label{eq:5.8} \\
\tau_d(V) &= 1.17 \times 10^{4}\, e^{-3.9V}\ \mathrm{s}. \label{eq:5.9}
\end{align}
Here $\tau_r$ is the effective filling time associated with $\alpha(V)$ in
Equations~\ref{eq:5.6} and~\ref{eq:5.7}, and $\tau_d$ is the screening time of the space-charge branch in
Equation~\ref{eq:5.4}. Table~\ref{table:5c} gives their ranges over the measured voltage window together with the
shared exponent and cascade depth.\\

\noindent One shared shape and voltage-law parameterisation reproduces the transients from $0.8$ to $1.5$\,V with a
median $R^2=0.93$ (Figure~\ref{fig:5m2}). The fit uses six biases and repeat trials from one Ti/Au-contacted device,
with unit capacitances making the source equations set the state time constants directly. This agreement establishes
the model's adequacy for the measured step responses of that device. Because the fitted measurements contain no
pulse-train recovery test and no device-to-device replication, the leakage paths permit recovery mathematically while
the coefficients describe only the measured device's voltage dependence.

\subsection[ITO Relaxation and Discharge Scale]{\abbrev{ITO} Relaxation and Discharge
Scale}\label{sec:5p-ito}

\noindent Separation of timescales motivates assigning the faster \abbrev{ITO} relaxation to the leakage time
$\tau_{leak}=R_{leak}C$: $\tau_d$ extends to hundreds of seconds (Equation~\ref{eq:5.9}), $\tau_r$ is of order ten
seconds (Equation~\ref{eq:5.8}), and the measured \abbrev{ITO} relaxation is faster than both. This assignment is a
modelling interpretation, since the measurements fix the decay profile without identifying a physical leakage
element. Treating the relaxation as discharge through $R_{leak}$ retains the two existing circuit branches instead
of adding another mechanism.\\

\noindent The stretched exponential of Equation~\ref{eq:3.1} describes the post-drive relaxation, with $I_0$ fitted
as a background that includes unresolved device and readout contributions. Applying the shape and fit-quality
criteria of Section~\ref{sec:3analysis} removes 8 and 9 transients, respectively, and leaves 53 fits whose median
$R^2$ is $0.98$, compared with $0.90$ for a single exponential. Most fitted exponents lie below unity; only two
fits, both at the lowest measured biases, exceed it (Figure~\ref{fig:5disch}).\\

\noindent The median fitted scale across the retained set is $\tau_{leak}\approx1.3$\,s, about an order of magnitude
shorter than the slow space-charge decay. For a stretched exponential, this scale is not the mean retention: the area
under the normalised decay is $(\tau_{leak}/\beta_{leak})\Gamma(1/\beta_{leak})$, which gives a mean retention of
about $2.3$\,s at the held-bias exponent $\beta_{leak}\approx0.54$. An exponent below unity describes dispersive
relaxation over a distribution of release times \cite{scher1975anomalous,kakalios1987stretched}.\\

\noindent These 53 fits sample biases and repeated measurements within one device platform, and they share devices,
sessions, and, in some cases, bias conditions; they do not represent 53 independent devices. The intervals and
$p$-values quoted below therefore describe variation within the fitted set rather than inference to a device
population. Establishing the field law across devices would require the matched and replicated device series
identified in Section~\ref{sec:5p2}.\\

\noindent Three analyses constrain the field-free discharge on different evidential terms. Extrapolating the field
law fitted to the held-bias measurements gives the primary estimate, although it extends beyond the measured voltage
range, and two direct reads at $|V|=1$\,mV test that estimate on separate devices. The fill--emission calculation
checks only the expected direction of the correction, because its floor ratio is not independently calibrated.\\

\noindent Equation~\ref{eq:3.1} remains an empirical description, with $\beta_{leak}$ measuring dispersion in the
fitted release rates without identifying their mechanism. Every relaxation in the main set was measured under a
held subthreshold bias, so $\tau_{leak}$ is an effective held-bias scale. Barrier lowering predicts a field-accelerated
emission rate \cite{frenkel1938pre}, and the fitted values follow
$\ln(1/\tau_{leak})\propto\sqrt{V}$ with a slope of $0.89\pm0.17$ (Figure~\ref{fig:5tf}A):
\begin{equation}
        \tau_{leak}(V) \approx 3.6\,e^{-0.89\sqrt{V}}\ \text{s}. \label{eq:5.11}
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the magnitude of the held sensing bias in volts. Extrapolation to zero field gives a discharge time of
a few seconds, with an interval of $[2.4,5.7]$\,s from resampling the 53 fits. Both values are extrapolations from
held-bias measurements and not direct zero-field measurements.\\

\noindent The fitted dispersion also depends on bias:
\begin{equation}
    \beta_{leak}(V) \approx \beta_0 - \kappa\,\log_{10}V, \qquad \kappa \approx 0.33\ \text{per decade}, \label{eq:5.11b}
\end{equation}
Across the fitted set, $r=-0.68$ (Figure~\ref{fig:5tf}B), and $\beta_{leak}$ rises from approximately $0.3$ at
$4$\,V to approximately $0.8$ near $0.1$\,V, with a held-bias mean near $0.54$. Equation~\ref{eq:5.11b} describes
only the measured range, and extrapolating it towards zero voltage would eventually give $\beta_{leak}>1$. Since the
voltage sequence was non-monotonic, the fitted voltage trend is separated from run order. This bias dependence is
consistent with field broadening of the fitted rate distribution, although the measurements cannot separate
broadening from refilling under the sensing bias.\\

\noindent The square-root field dependence is compatible with barrier-lowered emission, though it is not specific to
one mechanism. A drive-off measurement at exactly zero bias would remove the sensing field, although the current
readout also vanishes as the bias approaches zero. Two measurements at $|V|=1$\,mV therefore provide a
near-zero-bias check on separate devices (Figure~\ref{fig:5u}). Their fitted scales are $4.3$ and $9.2$\,s, with
$\beta_{leak}=0.82$ and $0.86$, making both less dispersive than the main held-bias set. These two reads support a
seconds-scale low-field discharge, although their limited duration may bias the fitted exponent upwards.
Temperature-dependent measurements or a contactless charge probe would separate thermal and field contributions more
directly.\\

\noindent A fill--emission balance provides a separate check on whether the field-free discharge should be slower
than the held-bias decay. The calculation assumes single-exponential relaxation, so it represents the dispersive
decay through a single effective rate. If $v$ denotes the occupancy proxy bounded by $V_{nmax}$,
field-driven filling and field-free emission compete according to
\begin{align}
\frac{dv}{dt} &= f\,(V_{nmax}-v) - g\,v, \qquad f = \alpha\,d(V),\quad g = 1/\tau_{leak}^{0}, \label{eq:5.12}
\end{align}
with $f$ the field-driven filling rate and $g$ the field-free emission rate.\\

\noindent Under held bias, the measured decay rate is the sum of filling and emission, while the steady-state floor
depends on their ratio:
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{\tau_{hb}} = f + g, \qquad \rho \equiv \frac{I_0}{A + I_0} = \frac{f}{f+g}, \label{eq:5.13}
\end{align}
Eliminating $f$ gives the emission rate and its associated field-free discharge scale:
\begin{align}
g = \frac{1-\rho}{\tau_{hb}}, \qquad \tau_{leak}^{0} = \frac{\tau_{hb}}{1-\rho}. \label{eq:5.14}
\end{align}
Here $d(V)$ scales the field-driven filling rate, $\tau_{hb}$ is the fitted held-bias decay scale,
$\tau_{leak}^{0}$ is the inferred field-free discharge scale, and $\rho$ expresses the fitted floor as a
fraction of the peak through the decaying amplitude $A$ and fitted background $I_0$ of
Equation~\ref{eq:3.1}.\\

\noindent The median floor ratio is $\rho\approx0.11$, and Equation~\ref{eq:5.14} gives a median
$\tau_{leak}^{0}\approx1.6$\,s across all biases, rising to approximately $3$\,s in the low-bias subset
(Figure~\ref{fig:5fs}). This increase agrees in direction with the $[2.4,5.7]$\,s barrier-lowering extrapolation.
Since the fitted $I_0$ includes steady background conduction that has not been calibrated separately, the numerical
floor ratio is not independently determined, and the fill--emission calculation therefore supports only the direction
of the correction. For the claim that the low-field discharge remains on a seconds scale and is less dispersive than
the held-bias response, the held-bias extrapolation and the two near-zero measurements provide the stronger
evidence.

\section[Summary]{Summary}

\noindent Comparison across the two device families separates the shared rapid rise from the slow decay under bias
found in the Ti/Au devices. Spectroscopy independently identifies a change in the stressed \abbrev{ITO}-related
signals that is consistent with interfacial reduction, while leaving the mobile species and microscopic traps unresolved. Removing
the slow branch exposes a post-drive relaxation that is strongly dispersive under held bias and approaches a slower,
less dispersive response near zero field.\\

\noindent Within the compact model, a short cascade reproduces the compressed filling response, and leakage
reproduces recovery and the measured post-drive relaxation. This cascade remains a device-level account of the driven
transient and is not used beyond this chapter. The later network and reward-learning chapters take only the fitted
decay profile of the post-drive relaxation, which gives their volatile state a measured shape instead of a freely
chosen lifetime.
