\chapter{Current Transients in Memristive Devices}\label{ch:transients}

\noindent The switching characterised in Chapter~\ref{ch:fab} is the device's non-volatile mode. This chapter turns to a different one. The \emph{current transient} is a slow, volatile, analogue conductance change that appears below the switching threshold. It shows both potentiation and depression in response to a step potential, and it has been largely unexplored in neuromorphic computing. The chapter reviews the competing transport models for the transient, develops a controlled method to induce and tune it, and shows that it behaves as a synaptic primitive. Fixing the mechanism fixes the timescale. That timescale is what lets the transient serve as the slow synaptic dynamic the later spiking-network chapters depend on. \\

\noindent The current transient was first studied within the UCL Electronic Materials and Devices group, in earlier doctoral work \cite{mannion2022current} and a co-authored paper on the subthreshold regime \cite{mannion2023unipolar}. That work established its core analogue, volatile potentiation and depression under a single voltage polarity, and supplied the step-potential characterisation and constant-current stressing protocol. The author reproduced the transient, adapted the stressing protocol for pulsed inputs, and ran the pulsed-input experiments reported here. In these experiments the bidirectional spike-pulse plasticity and the rate-dependent homeostasis develop it into a synaptic primitive.

\section[Origins of Current Transients]{Origins of Current Transients}

\noindent This section reviews the phenomenology of current transients and the competing transport models proposed for them, from space-charge-limited conduction to the electronic and ionic accounts that motivate the mechanism study later in the chapter.

\subsection[Transient Phenomenology]{Transient Phenomenology}

\noindent Ideally, capacitive decay and dielectric relaxation govern a capacitor's response to a step voltage. Under a constant voltage, the current declines to a steady leakage value, which is zero in an ideal device. Prolonged voltage stress or elevated temperatures, however, can degrade the oxide, reducing its resistance and raising the current \cite{ghibaudo1999emerging}. One form of this degradation is the "current transient", which produces a characteristic peak on a current-time graph (Figure \ref{fig:4a}). The current rises swiftly to a maximum and then decays gradually. Unlike permanent oxide degradation, this resistance change is volatile. \\

    \caption[Current transients illustration]{Idealised current transient for a closed circuit: the current rises to a peak $i_{peak}$ at time $t_{peak}$ and then decays. Redrawn after \cite{zafar2011measurement}.}
    \label{fig:4a}
\end{figure}

\noindent The transient's characteristic peak and long duration distinguish it from simple \abbrev{RC} decay. The peak typically occurs after 5 to 20 seconds, and the whole event can last hundreds of seconds \cite{saha2001transient}. These timescales far exceed those of standard integrated resistor-capacitor (\abbrev{RC}) circuits, which would need impractically large capacitors to reproduce such behaviour \cite{el2006space}. \\

\noindent The transient's appeal lies in this long duration, which enables computation on timescales far exceeding those of simple \abbrev{RC} circuits. These dynamics are not fixed. Raising the temperature \cite{manceau2007metal}, voltage \cite{zafar1998oxygen}, or laser irradiation \cite{li2015visible} accelerates the transient and brings its peak forward. If accelerated enough, the peak occurs faster than the measurement equipment can resolve. It then disappears, leaving only a decaying current. \\

\noindent The features of transients are similar across published research, even though these studies span diverse oxide materials \cite{saha2001transient}. Yet the current transient is not seen in every device, especially with regard to device polarity. Early investigations detected transients in a single voltage polarity only \cite{manceau2007current}, whereas other devices in the literature now produce transients in both polarities \cite{chen2010observation}. \\

\noindent Current transients with a distinct peak and subsequent decay under constant voltage stress have been reported in many oxide materials, implying a generic phenomenon in \abbrev{MIM} devices. These transients deviate from the usual resistance switching of silicon oxide devices. They are analogue and volatile, in contrast to the non-volatile binary switching of standard operation. Understanding their source and features matters because they bear on several research areas studying metal-oxide structures, such as transistor gates and the relatively emerging field of resistive switching memory.

\subsection[Space-Charge-Limited Conduction]{Space-Charge-Limited Conduction}

\noindent The space-charge-limited current (\abbrevdef{SCLC}) hypothesis remains the leading model for current transients. Developed to describe space charge in a perfect insulator \cite{many1962theory}, it was later adapted to charged oxygen vacancies \cite{zafar1998oxygen}. Under an applied bias, an ionic current flows alongside the electronic current, driven by the drift of charged species, typically oxygen vacancies. The metal electrode blocks these ions, which accumulate at the interface. This accumulation creates an opposing internal electric field, a Coulombic repulsion that impedes further migration and decays the ionic current. Early research therefore attributed transient behaviour to space charge accumulation in a trap-free insulator, establishing the \abbrev{SCLC} model. \\

\noindent The original \abbrev{SCLC} model assumes one-dimensional linear conduction with uniform current flow between parallel electrodes \cite{many1962theory, lampert1970current}. Although it applies to both electronic and ionic carriers, subsequent studies link the transient to charged defect migration within the oxide \cite{zafar1998oxygen}. The mobility term $\mu$ represents moving oxygen vacancies \cite{zafar1998oxygen} or other mobile ions \cite{chen2010observation}. Here the ionic current modulates the dominant electronic current. The model defines the total current density $J(t)$ as:
\begin{align}
J(t)              &= J_{cond} + J_{disp} \label{eq:4.1} \\
J_{cond}(x,t)     &= q n(x,t) \mu E(x,t) \label{eq:4.2} \\
J_{disp}(x,t)     &= \varepsilon \frac{\partial E(x,t)}{\partial t} \label{eq:4.3} \\
q n(x,t)          &= \varepsilon \frac{\partial E(x,t)}{\partial x} \label{eq:4.4}
\end{align}

\noindent Here $J(t)$ sums the conduction current density $J_{cond}$ and the displacement current density $J_{disp}$, the latter accounting for the time-varying electric field. $q$ is the elementary charge, $n$ the bulk space charge concentration, $\mu$ the mobility, $E$ the local electric field, $d$ the oxide thickness, and $\varepsilon$ the permittivity. Equation (\ref{eq:4.4}) is Poisson's equation.

\begin{align}
J(t) &= \varepsilon \left( \frac{\mu}{2} \frac{\partial E^{2}(x,t)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial E(x,t)}{\partial t} \right) \label{eq:4.5} \\
J(t) &= \frac{\varepsilon \mu}{2d} \left( E_a^{2}(t) - E_c^{2}(t) \right) \label{eq:4.6} \\
J(t) &= \frac{\mu Q(t)}{2d} \left( 2 E_a(t) - \frac{Q(t)}{\varepsilon} \right) \label{eq:4.7}
\end{align}

\noindent Substituting the current components (\ref{eq:4.2}, \ref{eq:4.3}) and Poisson's equation (\ref{eq:4.4}) into (\ref{eq:4.1}) yields the device current density (\ref{eq:4.5}). Even at fixed voltage, the current varies with time as the internal field $E(x,t)$ evolves. Integrating (\ref{eq:4.5}) from cathode to anode yields (\ref{eq:4.6}), relating current to the electrode fields $E_a(t)$ and $E_c(t)$. For a constant applied voltage ($\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = 0$), this simplifies to (\ref{eq:4.7}) in terms of total insulator charge $Q(t)$. \\

\noindent The peak time $\tau$, the moment the space charge front reaches the anode, follows from the evolution of the electric field. Although the applied voltage $V$ stays constant, the local field $E_a(t)$ at the anode varies over time. As the space charge redistributes across the device, it alters the internal field profile. To keep the total voltage drop $V$ constant (the spatial integral of the field across the thickness $d$), the field ahead of the migrating charge front must progressively increase. Assuming an Ohmic contact at the cathode ($E_c(t) \approx 0$) and a charge front that has not yet reached the anode ($J_{cond}(d,t) = 0$), the total current at the anode is purely displacement current: $J(t) = \varepsilon \frac{\partial E_a(t)}{\partial t}$. Equating this with the current of (\ref{eq:4.6}) yields the differential equation (\ref{eq:4.8}). Integrating it with the uniform initial condition $E_a(0) = V/d$ gives the time-dependent anode field (\ref{eq:4.9}).

\begin{align}
\frac{dE_a}{E_a^2} &= \frac{\mu}{2d} \,dt \label{eq:4.8} \\
E_a(t)             &= \frac{V}{d} \left( 1 - \frac{\mu V t}{2d^2} \right)^{-1} \label{eq:4.9} \\
d                  &= \mu \int_{0}^{\tau} E_a(t) \,dt \label{eq:4.10} \\
d                  &= -2d \left[ \ln\left( 1 - \frac{\mu V t}{2d^2} \right) \right]_{0}^{\tau} \label{eq:4.11} \\
\tau               &= \frac{2 d^2}{\mu V} \left[ 1 - \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \right) \right] \approx 0.787 \frac{d^2}{\mu V} \label{eq:4.12}
\end{align}

\noindent With the anode field determined, the time for the space charge front to reach the anode follows. Taking the space charge velocity as $\mu E(x,t)$ and the field as constant between the charge front and the anode, the field at the front equals that at the anode, $E(x,t) = E_a(t)$. \\

\noindent The \abbrev{SCLC} model owes its wide adoption to Equation (\ref{eq:4.12}), which gives the mobility of the migrating space charge directly. Combined with the Einstein-Nernst relation, this mobility yields the activation energy for the migration of specific defects, such as oxygen vacancies. This activation energy is distinct from that of the bulk material's electronic conductivity. On this basis the literature reads laser-induced transient acceleration as evidence of increased mobility \cite{li2015visible} or of charge transitions within the defect \cite{lee2019charge}. To derive such mobilities, however, the model's validity must first be established, and this is where problems arise.

\subsection[Competing Transport Hypotheses]{Competing Transport Hypotheses}

\abbrev{SCLC} is not the only model of these transients. Whereas \abbrev{SCLC} attributes the transient to substantial ionic current, alternative theories treat the ionic current as negligible and attribute the transient to variations in bulk and interface electronic conductivity \cite{meyer2005oxygen}. This view is supported by simulations of vacancy, electron, and hole redistribution within a barium strontium titanate (\abbrevdef{BST}) oxide layer, bounded by Schottky contacts at $BST-Pt$ interfaces. They used a finite difference method to model particle redistribution driven by drift and diffusion.
\begin{align}
    J_k = Z_k \left( -D_k \frac{dC_k}{dx} + \frac{Z_k}{|Z_k|} \mu_k C_k E \right), \quad k \in \{n, p, V_{\ddot{o}}\} \label{eq:4.13}
\end{align}


\noindent Here $k$ denotes the particle species (electrons, holes, or charged oxygen vacancies), $J_k$ is the current density, $D_k$ the diffusion coefficient, $C_k$ the particle concentration, $Z_k$ the charge in Coulombs, $\mu_k$ the mobility, and $E$ the local electric field. The simulation iteratively determines particle redistribution. It then computes the total device current from particle velocity and distribution, summing the electron, hole, and vacancy currents. Each species contributes diffusion and drift terms, as described by the drift--diffusion (Nernst--Planck) equation (\ref{eq:4.13}). The method separates electronic and ionic currents, which is impossible in experiment, and validates itself by comparing the total simulated current with experimental transient data.\\

\noindent The simulation reveals a rapid, asymmetric redistribution of electrons and holes, creating a highly n-type interface at the negative electrode and slightly depleting the other. Published data indicate this rate slows as the transient peaks. Oxygen vacancies then migrate much more slowly towards the negative electrode, markedly altering the internal field and bending the conduction band. Given the vacancies' low mobility, the resulting ionic displacement current is too small to cause the observed current increase. The current rise instead stems from modulated electronic conductivity driven by the altered vacancy distribution.\\

\noindent Varying the simulated device temperature gave an activation energy for increased conductance of 0.8 eV, comparable to that reported for oxygen vacancies in \abbrev{BST} devices \cite{saha2001transient}. Because the transient peak precedes significant vacancy redistribution, however, attributing this activation energy to vacancy migration is unsound. The results instead point to electron and hole migration, which dominates before and during the peak. This agrees with transient-current studies of Pt/TiO$_2$/Pt capacitors \cite{zhong2010transient}, which observe electronic traps with activation energies of 0.18--0.3 eV. They concluded that oxygen vacancy migration alters electron and hole concentrations, thereby regulating bulk conductivity. \\

\noindent Given these conflicting theories, studies seek to determine whether the effect is electronic or ionic (\abbrev{SCLC}). One analysis of transients used both the \abbrev{SCLC} model and electronic conductivity modulation. It found that the electronic model yielded a more credible dielectric constant, suggesting its superiority \cite{wakiya2006activation}. Another study \cite{el2006space} probed the question by assuming electronic traps depend on oxide crystallinity. Observing nearly identical transients in amorphous and polycrystalline devices, the authors concluded the effect is ionic, supporting earlier claims \cite{zafar1998oxygen}. This deduction rests on the premise that electronic trap distributions are sensitive to structural order. The invariance of transient behaviour across structural differences therefore implies a mechanism independent of crystallinity-related trap states, pointing instead to ionic migration such as oxygen vacancies. \\

\noindent A later publication \cite{el2007ionic} favoured an electronic explanation based on shallow electronic traps. On this view, transient currents arise not from ionic displacement but from the time-dependent filling and emptying of shallow trap states near the conduction band edge. These states modulate free carrier concentration and electronic conductivity. The ionic-versus-electronic debate remains unresolved, but the literature increasingly favours electronic conduction modulated by vacancy movement. In hindsight, the \abbrev{SCLC} model has proven useful yet not fully validated. Higher temperatures and voltages accelerate the transient as predicted, and peak time versus reciprocal voltage plots are linear in certain regimes, but the original \abbrev{SCLC} model warrants closer scrutiny.\\

\noindent Consider the thickness dependence of peak time predicted by Equation (\ref{eq:4.12}). If the phenomenon arose solely from vacancies traversing the oxide, the peak time should scale with the square of the device thickness $d$. Few studies have examined this, but the available data \cite{manceau2007current} show peak timing to be independent of device thickness. Oxide thicknesses of 900\r{A}, 450\r{A}, and 200\r{A} produce no increase in peak time. This flatly contradicts the prediction that the 450\r{A} and 900\r{A} devices should show peak times roughly 5 and 20 times greater than the 200\r{A} device. Thickness instead affects peak height and shape rather than timing. Thinner samples give broader, more dispersed peaks, and thicker samples more pronounced ones. This sharp discrepancy between the \abbrev{SCLC} equation and experiment confirms that thickness and peak timing are unrelated in practice. It raises serious concerns about the model, though not the only ones.\\

\noindent More troubling is the predicted linear relationship between peak time and reciprocal voltage, often taken as the primary indicator of \abbrev{SCLC} behaviour (\ref{eq:4.12}). Many papers claiming linearity rely on few data points. These plots also depend on measuring peak times, a difficult task: as Figure \ref{fig:4b} shows, shallow curves obscure the peaks. The resulting uncertainty for shallower transients makes peak time selection subjective. Some studies show linearity only in specific regions, while others observe exponential behaviour. These inconsistencies need further study and warrant scepticism in applying \abbrev{SCLC} theory to current transients.\\

\noindent Verifying these models matters, since they yield material properties such as mobility values and activation energies directly from the \abbrev{SCLC} equations. Further studies have examined the precise nature of defects and their transport mechanisms to ensure that extracted parameters accurately represent material properties. These findings carry direct implications for the nascent field of defect engineering.

\section[Inducing Transients by Constant-Current Stress]{Inducing Transients by Constant-Current Stress}

\noindent The device studied in this chapter shares the metal-insulator-metal architecture of those in Chapter \ref{ch:fab} but comes from a separate fabrication batch. Its nominal layer thicknesses therefore differ slightly from the values quoted there. It is shown in Figure \ref{fig:4c} and was originally developed for binary resistance switching \cite{mehonic2017intrinsic}. The bottom metal contact is a 280 nm molybdenum film deposited by magnetron sputtering. The insulator is a slightly sub-stochiometric, amorphous silicon oxide layer 35 nm thick, deposited by \abbrev{RF} magnetron sputtering. The top contact is a 110 nm gold film deposited by e-beam evaporation through a contact mask, a $200 \times 200 \mu m$ square that defines the device's active area. A 3 nm titanium layer, deposited before the gold, improves adhesion and also acts as a gettering layer that seeds the oxide with oxygen vacancies.\\

\subsection[Constant-Current Stressing]{Constant-Current Stressing}

\noindent Current transients were once attributed to oxide imperfections, implying inherently faulty devices. Defect engineering now allows oxide defects to be fabricated under control. Electroforming is the application of a strong electric field to induce a partial, reversible breakdown in a Metal-Insulator-Metal (\abbrev{MIM}) device. It is common in memristor and ReRAM research and is used for the silicon oxide devices in this thesis.\\

\noindent Electroforming is a logical starting point for introducing defects but has limitations. It typically induces large, discrete jumps in conductance, giving stable switching between high and low resistance states. These traits contrast sharply with the analogue, volatile nature of current transients, suggesting that transients occur in the pre-electroforming regime, before the device settles into stable binary behaviour.\\

\noindent The conductance of a device showing transients is closer to the High Resistance State (\abbrev{HRS}) than the Low Resistance State (\abbrev{LRS}) of an electroformed device, linking electroforming to current transients. A more subtle electroforming technique, applied before the main switching event, could thus create the transient. The positive feedback inherent in electroforming, however, makes such delicate control hard to achieve \cite{kozicki2016electrochemical}.\\

% \noindent Current flow injects charge into the oxide, creating defects like vacancies that increase conductance. This process is inherently unstable: increased conductance leads to higher current densities, which accelerate defect creation, causing a runaway increase in current until catastrophic breakdown occurs. This exponential rise is most evident when applying high constant voltages (13V to 15V). The positive feedback makes regulated, gradual modulation of conductance difficult.\\

\noindent Subtle modulation requires avoiding the positive feedback of electroforming, achieved by constant current stressing instead of conventional voltage-based electroforming. The peak voltage reached during constant current stressing is comparable in magnitude to the high voltages (e.g., 13–15V) applied during voltage-based electroforming.\\

% \noindent The key distinction is that during constant current stressing, the voltage across the device decreases as it breaks down, a result of the current source's negative feedback. Constant current stressing is a standard method for evaluating time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB) in oxides, a failure mode caused by defects that increase conductance via electron trapping \cite{1487102}.\\

\noindent Applying a constant current rather than a fixed voltage lowers the voltage across the device as its conductance increases. This negative feedback slows oxide defect creation. Equilibrium is reached when the voltage drops to a level where defect formation ceases but current still flows, allowing subtle, controlled changes in device conductance. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter4/Figs/stress_current_response.png}
    \caption[Response of devices to different magnitudes of stressing currents.]{Voltage across each of six devices during the initial constant current stress, each device stressed at a different current magnitude \cite{mannion2023unipolar}.}
    \label{fig:4d}
    \end{figure}
    
\noindent To stress the devices, the molybdenum contact is grounded and negative voltages applied to the gold contact, injecting electrons at the gold contact and holes at the molybdenum contact. The constant current is applied for 100 seconds at magnitudes from $-0.1\mu A$ to $-100\mu A$ to set the stress level. The induced voltage across the device then decreases gradually to a steady-state value (Figure \ref{fig:4d}).\\

\noindent After electrical stress, the device must relax before showing current transients. Stressing displaces charged defects, likely oxygen vacancies, into a polarised state. During this relaxation, distinct from the seconds-scale transient decay discussed below, these low-mobility defects diffuse back to thermal equilibrium. A positive potential on the top contact speeds recovery, but restricted defect mobility at room temperature still typically requires a 24-hour rest. This mandatory delay confirms that stressing generates and displaces defects. These defects must settle into a specific configuration to produce transients.

\subsection[Polarity Dependence]{Polarity Dependence}

\noindent The total number of oxide defects, not the device conductance, sets the transient amplitude. Thicker devices inherently hold more defects, which explains the suppression of transients reported in thinner devices \cite{el2007ionic}. Electrical stress generates the necessary defects, but transients emerge only under negative current stress, which injects electrons at the top gold-titanium contact. Positive current stress at the molybdenum contact produces no transients.\\

\noindent Scanning electron microscopy (\abbrevdef{SEM}) shows that aggressive stress produces distinct macroscopic defects at the electrical contacts, matching observations in electroformed devices \cite{waser1990dcI,waser1990dcII}. Excessive negative stress creates clustered holes 1 to 5 $\mu m$ in diameter spanning regions over 20 $\mu m$. These non-mechanical clusters are orders of magnitude larger than the nanoscale conductive filaments imaged by conductive \abbrev{AFM} in these devices \cite{buckwell2015conductance}. Excessive positive stress instead roughens the contact surface uniformly. These macroscopic defects mark overstress; they are not needed to produce current transients and were deliberately avoided here.

\section[Subthreshold Synaptic Plasticity]{Subthreshold Synaptic Plasticity}

\noindent Current transients are hard to define. They are elusive, frequently mistaken for faults, and rare across device batches. This scarcity reflects the difficulty of fabricating devices that reliably show the phenomenon. Detailed characterisation requires inducing the effect gradually so it can be correlated with specific changes in device attributes. Existing analysis is limited, since published research relies on the sporadic appearance of transients. A reliable method to induce transients is therefore essential for a full understanding of the phenomenon.\\

\subsection[Bidirectional Plasticity and Tunability]{Bidirectional Plasticity and Tunability}

\noindent Current transients enable analogue potentiation and depression under the same voltage polarity and amplitude. Here potentiation is an increase in device conductance (a rise in current) and depression a decrease (a fall in current). These changes can be long-term, as \abbrev{LTP} or \abbrev{LTD}, or short-term, as \abbrev{STP} and short-term depression (\abbrevdef{STD}). Memristors and ReRAM devices often replicate these behaviours but typically require opposite polarities for potentiation and depression \cite{jo2010nanoscale}, which complicates neuron circuits. Single-rail operation, common in digital electronics, is preferable. Some devices, such as complementary \cite{khan2020comparison} or unipolar ReRAM \cite{mehonic2012electrically}, operate with a single polarity but often switch in binary rather than analogue fashion.\\

\noindent A negative step potential on the top gold electrode demonstrates the device's potentiation and depression (Figure \ref{fig:4e}). Rapid potentiation (current increase) occurs within seconds, followed by a slower depression phase (current decrease) that drives the conductance below its initial level over hundreds of seconds.\\

\noindent The response mirrors the \abbrev{DC} behaviour: initial potentiation over four pulses, then depression. Both potentiation and depression thus occur under \abbrev{DC} and spike train operation. Interrupting the pulse train lets the conductance relax, demonstrating volatile, repeatable short-term plasticity. The subthreshold regime offers high resistance ($\sim 10 M\Omega$), greatly reducing current consumption compared with typical resistance switching devices ($1 k\Omega - 100 k\Omega$). This high resistance, however, raises sensitivity to noise and voltage fluctuations. The voltage dependence of depression (Figure \ref{fig:4e}) also limits the operating range, since depression is minimal below -1 V. \\

\noindent Selecting between potentiation and depression is central to neuromorphic circuit design, and pulse magnitude offers an adaptable control. As Figure \ref{fig:4e} shows, lower voltages (e.g., -1 V) induce potentiation but not depression, consistent with low-voltage spike trains.\\

\end{figure}

\noindent Higher stressing currents induce strong potentiation and increased conductance. The device stressed at the minimum current (-0.1 $\mu$A) shows only depression, whereas the one at the maximum current (-100 $\mu$A) potentiates to nearly ten times its initial conductance. These methods set the device's potentiation level. The adjustment is irreversible. Initial stressing produces permanent structural changes and defect distributions in the oxide, preventing a return to the pristine state, so baseline potentiation characteristics are fixed during fabrication. Altering spike amplitude, by contrast, provides dynamic, run-time control. Because the device both increases and decreases conductance at differing rates and relaxation times, steady-state conductance inevitably varies with spike train period. Confirming this behaviour is a prerequisite to investigating its computational potential.\\

\noindent Figure \ref{fig:4k} plots device current for spike trains with varying inter-spike periods. The device settles into a steady-state conductance where spike-induced changes balance its relaxation dynamics. As expected, this steady-state conductance depends on pulse frequency, falling as frequency rises. Such frequency-dependent resistance adjustment suggests utility in neuromorphic applications.

\subsection[Homeostatic Function]{Homeostatic Function}

\noindent This study illustrates the potential of distinctive memristive behaviours in novel computing architectures. One application is adjusting synaptic weight by firing frequency, mimicking biological homeostasis \cite{tien2018homeostatic}. In biology, homeostasis maintains optimal operating conditions, such as body temperature \cite{turrigiano1999homeostatic}. In spiking neural networks, it regulates spiking activity. This cuts power consumption from unnecessary events and prevents faulty neurons from driving chaotic, high-activity states that could destabilise the network \cite{marder2006variability}. \\

\noindent Habituation, a form of homeostasis in which output changes temporarily with input variations before settling to a steady state, has practical uses. Memristive devices implementing such homeostatic self-regulation in hardware can stabilise activity and improve the efficiency of unsupervised neuromorphic learning \cite{munoz2021brain}.\\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
\centering    
\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Chapter4/Figs/spike_frequency_response.png}
\caption[Response of the device to different frequency of spike pulses]{Response of the device to varying spike-pulse frequencies. The device was first driven with 1Hz spike pulses, then 10Hz, then returned to 1Hz.}
\label{fig:4l}
\end{figure}

\noindent As shown earlier, the device's steady-state conductance depends on the incoming spike rate, falling at higher spiking frequencies and settling higher at lower frequencies. This behaviour inhibits suboptimal input signals with chaotic, high-frequency activity, as Figure \ref{fig:4l} illustrates. The device acts as a synapse rather than a neuron, so the relevant input is the firing rate of the pre-synaptic neuron driving it. The frequencies are chosen to fall within the slow relaxation timescale of the transient (seconds), the band over which the device's conductance is rate-sensitive, rather than to model any single neuron's intrinsic dynamics. A signal generator therefore applies 1 Hz Gaussian pulses to emulate a low background firing rate, representative of a resting pre-synaptic neuron, letting the device reach a baseline steady-state conductance. At t = 1.8 minutes, a dysfunctional, high-activity state (e.g., from upstream circuit damage) is simulated by abruptly raising the spike train frequency to 10 Hz. \\

\noindent Without homeostasis, this erroneous input could propagate through the network, driving subsequent neurons into similar states. The device's transient behaviour, however, reduces conductivity under high-frequency stress. This limits the current supplied to the output neuron and prevents the propagation of high-frequency activity. Such temporary suppression protects the network but must be reversible upon recovery. At t = 2.8 minutes, the input returns to the 1 Hz baseline, and the device conductance recovers to its initial steady-state value, demonstrating the reversibility of the homeostatic mechanism. This is a single representative device trace illustrating the mechanism rather than a population statistic. The underlying rate-dependent conductance change it relies on is the same effect quantified across repeated measurements earlier in this chapter (Figure~\ref{fig:4f}). \\

\noindent Reversibility is fundamental to homeostasis. This device demonstrates homeostatic habituation, but limitations remain; in particular, holding a constant conductance during the transient state is difficult. Electroforming the device, like a standard memristor, would enable precise analogue weight adjustment. Physical implementation of homeostasis may require connecting the transient device in series with a programmable one. Since both share identical architectures and manufacturing processes, differing only in electrical stressing, they can be integrated on a single wafer. \\

\subsection[Mechanistic Interpretation]{Mechanistic Interpretation}

\noindent With the homeostatic function established, attention turns to its physical origin. Early theories attributed the transient to an ionic current superimposed on an electronic current \cite{many1962theory, lampert1970current}. This ionic current arises from the field-driven drift of space charge, typically assumed to be charged oxygen vacancies \cite{saha2001transient}. The observed conductance variations, however, appear to stem from two simultaneous modulations. An ionic current could explain these only if the space charge drifted at constant velocity indefinitely. A stronger account treats the transient as an electronic current modulated by changes in device conductance \cite{meyer2005oxygen}. Electronic conductance in \abbrev{MIM} devices can change through several simultaneous mechanisms, and the literature generally favours electronic over ionic explanations \cite{wakiya2006activation, el2007ionic}. \\

\noindent An \abbrev{MIM} device comprises three series components: a metal-insulator interface, a bulk insulator, and a second metal-insulator interface \cite{sze2021physics}. All three determine conductance, but one usually dominates through much higher resistivity. Which layer dominates in devices showing current transients is still unknown. Rectifying behaviour implies interface-limited conductance, yet interface models do not necessarily capture the dynamics accurately. \\

\noindent Under reverse bias, the applied voltage raises the energy barrier at the rectifying metal-oxide interface and restricts charge carrier injection; the interface then has high resistivity and dictates the overall device conductance, making an interface model most accurate. Under forward bias, the applied voltage lowers this barrier and lets carriers inject freely, so the interface becomes highly conductive. The bulk oxide then dominates the total resistance, calling for a bulk-based model.\\

\noindent Determining the physical origin of current transients requires examining all device layers. The amorphous bulk oxide is highly insulating because of its large bandgap. At high voltages and low temperatures, Fowler-Nordheim tunnelling can dominate conduction, but direct tunnelling is highly unlikely for oxide layers thicker than $\sim$1 nm and is therefore negligible in the 35 nm films studied here. Electron trap states instead enable higher current densities through alternative bulk pathways such as trap-assisted tunnelling or Poole-Frenkel hopping. Interface-limited mechanisms such as Schottky emission may also contribute, depending on barrier height and temperature. \\

\noindent The amorphous silicon oxide studied here holds more oxygen vacancies than stoichiometric silica, and these vacancies act as the electron and hole traps that mediate conduction \cite{el2013identification, gao2016mechanism}. \abbrev{TEM} \cite{munde2017intrinsic, wu2015evolution} and conductive atomic force microscopy (\abbrevdef{CAFM}) \cite{buckwell2015conductance} resolve conductive bridges formed through these trap states. Trapped electrons hop between them or into the conduction band via Poole--Frenkel emission \cite{frenkel1938pre}. This trap-hosting role of the vacancies is independent of the question, addressed below, of which species drifts to cause the decay.

\begin{align}
    \sigma_{PF} &= q \mu_n n_{PF} = q \mu_n n_0 \exp\left( -\frac{q\left(\phi_B - \Delta\phi_{PF}\right)}{k_B T} \right) \label{eq:4.14} \\
    J_{PF}      &= E \sigma_{PF} = q \mu_n n_0 E \exp\left( -\frac{q\left(\phi_B - \Delta\phi_{PF}\right)}{k_B T} \right) \label{eq:4.15}
\end{align}

\noindent Equations (\ref{eq:4.14}) and (\ref{eq:4.15}) write the Poole--Frenkel conductivity $\sigma_{PF}$ and current density $J_{PF}$ in the form derived in Section \ref{subsec:transport} (Equation \ref{eq:ct-pf1}). Here the field-dependent barrier reduction $\Delta \phi_{PF} = \sqrt{qE/\pi \varepsilon}$ lowers the trap barrier $\phi_B$ and raises the free-electron concentration from $n_0$ to $n_{PF}$. For the transient analysis, what matters is that the device conductance depends on two factors, both central to modelling: the trap carrier concentration $n_0$ and the field-dependent barrier reduction.\\

\noindent Poole-Frenkel conduction is not the only mechanism for trap-mediated conductivity \cite{dimaria1995mechanism}. Trap-assisted tunnelling (\abbrevdef{TAT}) transfers carriers between proximate traps \cite{jimenez2001physical} and describes conduction in resistive switching amorphous silicon dioxide \cite{mehonic2012resistive}. Inelastic trap-assisted tunnelling (\abbrevdef{ITAT}) \cite{ielmini2000modelingII} has electrons tunnel into traps and relax via phonon emission, enabling hopping between spatially and energetically distributed traps. Both Poole-Frenkel and \abbrev{ITAT} models can explain conductance changes during current transients. This agrees with the steady-state conduction analysis of Chapter \ref{ch:fab} (Table \ref{table:3a}), where fitting the measured I-V characteristics of the same device family to each candidate mechanism identified trap-assisted tunnelling as the dominant steady-state channel. \\

\noindent Charge trapping drives the conductivity increase. The Poole--Frenkel model of Equation~(\ref{eq:4.15}) makes the current proportional to the populated trap concentration $n_0$ and the thermal excitation probability. Traps in a grounded device are initially depleted, so conductance is low when bias is first applied. Under bias the population fills, and treating it as a time-dependent $n(t)$ rather than a static constant directly explains the rising transient current. The \abbrev{ITAT} model behaves the same way, through time-varying trap occupancy.

\begin{align}
    E(x) &= \frac{V}{d} \label{eq:4.16} \\
    E(x) &= \frac{V}{d} - \frac{Q x}{\varepsilon} \label{eq:4.17} \\
    E(x) &= \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{V}{d} - \dfrac{Q x}{\varepsilon}, & x < \delta \\[4pt]
        \dfrac{V}{d} - \dfrac{Q \delta}{\varepsilon},          & x \geq \delta
    \end{cases} \label{eq:4.18}
\end{align}

\noindent The decay is the complementary effect: because the trap-assisted current depends exponentially on the local field, anything that screens that field sharply lowers the current. A drifting mobile space charge does exactly this. In an ideal insulator the field is uniform (\ref{eq:4.16}), and a homogeneous space charge $Q$ tilts it (\ref{eq:4.17}). Charge spread through the bulk shares the potential drop and barely perturbs individual traps. Charge that accumulates within an interfacial region of width $\delta$ (\ref{eq:4.18}), by contrast, concentrates the field reduction there, lowering the field at nearby traps so the device current falls.\\

\noindent Interface-based models, often used to explain resistance switching in memristors, offer an alternative. Rectifying behaviour suggests a Schottky-like barrier at the metal-oxide interface, formed when defective oxide behaves as an n-type \cite{fujii2005hysteretic} or p-type \cite{sawa2004hysteretic} semiconductor. Work function offsets drive charge transfer between the metal and oxide defects, forming a space charge region and an electronic barrier. Conductance changes then follow from modulations in barrier height (affecting thermionic current) or width (affecting tunnelling current).\\

\noindent Interface states \cite{seong2007hpha}, or surface states \cite{bardeen1947surface}, influence the interface space charge. Depending on their type (n-type or p-type), they can modulate barrier height or width \cite{cowley1965surface}. High interface state concentrations can pin the Fermi level, rendering work functions irrelevant, and charge trapping in these states can modulate the barrier; surface states in metal-amorphous silicon Schottky barriers, for example, decrease barrier height \cite{wronski1977surface}.\\

\noindent Bulk and interface pictures need not compete. In a bulk-limited device, mobile space charge reducing local fields explains the decay while trap filling drives the increase. In an interface-limited one, charge trapping in surface states can raise conductance by Fermi-pinning while defect drift to the contact modulates band alignments and causes decay \cite{asanuma2009relationship}. The true mechanism likely combines the two. \\

\noindent Two points are deliberately left open here and settled by the modelling of Chapter~\ref{ch:model}. First, the drifting space charge is taken throughout this chapter to be charged oxygen vacancies, following the prevailing literature, but its identity is not fixed by the measurements so far. Second, conduction is treated as electronic and trap-mediated, the mobile charge acting only by screening the local field rather than carrying appreciable current itself. Chapter~\ref{ch:model} resolves the species by electrode substitution and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, which point instead to mobile protons (with oxygen vacancies a coupled second species), and confirms the conduction stays electronic throughout.

\section[Summary]{Summary}

\noindent This chapter establishes the subthreshold regime as a distinct operational mode for silicon oxide memristors, capable of concurrent potentiation and depression under unipolar voltage pulses. Unlike the binary switching of the previous chapter, these transient dynamics bridge the pristine and electroformed states. The methodology developed here for inducing and tuning them provides the experimental basis for the physical modelling framework of the next chapter. Through the same rate-dependent conductance change, the device emulates the signatures of synaptic homeostasis, habituation, and short-term memory. It implements forgetting processes \cite{li2020enhanced} and Spike-Rate-Dependent Plasticity (\abbrevdef{SRDP}) \cite{huang2019binary} directly, so simplifying the hardware for Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) by removing the need for inhibitory pulses. The homeostatic behaviour is shown here at the single-device level as a proof of mechanism. Its statistical reproducibility across devices is left to the modelling and system-level chapters that follow.\\

\noindent Their slow dynamics limit high-frequency operation, but these transients suit biological timescales and monitoring applications \cite{vieluf2022seizure}. Future optimisation hinges on the interplay between interface-limited charge trapping and bulk defect migration. The regime is thus validated as a sound basis for single-rail neuromorphic circuits that exploit inherent material physics for efficient, brain-inspired computation.
