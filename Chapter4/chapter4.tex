\chapter{Switching and Volatile Conductance}\label{ch:transients}

\noindent Electroforming and constant-current conditioning place SiO$_x$ devices in two distinct operating regimes,
the first enabling non-volatile switching and the second producing a pre-electroformed current transient that rises
under bias and then decays. Each preparation route was applied to a separate device family of silicon-rich SiO$_x$
from the same fabrication platform, so the two regimes arise in one material system while remaining distinct
evidence. Pulse experiments on the conditioned devices then test how amplitude and spacing control that
transient.\\

\noindent Earlier work established the conditioning protocol and step-voltage transient
\cite{mannion2022current, mannion2023unipolar}. The author reproduced the conditioned regime and carried out the
pulse-train experiments, and the contribution claimed here is the demonstration that pulse amplitude and spacing
control a reversible volatile conductance state. These measurements do not identify its microscopic species or
implement learning in hardware.

\section[Resistive Switching and Conduction]{Resistive Switching and Conduction}\label{sec:4p-switch}

\noindent Switching measurements from 25 samples span two device families that differ in electrode stack and in
switching polarity together. Symmetric Mo\allowbreak/SiO$_x$\allowbreak/Au
devices switch unipolarly, whereas asymmetric Mo\allowbreak/SiO$_x$\allowbreak/Ti\allowbreak/Au devices switch
bipolarly. In both families, an irreversible electroforming event enables subsequent reversible transitions between
the high-resistance (\abbrev{HRS}) and
low-resistance (\abbrev{LRS}) states. Because stack and forming conditions differ together, the families are not a
controlled electrode comparison, and no device identifier links the two measurement campaigns, so their evidence units
remain separate. The switching measurements define the non-volatile regime used below to distinguish the conditioned,
pre-electroformed transient.

\subsection[Switching Modes]{Switching Modes}

\noindent Electroforming of the symmetric Mo\allowbreak/SiO$_x$\allowbreak/Au devices proceeded under a positive sweep
to $18$\,V (Figure~\ref{fig:3form}a). Current fluctuated before rising abruptly near $14$\,V as the
device entered the \abbrev{LRS}, and subsequent sweeps switched smoothly between stable states. The asymmetric
Mo\allowbreak/SiO$_x$\allowbreak/Ti\allowbreak/Au devices were formed instead under a negative sweep to $-10$\,V with
$0.1$\,mA compliance (Figure~\ref{fig:3form}b).\\

\noindent Forming leaves behind a stable, non-volatile \abbrev{LRS}: a $3$\,V sweep under $3$\,mA
compliance sets the device near a threshold of $2.3$\,V, and lowering the voltage below that threshold leaves the state
unchanged (Figure~\ref{fig:3sw}a) \cite{mehonic2017intrinsic}. Reset occurs only when the current reaches a level about
an order of magnitude above the Set compliance, returning the device to the \abbrev{HRS}. Without compliance, the sweep
can instead cause breakdown or multiple transitions. The \abbrev{HRS} conductance lies between the \abbrev{LRS} and the
pristine state. These abrupt transitions show no clear dependence on sweep speed, and they repeat over the sweeps taken
on that device.\\

\noindent A second unipolar mode operates without any imposed compliance, the device setting near $\sim$1.15\,V and
resetting near $\sim$2.85\,V once the current exceeds its critical level, so that the switching becomes self-limiting
(Figure~\ref{fig:3sw}b). In the asymmetric family, a $-2$\,V sweep set the device after forming and a $+2$\,V sweep
reset it (Figure~\ref{fig:3sw}c). The $100$\,mA compliance used for the illustrated Set transition was chosen to show
the full conductance contrast and does not represent routine operation.\\

\noindent Area scaling distinguishes conduction distributed across the contact from a localised path, because a
distributed current should grow with pad area and a localised current need not. Every measured contact size supported
unipolar switching as well as bipolar switching, with the unipolar pads switching at
$\sim$3.5\,V under a $3$\,mA compliance and resetting at $\sim$5\,mA (Figure~\ref{fig:3k}). The bipolar pads took
$-2$\,V Set sweeps at $5$\,mA compliance with $+2$\,V Reset sweeps at $100$\,mA (Figure~\ref{fig:3o}). No monotonic scaling of \abbrev{LRS} current with pad
area appears in the unipolar measurements, and the bipolar thresholds and contrast vary
between pads, so the present measurements support only a qualitative area comparison.\\

both device families access them. Successive unipolar Set sweeps produce
two stable low-resistance levels (Figure~\ref{fig:3p}), while restricted bipolar sweeps change the conductance
gradually before the final transition (Figure~\ref{fig:3q}). These measurements show that multilevel operation is
available in both families, without quantifying how repeatably the intermediate levels are reached.\\

\noindent Cycling tests measure how long the non-volatile states remain distinguishable and how switching eventually
fails. Under pulsed cycling, a unipolar device sets on a $100$\,ns, $4.5$\,V pulse and resets
on a longer $2$\,ms, $2.5$\,V pulse, each followed by a $1$\,ms, $0.7$\,V reading pulse. The read resistances form two bands
spanning roughly two orders of magnitude, with the \abbrev{LRS} near $50$\,k$\Omega$ and the \abbrev{HRS} near
$3$\,M$\Omega$. The median \abbrev{HRS}/\abbrev{LRS} ratio is about $60$, and individual cycles exceed $200$.\\

\noindent A bipolar device cycles under $-2$\,V Set and $+2$\,V Reset pulses, each followed by five
$\pm0.1$\,V reading pulses chosen to measure the resistance without triggering switching. Cycling in this configuration
gives an \abbrev{HRS} of $\sim$300\,$\Omega$ and an \abbrev{LRS} of $\sim$100\,$\Omega$ (Figure~\ref{fig:3n}). Both are
two-terminal reads, so each includes the probe, lead, and contact resistance in series with the oxide, and at values
this low that series contribution is not negligible. These reads therefore bound the device resistance from above and
do not isolate the switching layer.\\

\noindent Reducing the compliance current extends endurance at the cost of resistance contrast: at the $100$\,mA
compliance used above, the device endures only a few tens of cycles before it loses switching irreversibly. Over
repeated cycling the
\abbrev{LRS} first converges to a narrower, lower resistance range, and the device eventually fails at high resistance
with switching lost altogether.\\

\noindent Volatile relaxation also appears after electroforming, so its presence alone does not identify the
conditioned transient. Under constant voltage, a
unipolar device sets and then relaxes, with the fitted time constant decreasing from about $7$\,s at $1.4$\,V to
$1$\,s at $1.8$\,V (Figure~\ref{fig:3r}). Constant current instead drives repeated voltage spikes that grow in
amplitude and frequency until the bipolar device switches and the spiking subsides (Figure~\ref{fig:3s}). Both
responses belong to the electroformed device and are distinct from the pre-electroformed transient examined in
Section~\ref{sec:4p-transient}.

\subsection[Transport and Filament Interpretation]{Transport and Filament Interpretation}

\noindent The current--voltage fits and the filament model address different parts of the switching process. The fits
describe current through the formed device, whereas the filament model interprets how the conductive path forms and
resets. Unipolar Reset occurs only after a critical current, bipolar Reset requires an opposing field, and neither
family shows clear scaling with contact area. These observations are consistent with localised conduction, thermal
rupture in the unipolar mode, and field-driven reconfiguration in the bipolar mode. Since the stack carries no active
metal electrode, a conductive-metal bridge (\abbrevdef{CBRAM}) is an unlikely origin of the conductive path
\cite{mehonic2012resistive}.\\

\noindent Earlier fitting on the same platform favoured trap-assisted tunnelling over the other candidate
linearised transport models in both switching modes. That work also interpreted the near-ohmic low-resistance state
after unipolar switching as a more continuous filament. The fitted trap depths of a few tenths of an electronvolt are
comparable with those reported for stress-induced leakage in thin silicon dioxide \cite{mehonic2014resistive, mehonic2012resistive, mehonic2012electrically, dimaria1995mechanism}. In the same work, bipolar Reset was assigned to
field-driven motion and unipolar Reset to thermal rupture, consistent with the polarity dependence measured above.\\

\noindent Figure~\ref{fig:4filament} shows the vacancy-based interpretation adopted from that literature. In this
account, the silicon-rich oxide phase-separates under the applied field, and both switching families use the resulting
defect-rich path \cite{gao2016mechanism}. Hydrogen and other mobile species have been proposed for the same role
\cite{cox2024hydrogen}, so the vacancy account is carried through the sections below as a working interpretation of
both switching families.\\

\begin{figure}[htbp!]
    \centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Chapter4/Figs/filament_mechanism_schematic.pdf}}
    \caption[Conceptual vacancy-filament model.]{\emph{Conceptual} vacancy-filament model
    adopted from the literature: dispersed defects before forming, a conductive path after forming, and a ruptured
    path after Reset.}
    \label{fig:4filament}
\end{figure}

\noindent In the bipolar working picture, electroforming reorganises intrinsic defect-rich regions into a conductive
path without an added mobile-metal source. Sputtered silicon suboxides grow with a columnar microstructure, oxygen
vacancies are expected to concentrate at column boundaries and oxide--electrode interfaces where the defect density
is highest \cite{munde2017intrinsic}, and vacancy diffusion remains slow at ambient temperature.\\

\noindent Under bias, oxygen anions drift towards and accumulate at the interface, and the displaced oxygen has been
proposed to form molecular $O_2$ that, under sufficiently aggressive stressing, can nucleate gas pockets there
\cite{mehonic2017intrinsic}. Electroforming is understood to reduce the oxide locally, removing oxygen and leaving a
vacancy-rich pathway that carries current. This structural change provides the working interpretation of the
conductance jump at forming. Once the filament shunts the applied field, the controlled breakdown self-terminates
before it develops into destructive hard breakdown.\\

\noindent Unipolar switching shares this filament picture but differs in its reset. The unipolar filament is described as
denser and more nanowire-like, consistent with the low-resistance, weakly voltage-dependent \abbrev{LRS} current.
The resulting current drives Joule heating until the filament ruptures above a critical level. Bipolar Reset is
instead attributed to oxygen anions returning to vacancies under the opposing field. Current compliance can restrict
filament growth, so partial or parallel filaments provide one interpretation of the intermediate conductance states.\\

\noindent These constant-drive measurements do not distinguish repeated filament rupture
\cite{gao2016mechanism, buckwell2015conductance} from trap filling \cite{el2013identification, zhong2010transient},
thermal effects \cite{shen2021experimentally}, or ionic motion \cite{meyer2005oxygen, el2007ionic}. Repeated formation
and rupture could account for the voltage bursts and their stronger, faster response at larger drive, but several
processes may contribute together. Pad-to-pad variation in switching voltage and contrast was not characterised
statistically, so the area comparison remains qualitative. Competing conduction channels may also coexist across a
sweep, and separating their individual contributions lies beyond both the present measurements and the earlier
transport fits \cite{mehonic2014resistive, mehonic2012resistive}.

\section[Subthreshold Current Transient]{Subthreshold Current Transient}\label{sec:4p-transient}

\noindent Constant-current conditioning produces a separate, pre-electroformed operating regime whose conductance
first rises and then decays under a fixed voltage, giving a volatile analogue state rather than non-volatile
switching. Conditioning establishes the device state before measurement; the subsequent voltage step reads and
perturbs that state without electroforming the device.

\subsection[Transient Response and Operating Window]{Transient Response and Operating Window}

\noindent Under a negative step potential on the gold electrode, the device potentiates within seconds, and a slower
depression phase follows that drives the conductance below its initial level over hundreds of seconds
(Figure~\ref{fig:4step}a). These two phases respond differently to the applied magnitude: at low magnitude,
$-1$\,V, the response is predominantly potentiating, and larger magnitudes introduce progressively stronger
depression together with a faster decay after the peak. Potentiation occurs at every voltage measured and accelerates
with magnitude.\\

\noindent Across 132 repeated measurements on one device at $-0.8$\,V, the response retains the same rise-and-decay
form although its magnitude varies (Figure~\ref{fig:4step}b). Reproducing that form requires full relaxation between
measurements, obtained by grounding both contacts for an hour or by applying a positive potential to the gold contact.\\

\noindent Holding the current rather than the voltage keeps the conditioning self-limiting. A rising conductance
lowers the field across the film instead of raising the current through it, so the runaway that drives electroforming
is suppressed and the device is left conditioned but unformed. This protocol, described in
Section~\ref{sec:3cond} \cite{mannion2023unipolar, mannion2022current}, was applied to the unelectroformed,
Ti/Au-contacted devices of Section~\ref{sec:matstack} \cite{mehonic2017intrinsic}. The conditioning study of
Figure~\ref{fig:4stack} used $200 \times 200\,\mu$m contacts, and the device voltage fell as the conductance rose
(Figure~\ref{fig:4stack}a).\\

\noindent Both the conditioned baseline and the later transient shape are associated with the conditioning current. Across
seven devices, larger conditioning currents accompanied stronger initial potentiation: the $-0.1\,\mu$A device
showed only depression, whereas the $-100\,\mu$A device reached nearly ten times its initial conductance
(Figure~\ref{fig:4stack}b). Each stress level was applied to a different device, so conditioning magnitude and device
identity vary together, and no device was conditioned twice at different currents. The ordering across the seven
devices is therefore an association and not a measured causal dependence. Conditioning current is a preparation variable;
pulse amplitude later controls the response during operation.\\

\noindent Negative current applied to the gold contact produced transients; under the opposite polarity, the
earlier study reported roughening at the molybdenum contact but no transient \cite{mannion2022current}. Transient
amplitude followed conditioning stress more closely than final conductance, pointing to an internal state
established during conditioning rather than to conductance alone. The proposal that the total defect number
governs that state \cite{el2007ionic} is untested here.\\

\noindent Excessive stress produced visible contact damage, orders of magnitude larger than the nanoscale conductive
filaments imaged in these devices \cite{buckwell2015conductance} and similar to damage observed in electroformed
devices \cite{waser1990dcI, waser1990dcII}. This damage was not required for the transient, and all transient
measurements remained below its onset. The operating window therefore requires negative conditioning strong enough to
produce the transient but below visible contact damage and non-volatile switching.\\

\noindent Duration alone separates this response from a compact passive \abbrev{RC} decay. The
peak typically falls between 5 and 20 seconds, and the complete transient can last hundreds of seconds
\cite{saha2001transient}, a duration that no passive \abbrev{RC} time constant can explain
\cite{el2006space}. The largest pad measured holds about a nanofarad, including probe and instrument capacitance,
which at the megohm resistances of this regime gives a time constant of order ten milliseconds, four orders of
magnitude shorter than the observed peak.\\

\noindent Within this nondestructive window the transient provides a volatile analogue state that can retain recent
activity across a delayed signal, and the non-volatile switching of Section~\ref{sec:4p-switch} supplies the separate
persistent weight. A physical account of the transient must therefore explain the rapid rise, slower decay, and
dependence on prior conditioning without invoking non-volatile switching.

\subsection[Physical Interpretation]{Physical Interpretation}

\noindent The rise and the decay place different demands on the mechanism: the initial rise requires electronic
conduction to increase once bias is applied, whereas the slower decay requires that same conduction to be
progressively suppressed. Conditioning changes the strength of both responses without producing non-volatile
switching \cite{mannion2022current, mannion2023unipolar}. These observations motivate a separation between the
measured electronic current and a slower internal state that modulates it
\cite{kim2015secondorder, du2017metal}.\\

\noindent A transient space-charge model treats a mobile charge front as crossing a trap-free insulator and
accumulating at a blocking electrode. Developed for transient space-charge-limited currents in insulators, it was
later applied to oxygen-vacancy motion in oxides \cite{many1962theory, lampert1970current, zafar1998oxygen}. Under
constant voltage, it predicts the arrival time given by Equation~\ref{eq:4.1},
\begin{align}
t_p &= \frac{2 d^2}{\mu V} \left[ 1 - \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \right) \right] \approx 0.787 \frac{d^2}{\mu V} \label{eq:4.1}
\end{align}

\noindent where $t_p$ is the arrival time, $d$ the insulator thickness, $\mu$ the carrier mobility and
$V = |V_{applied}|$ the magnitude of the applied voltage, the transient regime being driven at negative bias. The
model predicts $t_p \propto d^2$ and a linear relation between $t_p$ and $1/V$, and the present measurements test
neither: the conditioned family was made at one oxide thickness, and the transient peaks sit on shallow maxima whose
position moves with how the peak is defined. Equation~\ref{eq:4.1} therefore cannot support extracting a mobility or
an activation energy from the present transient.\\

\noindent Assessment of the scaling therefore relies on measurements published for other oxides. Transit-limited
space charge is set aside as the account of the peak because published tests that extract a mobility from such a peak
do not consistently observe the required thickness and voltage scalings \cite{zafar1998oxygen, el2006space, saha2001transient}. Slower ionic redistribution may still contribute to the transient even though the transit model
does not describe the peak.\\

\noindent Drift--diffusion simulations assign these roles differently \cite{meyer2005oxygen}: electrons and holes
redistribute quickly enough to carry the measured current, whereas the slower ionic displacement current is too
small to explain the rise. This mobile charge can nevertheless alter the internal field and thereby modulate
electronic conduction. Independent transient measurements have also resolved shallow electronic traps
\cite{zhong2010transient}, supporting trap-mediated transport rather than a current carried directly by migrating
ions.\\

\noindent The working interpretation assigns the measured current and its slower modulation to different processes.
Electronic transport through trap-assisted tunnelling or Poole--Frenkel channels carries the measured current
(Section~\ref{subsec:transport}), with a possible contribution from the electrode interface. Trap filling raises the
current after bias is applied \cite{el2013identification}, and slower charge redistribution then screens the local
field and lowers the trap-mediated current. This sequence is consistent with the measured rise and decay, although
those transients do not identify either process or separate bulk screening from interface trapping. Under pulsed
drive, each pulse promotes filling, while the interval before the next pulse permits relaxation, so
Section~\ref{sec:4p-pulse} tests pulse amplitude and spacing as controls of these competing responses.

\section[Pulse-Driven Conductance Dynamics]{Pulse-Driven Conductance Dynamics}\label{sec:4p-pulse}

\noindent A pulse train separates the state written during each pulse from the relaxation before the next pulse.
Amplitude controls the change induced by each event, while spacing controls how much survives to the following
event. Each figure in this section reports one measurement on one conditioned device, so the results establish the
existence and reversibility of the response without defining a population-level amplitude or rate law.

\subsection[Pulse Response and Amplitude Dependence]{Pulse Response and Amplitude Dependence}

\noindent Every pulse run follows the ordering and recovery controls of Sections~\ref{sec:3pulse}
and~\ref{sec:3cond}. Amplitude sweeps proceed from the smallest magnitude upwards, rate sweeps proceed from the
longest period downwards, and the pre-stimulus baseline is checked against the value measured before the preceding
run. Under Gaussian pulses of $-3$\,V amplitude, 20\,ms full width at half maximum, and 300\,ms period, the current
rises over the first four pulses and then decays (Figure~\ref{fig:4g}). Potentiation and depression here denote those
increases and decreases in device conductance. Conductance relaxes after the train is interrupted, which is the
signature of a volatile state. Many memristive synapses require opposite polarities for the two responses, and
reported single-polarity alternatives often switch discretely \cite{jo2010nanoscale, khan2020comparison, mehonic2012electrically}, whereas the conditioned device produces both under negative pulses.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering

    \caption[Device response to a spike train.]{Device current under a Gaussian spike train applied to the top contact,
    20\,ms full width at half maximum, $-3$\,V amplitude and 300\,ms period, after conditioning at $-100\,\mu$A for
    100\,s. The current polarity is inverted for clarity. First published in co-authored work
    \cite{mannion2023unipolar}.}
    \label{fig:4g}
\end{figure}

\noindent This reversal occurs while pulse amplitude and spacing remain fixed, so the response cannot be described as
a fixed conductance increment accumulated once per pulse. Each pulse instead acts on the state left by earlier pulses:
the initial pulses increase conductance, and the balance shifts until the slower depressing response dominates. The
train of constant amplitude and period therefore shows that the pulse response changes as the device state evolves,
and the amplitude sweep that follows tests whether the applied magnitude shifts the balance between potentiation and
depression.\\

\noindent Increasing the pulse magnitude shifts this balance towards depression. Relative to the pre-train
conductance, the cumulative change remains positive from $-2.5$ to $-3.75$\,V but becomes negative at $-4.5$\,V
(Figure~\ref{fig:4pulse}). The crossover shows that pulse amplitude selects the net response during operation, and
its direction is consistent with a field-dependent process becoming more influential at larger amplitudes.
Conditioning establishes the device's initial operating regime; pulse amplitude then controls its response within
that regime.

\subsection[Rate Dependence and Reversibility]{Rate Dependence and Reversibility}

\noindent Pulse spacing provides a second control because the volatile state continues to relax between events.
Closely spaced pulses arrive before the preceding response has dissipated, whereas longer intervals allow greater
recovery. Each train lasts about $112$\,s, so reducing the period also increases the number of pulses and the total
electrical exposure, and the experiment therefore tests the combined effect of pulse spacing and accumulated
stimulation.\\

\noindent The mean maximum current over the final ten pulses defines the operational steady-state measure after the
response has settled. Each pulse uses the same voltage waveform, but the changing pulse count prevents a spacing-only
interpretation: the measurement does not separate timing from cumulative charge injection or average heating.
A separate $1 \rightarrow 10 \rightarrow 1$\,Hz sequence tests reversibility by returning the device to its
original rate after the period-dependent response.\\

\noindent The steady-state measure depends on pulse period (Figure~\ref{fig:4k}), showing that the device retains
information about recent stimulation over the interval tested. Increasing the rate to $10$\,Hz lowers the
conductance, and returning to $1$\,Hz restores it towards the initial level (Figure~\ref{fig:4l}). This recovery
establishes reversible device-level rate dependence, while network-level regulation remains outside the scope of the
measurement.

\section[Summary]{Summary}

\noindent Electroforming and constant-current conditioning give \abbrev{SiOx} two distinct operating roles.
Persistent states suitable for storing weights follow electroforming, while conditioning produces a volatile state
that rises during stimulation and relaxes afterwards. These two regimes were measured in separate device families, so
their coexistence within one element remains untested.\\

\noindent Within the conditioned regime, pulse amplitude controls the state change, and pulse spacing controls how
much survives between events. Rapid trap filling opposed by slower field screening is consistent with the response,
although the mobile species and rate-limiting process remain unresolved. This local, timing-sensitive state is used
for biorealistic learning, while separate circuitry must read it and convert it into a weight change.
