\chapter{Current Transients in Memristive Devices}


\section[Theoretical Background of Current Transients]{Theoretical Background of Current Transients}

This thesis initially characterizes silicon-based memristors as fundamental components for memristive systems. This chapter investigates "current transients", a phenomenon largely unexplored in neuromorphic computing. Current transients manifest as dynamic conductance changes—exhibiting both potentiation and depression—in defective capacitors responding to step potentials. These rich dynamics offer significant potential for computational devices. \\

\noindent The chapter begins by reviewing the literature on current transients. It then details the experimental procedures and strategies developed to characterize them. These investigations clarify the underlying physical models, facilitating the development of integrative memristive systems based on silicon oxide.

\subsection[Transient Characteristics and Volatility]{Transient Characteristics and Volatility}

Ideally, capacitive decay and dielectric relaxation govern a capacitor's response to a step voltage. When a constant voltage is applied, the current declines to a steady leakage value—zero in an ideal device. However, prolonged voltage stress or elevated temperatures can degrade the oxide, reducing its resistance and causing the current to rise \cite{ghibaudo1999emerging}. One specific form of this degradation is the "current transient", which creates a characteristic peak on a current-time graph (Figure \ref{fig:4a}) as the current swiftly rises to a maximum and then gradually decays. Surprisingly, unlike permanent oxide degradation, this resistance change is volatile. \\

    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Chapter4/Figs/a.png}
    \caption[Current transients illustration]{Current transient caused by a change over time for a closed circuit \cite{zafar2011measurement}.}
    \label{fig:4a}
\end{figure}

\noindent The transient's characteristic peak and long duration distinguish it from simple RC decay. The peak typically occurs after 5 to 20 seconds, and the entire event can last for hundreds of seconds \cite{saha2001transient}. These timescales are far too long for standard integrated resistor-capacitor (RC) circuits, which would need impractically large capacitors to replicate such behavior \cite{el2006space}. \\

\noindent The transient's appeal lies in its long duration, which enables computations on timescales far exceeding those of simple RC circuits. However, these dynamics are not fixed; increasing the temperature \cite{manceau2007metal}, voltage \cite{zafar1998oxygen}, or laser irradiation \cite{li2015visible} accelerates the transient, causing its peak to appear sooner. If accelerated enough, the peak occurs too quickly for the measurement equipment's temporal resolution to capture, making it disappear and leaving only a decaying current. \\

\noindent Given that the features of transients have been similar across published research, it is worth noting that these studies have used a variety of oxide materials \cite{saha2001transient}. However, despite being present in a variety of device setups, the current transient is not observed in every instance, and this is especially noticeable with regard to device polarity. Early investigations on current transients only detected transients in a single voltage polarity \cite{manceau2007current}, however other devices in the literature now produce transients in both polarities \cite{chen2010observation}. \\

\noindent Current transients characterized by a distinct peak and subsequent decay under constant voltage stress have been reported in a wide range of materials, implying that they are a generic phenomenon in MIM devices. These transients deviate from typical resistance switching behaviors in silicon oxide devices; they are analog and volatile, contrasting with the non-volatile binary switching observed in standard operation. Understanding the source and features of these specific current transients is significant because they may have ramifications for a variety of research areas studying metal-oxide structures, such as transistor gates or the comparatively emerging subject of resistive switching memory.

\subsection[Space Charge Limited Current Models]{Space Charge Limited Current Models}

The Space Charge Limited Current (SCLC) hypothesis remains the most prominent model for explaining current transients. Originally developed to describe space charge in a perfect insulator \cite{many1962theory}, it has been adapted to account for charged oxygen vacancies \cite{zafar1998oxygen}. Under an applied bias, an ionic current flows alongside the electronic current, driven by the drift of charged species—typically oxygen vacancies. The metal electrode blocks these ions, causing accumulation at the interface. This accumulation creates an opposing internal electric field (Coulombic repulsion) that impedes further migration, causing the ionic current to decay. Consequently, early research attributed transient behaviors to space charge accumulation within a trap-free insulator, establishing the SCLC model. \\

\noindent The original SCLC model assumes one-dimensional linear conduction with uniform current flow between parallel electrodes \cite{many1962theory, lampert1970current}. While applicable to both electronic and ionic carriers, subsequent studies link the transient to charged defect migration within the oxide \cite{zafar1998oxygen}. The mobility term, $\mu$, represents moving oxygen vacancies \cite{zafar1998oxygen} or other mobile ions \cite{chen2010observation}. In this framework, the ionic current modulates the dominant electronic current. The model defines the total current density $j(t)$ as:
\begin{align}
j(t) &= j_{cond} + j_{disp} \label{eq:4.1} \\
j_{cond}(x,t) &= qn(x,t)\mu E(x,t) \label{eq:4.2} \\
j_{disp}(x,t) &= \epsilon \frac{\partial E(x,t)}{\partial t} \label{eq:4.3} \\
qn(x,t) &=  \epsilon \frac{\partial E(x,t)}{\partial x} \label{eq:4.4}
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $j(t)$ sums the conduction current density, $j_{cond}$, and the displacement current density, $j_{disp}$. The displacement current accounts for the time-varying electric field. $q$ is the elementary charge, $n$ is the bulk space charge concentration, $\mu$ is mobility, $E$ is the local electric field, $L$ is oxide thickness, and $\epsilon$ is permittivity. Equation (\ref{eq:4.4}) is Poisson's equation.

\begin{align}
j(t) &= \epsilon \left( \frac{\mu}{2} \frac{\partial E^{2}(x,t)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial E(x,t)}{\partial t} \right) \label{eq:4.5} \\
j(t) &= \frac{\epsilon \mu}{2L}\left( E^{2}_{a}(t) - E^{2}_{c}(t) \right) \label{eq:4.6} \\
j(t) &= \frac{\mu Q(t)}{2L}\left( 2E_a(t) - \frac{Q(t)}{\epsilon} \right) \label{eq:4.7}
\end{align}

\noindent Substituting the current components (\ref{eq:4.2}, \ref{eq:4.3}) and Poisson's equation (\ref{eq:4.4}) into (\ref{eq:4.1}) yields the device current density (\ref{eq:4.5}). Even with a fixed voltage, the current varies with time as the internal field $E(x,t)$ evolves. Integrating (\ref{eq:4.5}) from cathode to anode yields (\ref{eq:4.6}), relating current to the electrode fields $E_a(t)$ and $E_c(t)$. Assuming a constant applied voltage ($\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = 0$), this simplifies to (\ref{eq:4.7}) in terms of total insulator charge $Q(t)$. \\

\noindent To determine the peak time $\tau$, defined as the moment the space charge front reaches the anode, the evolution of the electric field must be analyzed. Although the applied voltage $V$ remains constant, the local electric field $E_a(t)$ at the anode varies over time. As the space charge redistributes across the device, the internal electric field profile is altered. To maintain a constant total voltage drop $V$ (which is the spatial integral of the electric field across the thickness $L$), the field ahead of the migrating charge front must progressively increase. Assuming an Ohmic contact at the cathode ($E_c(t) \approx 0$) and that the charge front has not yet reached the anode (implying $j_{cond}(L,t) = 0$), the total current at the anode is purely displacement current: $j(t) = \epsilon \frac{\partial E_a(t)}{\partial t}$. Equating this with the current derived in (\ref{eq:4.6}) yields the differential equation (\ref{eq:4.8}). Integrating this equation with the uniform initial field condition $E_a(0) = V/L$ provides the time-dependent electric field at the anode (\ref{eq:4.9}).

\begin{align}
\frac{dE_a}{E_a^2} &= \frac{\mu}{2L} dt \label{eq:4.8} \\
E_a(t) &= \frac{V}{L}\left( 1 - \frac{\mu V t}{2L^2} \right)^{-1} \label{eq:4.9} \\
L &= \mu \int_{0}^{\tau} E_a(t) dt \label{eq:4.10} \\
L &= -2L \left[ ln \left( 1 - \frac{\mu V t}{2L^2} \right) \right]_0^{\tau} \label{eq:4.11} \\
\tau &= \frac{2 L^2}{\mu V} \left[ 1 - exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \right) \right] \cong 0.787 \frac{L^2}{\mu V} \label{eq:4.12} 
\end{align}

\noindent After the field at the anode has been determined, it is now possible to calculate the time it takes for the front of the space charge to reach the anode. Assuming that the space charge's velocity is given by $\mu E(x,t)$, and the field is constant between the charge front and the anode, it can be assumed that the field experienced by the front of the space charge is equal to the field at the anode, $E(x,t) = E_a(t)$. \\

\noindent Equation (\ref{eq:4.12}) is critical for extracting transport parameters. It allows calculation of mobility, which, combined with the Einstein-Nernst relation, yields the activation energy for defect migration. This framework characterizes transport dynamics in current transients. Literature cites laser-induced transient acceleration as evidence of increased mobility \cite{li2015visible} or charge transitions \cite{lee2019charge}. However, relying on this model requires ensuring its validity, which presents challenges. \\

\noindent The widespread adoption of the SCLC model is largely due to the utility of Equation (\ref{eq:4.12}) in extracting fundamental transport parameters. It offers a direct method to calculate the mobility of the migrating space charge. By combining this mobility with the Einstein-Nernst relation, researchers can determine the activation energy required for the migration of specific defects, such as oxygen vacancies, distinct from the activation energy of the bulk material's electronic conductivity. Consequently, this framework has been extensively used to characterize the transport dynamics of various defects observed in current transients. \\

\noindent According to literature, the changes in the transient that occur due to laser illumination are evidence of accelerated mobility \cite{li2015visible} or charge transitions within the defect \cite{lee2019charge}. However, if researchers intend to use this model to derive mobilities and secondary observations based on changes in mobility, the model's validity must be ensured. This is where problems may arise.

\subsection[Alternative Transport Mechanisms]{Alternative Transport Mechanisms}

SCLC is not the sole model explaining these transients. While SCLC attributes the transient to substantial ionic current, alternative theories suggest ionic currents are negligible, attributing the transient to variations in bulk and interface electronic conductivity \cite{meyer2005oxygen}. Simulations of vacancy, electron, and hole redistribution within a Barium Strontium Titanate (BST) oxide layer, bounded by Schottky contacts at $BST-Pt$ interfaces, support this conclusion. These simulations employed a finite difference method to model particle redistribution driven by drift and diffusion. 
\begin{align}
    j_k = -D_k\frac{\mathrm{d} C_k}{\mathrm{d} x} + \frac{Z_k}{|Z_k|} \mu_k C_k E , \mbox{ where $k \in \left \{ n,p,V_{\ddot{o}} \right \}$ } \label{eq:4.13} 
\end{align}


\noindent Here, $k$ denotes the particle species (electrons, holes, or charged oxygen vacancies), $j_k$ is current density, $D_k$ is the diffusion coefficient, $C_k$ is particle concentration, $Z_k$ is charge in Coulombs, $\mu_k$ is mobility, and $E$ is the local electric field. The simulation iteratively determines particle redistribution and computes the total device current based on particle velocity and distribution. This total current sums the electron, hole, and vacancy currents. For each species, the current includes diffusion and drift terms, as described by the Nernst-Einstein equation (\ref{eq:4.13}). This method advantageously separates electronic and ionic currents—a feat impossible in experiments—while validating the simulation by comparing total simulated current with experimental transient data.\\

\noindent The simulation reveals a rapid, asymmetric redistribution of electrons and holes, creating a highly n-type interface at the negative electrode while slightly depleting the other. Published data indicates this rate slows as the transient peaks. Subsequently, oxygen vacancies migrate much more slowly towards the negative electrode. This migration significantly alters the internal electric field, bending the conduction band. Given the vacancies' low mobility, the resulting ionic displacement current is insufficient to cause the observed increase in device current. Instead, the current rise stems from modulated electronic conductivity driven by the altered vacancy distribution.\\

\noindent Varying the simulated device temperature yielded an activation energy for increased conductance of 0.8 eV, consistent with vacancies in BST devices \cite{zhong2010transient}. However, since the transient peak occurs before significant vacancy redistribution, attributing this activation energy to vacancy migration is unsound. Instead, results suggest electron and hole migration, which dominates before and during the peak, drives this behavior. Other researchers support this conclusion \cite{zhong2010transient}, observing electronic traps with activation energies of 0.18–0.3 eV. They concluded that oxygen vacancy migration alters electron/hole concentrations, thereby regulating bulk conductivity. \\

\noindent Given these conflicting theories, studies seek to determine whether the effect is electronic or ionic (SCLC). For example, an analysis of transients using both the SCLC model and electronic conductivity modulation found that the electronic model yielded a more credible dielectric constant, suggesting its superiority \cite{wakiya2006activation}. Conversely, another study \cite{el2006space} investigated whether the effect is ionic or electronic by assuming electronic traps depend on oxide crystallinity. Observing nearly identical transients in amorphous and polycrystalline devices, the authors concluded the effect is ionic, supporting previous claims \cite{zafar1998oxygen}. This deduction relies on the premise that electronic trap distributions are sensitive to structural order; thus, the invariance of transient behavior across structural differences implies a mechanism independent of crystallinity-associated trap states, pointing instead to ionic migration, such as oxygen vacancies. \\

\noindent A subsequent publication \cite{el2007ionic}, however, favored an electronic explanation based on shallow electronic traps. This theory posits that transient currents arise not from ionic displacement, but from the time-dependent filling and emptying of shallow trap states near the conduction band edge, modulating free carrier concentration and electronic conductivity. While the ionic versus electronic debate remains unresolved, the literature increasingly favors electronic conduction modulated by vacancy movement. Retrospectively, the SCLC model has proven useful but lacks full validation. While higher temperatures and voltages accelerate the transient as predicted, and peak time versus reciprocal voltage plots are linear in certain regimes, the original SCLC model warrants closer scrutiny.\\

\noindent Consider the thickness dependence of peak time predicted by Equation (\ref{eq:4.12}). If this phenomenon were solely the result of vacancies traversing the oxide, the peak time should be proportional to the square of the device thickness, $L$. Although few studies have specifically investigated this, available published data \cite{manceau2007current} demonstrates that peak timing is actually independent of device thickness. Studies examining oxide thicknesses of 900\r{A}, 450\r{A}, and 200\r{A} show no increase in peak time, completely contradicting the prediction that the 450\r{A} and 900\r{A} devices should exhibit peak times roughly 5 and 20 times greater, respectively, than the 200\r{A} device. Instead, thickness appears to affect peak height and shape rather than timing. Thinner samples exhibit broader, more dispersed peaks, while thicker samples show more pronounced peaks. This stark discrepancy between the SCLC equation and experimental evidence confirms the lack of a relationship between thickness and peak timing in practice, raising significant concerns about the model's applicability, though it is not the only issue.\\

\noindent More concerning is the predicted linear relationship between peak time and reciprocal voltage, often used as the primary indicator of SCLC behavior (\ref{eq:4.12}). Many papers claiming linearity rely on few data points. Furthermore, these plots depend on measuring peak times, a difficult process as shown in Figure \ref{fig:4b}, where shallow curves obscure the peaks. This introduces significant uncertainty for shallower transients, making peak time selection subjective. Moreover, some studies show linearity only within specific regions, while others observe exponential behavior. Although these inconsistencies require further study, they warrant skepticism in applying SCLC theory to current transients.\\

\noindent Verifying these models is critical, as they are used to determine material properties such as mobility values and activation energies directly from SCLC equations. Further studies have investigated the precise nature of defects and their transport mechanisms to ensure extracted parameters accurately represent material properties. These findings have significant implications, particularly for the nascent field of defect engineering.

\section[Device Preparation and Conditioning]{Device Preparation and Conditioning}

The device studied in this thesis has a metal-insulator-metal structure as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:4c} and was originally developed for binary resistance switching applications \cite{mehonic2017intrinsic}. The bottom metal contact is a molybdenum film of 280 nm thickness deposited via magnetron sputtering. The insulator layer is a slightly sub-stochiometric and amorphous layer of silicon oxide with a thickness of 35 nm, deposited via RF magnetron sputtering. The top metal contact is a 115 nm thick gold film deposited via e-beam evaporation through a contact mask. The shape of the top contact is a square of $200 \times 200 \mu m$ defining the active area of the device. To improve adhesion of the gold contact, a 3 nm layer of titanium was deposited prior to the gold evaporation. This also serves as a gettering layer to seed the oxide layer with oxygen vacancies.

\subsection[Defect Engineering via Current Stress]{Defect Engineering via Current Stress}

\noindent Current transients were once attributed to oxide imperfections, implying such devices were inherently faulty. However, the field of defect engineering now allows for the controlled fabrication of oxide defects. Electroforming—the application of a strong electric field to induce a partial, reversible breakdown in a Metal-Insulator-Metal (MIM) device—is a common technique in memristor and ReRAM research and is also used for the silicon oxide devices in this thesis.\\

\noindent Electroforming is a logical starting point for introducing defects, but it has limitations. The process typically induces large, discrete jumps in conductance, resulting in stable switching between high and low resistance states. These characteristics contrast sharply with the analog and volatile nature of current transients, suggesting that transients occur in the pre-electroforming regime before the device settles into stable binary behavior.\\

\noindent The conductance of a device exhibiting transients is more comparable to the High Resistance State (HRS) than the Low Resistance State (LRS) of an electroformed device, linking electroforming to current transients. This suggests that a more subtle electroforming technique, applied before the main switching event, could create the transient. However, the positive feedback inherent in electroforming makes such delicate control difficult to achieve \cite{kozicki2016electrochemical}.\\

% \noindent Current flow injects charge into the oxide, creating defects like vacancies that increase conductance. This process is inherently unstable: increased conductance leads to higher current densities, which accelerate defect creation, causing a runaway increase in current until catastrophic breakdown occurs. This exponential rise is most evident when applying high constant voltages (13V to 15V). The positive feedback makes regulated, gradual modulation of conductance difficult.\\

\noindent To achieve subtle modulation, the positive feedback of electroforming must be avoided. This is accomplished by using constant current stressing instead of conventional voltage-based electroforming. The voltage reached at the peak of constant current stressing is comparable in magnitude to the high voltages (e.g., 13–15V) applied during voltage-based electroforming.\\

% \noindent The key distinction is that during constant current stressing, the voltage across the device decreases as it breaks down, a result of the current source's negative feedback. Constant current stressing is a standard method for evaluating time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB) in oxides, a failure mode caused by defects that increase conductance via electron trapping \cite{1487102}.\\

\noindent Applying a constant current instead of a fixed voltage reduces the voltage across the device as its conductance increases. This negative feedback slows the creation of oxide defects. An equilibrium is reached when the voltage drops to a level where defect formation ceases, but current still flows. This negative feedback process allows for subtle, controlled alterations in device conductance. \\

    \label{fig:4d}

\noindent To stress the devices, the molybdenum contact is grounded and negative voltages are applied to the gold contact, injecting electrons at the gold contact and holes at the molybdenum contact. The constant current is applied for 100 seconds, with magnitudes ranging from $-0.1\mu A$ to $-100\mu A$ to achieve the desired stress level. During this process, the induced voltage across the device gradually decreases to a steady-state value (Figure \ref{fig:4d}).\\

\noindent After electrical stress, the device must relax before exhibiting current transients. Stressing displaces charged defects, likely oxygen vacancies, creating a polarized state. During relaxation, these low-mobility defects diffuse back to thermal equilibrium. Although applying a positive potential to the top contact accelerates recovery, restricted defect mobility at room temperature still typically requires a 24-hour rest. This mandatory delay confirms that stressing generates and displaces defects that must settle into a specific configuration to produce transients.

\subsection[Polarity Dependence of Induced Transients]{Polarity Dependence of Induced Transients}

The total number of oxide defects, rather than device conductance, dictates transient amplitude. Thicker devices inherently contain more defects, explaining the suppression of transients previously observed in thinner devices \cite{el2007ionic}. Although electrical stress generates these necessary defects, transients only emerge under negative current stress, which injects electrons at the top gold-titanium contact. Positive current stress at the molybdenum contact fails to elicit transients.\\

\noindent Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) reveals that aggressive stress produces distinct macroscopic defects at the electrical contacts, matching observations in electroformed devices \cite{waser1990dcI,waser1990dcII}. Excessive negative stress creates clustered holes 1 to 5 $\mu m$ in diameter that span regions over 20 $\mu m$. These non-mechanical clusters are much larger than the typical 1 $\mu m$ conductive filaments \cite{buckwell2015conductance}. In contrast, excessive positive stress uniformly roughens the contact surface. Crucially, these macroscopic defects indicate overstress; they are not required to produce current transients and were intentionally avoided in this study.

\section[Neuromorphic Plasticity in the Subthreshold Regime]{Neuromorphic Plasticity in the Subthreshold Regime}

Defining current transients is difficult due to their elusive nature, frequent misidentification as faults, and low occurrence rate in device batches. This scarcity stems from the challenge of fabricating devices that reliably exhibit the phenomenon. Detailed characterization necessitates gradually inducing the effect to correlate it with specific changes in device attributes. Existing analysis remains limited, as published research relies on the sporadic appearance of transients. Consequently, establishing a reliable method to induce transients is essential for a comprehensive understanding of this phenomenon.

\subsection[Unipolar Bidirectional Plasticity]{Unipolar Bidirectional Plasticity}

Current transients enable analog potentiation and depression under the same voltage polarity and amplitude. In this physical context, potentiation corresponds to an increase in device conductance (a rise in current), while depression corresponds to a decrease in conductance (a fall in current). These changes can be long-term (LTP/LTD) or short-term (STP/STD). While memristors and ReRAM devices often replicate these behaviors, they typically require opposite polarities for potentiation and depression \cite{jo2010nanoscale}. This requirement complicates neuron circuits. Single-rail operation, common in digital electronics, is preferable. Although some devices, such as complementary \cite{khan2020comparison} or unipolar ReRAM \cite{mehonic2012electrically}, operate with a single polarity, they often exhibit binary rather than analog switching.\\

\noindent Applying a negative step potential to the top gold electrode demonstrates the device's potentiation and depression (Figure \ref{fig:4e}). A rapid potentiation (current increase) occurs within seconds, followed by a slower depression phase (current decrease) that eventually reduces the conductance below the initial level over tens of minutes.\\

\subsection[Dynamic Synaptic Tuning]{Dynamic Synaptic Tuning}

Selecting between potentiation and depression is critical for neuromorphic circuit design. Regulating pulse magnitude offers an adaptable control mechanism. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:4e}, lower voltages (e.g., -1 V) induce potentiation but not depression, consistent with low-voltage spike trains.\\

\noindent Higher stressing currents induce significant potentiation and increased conductance. In contrast, the device stressed with the minimum current (-0.1 $\mu$A) exhibits only depression. Conversely, the device exposed to the maximum current (-100 $\mu$A) potentiates to nearly ten times its initial conductance. These methods allow modification of the device's potentiation level. Crucially, this adjustment is irreversible; initial stressing induces permanent structural modifications and defect distributions within the oxide, preventing a return to the pristine state. Consequently, baseline potentiation characteristics are fixed during fabrication. In contrast, altering spike amplitude offers a dynamic, run-time control mechanism. Since the device exhibits both conductance increase and decrease with varying rates and relaxation times, steady-state conductance inevitably varies with spike train period. Confirming this behavior is a prerequisite to investigating its computational potential.\\

\subsection[Implementation of Homeostatic Mechanisms]{Implementation of Homeostatic Mechanisms}

\noindent This study illustrates the potential of distinctive memristive behaviors in innovative computing architectures. A key application is the adjustment of synaptic weight based on firing frequency, mimicking biological homeostasis \cite{tien2018homeostatic}. In biology, homeostasis maintains optimal operating conditions, such as body temperature regulation \cite{turrigiano1999homeostatic}. In spiking neural networks, homeostasis regulates spiking activity, reducing power consumption from unnecessary events and preventing faulty neurons from inducing chaotic, high-activity states that could destabilize the network \cite{marder2006variability}. \\

\noindent Habituation, a form of homeostasis where output temporarily changes in response to input variations before settling to a steady state, has practical applications. For instance, incorporating habituation in recurrent neural networks improved MNIST classification accuracy by 20\% compared to networks without homeostasis \cite{wang2018fully}.\\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
\centering    
\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Chapter4/Figs/l.png}
\caption[Response of the device to different frequency of spike pulses]{Response of the device to varying frequencies of spike pulses. The device was initially operated with a sequence of 1Hz spike pulses, followed by a period of 10Hz spike pulses, before finally returning to 1Hz spike pulses.}
\label{fig:4l}
\end{figure}

\noindent As demonstrated previously, the device's steady-state conductance depends on the incoming spike rate. Conductance decreases at higher spiking frequencies and settles at higher values for lower frequencies. This behavior effectively inhibits suboptimal input signals exhibiting chaotic, high-frequency activity. Figure \ref{fig:4l} illustrates this concept. While the memristive device acts as a synapse rather than a neuron, its response must be evaluated under realistic neural operating conditions. Therefore, a signal generator applies 1 Hz Gaussian pulses to emulate the typical low-frequency background firing rate of a resting pre-synaptic neuron, allowing the device to reach a baseline steady-state conductance. At t = 1.8 minutes, a dysfunctional, high-activity state (e.g., due to upstream circuit damage) is simulated by abruptly increasing the spike train frequency to 10 Hz. \\

\noindent Without homeostasis, this erroneous input could propagate through the network, driving subsequent neurons into similar states. However, the device's transient behavior reduces conductivity under high-frequency stress. This limits the current supplied to the output neuron, preventing the propagation of high-frequency activity. Temporary suppression protects the network but must be reversible upon recovery. At t = 2.8 minutes, the input returns to the 1 Hz baseline. The device conductance recovers to its initial steady-state value, demonstrating the reversibility of the homeostatic mechanism. \\

\noindent Reversibility is fundamental to homeostasis. While this device demonstrates homeostatic habituation, limitations exist; specifically, maintaining a constant conductance value during the transient state is challenging. Electroforming the device, like a standard memristor, would enable precise analog weight adjustment. Physical implementation of homeostasis may require connecting the transient device in series with a programmable one. Since both devices share identical architectures and manufacturing processes—differing only in electrical stressing methods—they can be integrated on a single wafer.

\subsection[Mechanistic Analysis of Conductance Modulation]{Mechanistic Analysis of Conductance Modulation}

\noindent Early theories attributed the transient to an ionic current superimposed on an electronic current \cite{many1962theory, lampert1970current}. This ionic current results from the field-driven drift of space charge, typically assumed to be charged oxygen vacancies \cite{saha2001transient}. However, conductance variations appear to stem from two simultaneous modulations. An ionic current could only explain this if the space charge drifted at a constant velocity indefinitely. A more robust explanation posits that the transient is an electronic current modulated by changes in device conductance \cite{meyer2005oxygen}. Electronic conductance in MIM devices can change via multiple simultaneous mechanisms. Literature generally favors electronic over ionic explanations \cite{wakiya2006activation, el2007ionic}. \\

\noindent An MIM device comprises three series components: a metal-insulator interface, a bulk insulator, and a second metal-insulator interface \cite{sze2021physics}. While these components collectively determine conductance, one often dominates due to significantly higher resistivity. Research has yet to identify which layer dominates conductance in devices exhibiting current transients. Although rectifying behavior implies interface-limited conductance, interface models do not necessarily capture the dynamics accurately. \\

\noindent Under reverse bias, where the applied voltage raises the energy barrier at the rectifying metal-oxide interface to restrict charge carrier injection, the interface exhibits high resistivity and dictates the overall device conductance, making an interface model most accurate. Conversely, under forward bias, where the applied voltage lowers this barrier and allows carriers to inject freely, the interface becomes highly conductive. In this forward-biased state, the bulk oxide dominates the total resistance, necessitating a bulk-based model.\\

\noindent Determining the physical origin of current transients requires investigating all device layers. The amorphous bulk oxide is highly insulating due to its large bandgap. At high voltages and low temperatures, Fowler-Nordheim tunnelling can dominate conduction, but direct tunnelling is highly unlikely for oxide layers thicker than 1 $\mu m$. Instead, electron trap states enable higher current densities via alternative bulk pathways like trap-assisted tunnelling or Poole-Frenkel hopping. Interface-limited mechanisms, such as Schottky emission, may also contribute depending on barrier height and temperature. \\

\noindent This thesis examines amorphous silicon oxide, which contains a higher concentration of oxygen vacancies than stoichiometric silicon dioxide. These vacancies act as electron/hole traps and affect electronic conduction. Amorphous silicon dioxide features wide $O-Si-O$ bond angles \cite{el2013identification} that trap up to two electrons, lowering the energy barrier for vacancy generation \cite{gao2016mechanism}. Conduction occurs via these localized vacancy sites, which do not necessarily act as permanent traps. TEM \cite{munde2017intrinsic, wu2015evolution} and CAFM \cite{buckwell2015conductance} studies reveal conductive bridges formed via trap states. At elevated temperatures, trapped electrons can move between trap states or into the conduction band via Poole-Frenkel conduction \cite{frenkel1938pre}.

\begin{align}
    \sigma_{PF} &= q \mu_n n_{PF} = q \mu_n n_0 \exp\left( \frac{-q (\Phi_B - \Delta\Phi_{PF}) }{k_B T} \right) \label{eq:4.14} \\
    j_{PF} &= E \sigma_{PF} = E q \mu_n n_0 \exp\left( \frac{-q (\Phi_B - \Delta\Phi_{PF}) }{k_B T} \right) \label{eq:4.15}
\end{align}

\noindent The Poole-Frenkel effect increases oxide conductance, $\sigma_{PF}$, by thermally exciting electrons from traps into the conduction band. The applied field lowers the barrier height, $\Phi_B$, increasing the free electron concentration from $n_0$ to $n_{PF}$. Equation (\ref{eq:2.15}) describes the barrier reduction $\Delta \Phi_{PF} = \sqrt{\frac{q^3 E}{\pi \epsilon}}$ \cite{sze2021physics}. Although electrons follow Fermi-Dirac statistics, the Boltzmann distribution approximates the non-degenerate limit, determining the number of extra electrons based on thermal energy $k_B T$. Device conductance under the Poole-Frenkel effect depends on trap carrier concentration, $n_0$, and barrier height reduction, which varies with the local electric field, $E$. Both factors are crucial for modeling.\\

\noindent Poole-Frenkel conduction is not the sole mechanism for trap-mediated conductivity \cite{dimaria1995mechanism}. Trap-assisted tunnelling (TAT) allows transfer between proximate traps \cite{jimenez2001physical} and describes conduction in resistive switching amorphous silicon dioxide \cite{mehonic2012resistive}. Inelastic trap-assisted tunnelling (ITAT) \cite{ielmini2000modelingII} involves electrons tunnelling into traps and relaxing via phonon emission, enabling hopping between spatially and energetically distributed traps. Both Poole-Frenkel and ITAT models can explain conductance changes during current transients. \\

\noindent Charge trapping likely causes the conductivity increase. For example, the standard Poole-Frenkel model (\ref{eq:4.15}) demonstrates that current is directly proportional to the populated trap concentration (denoted as the static term $n_0$) and the thermal excitation probability. By treating this trap population as a dynamic, time-dependent variable $n(t)$ rather than a static constant, the progressive filling of traps directly explains the observed transient current increase.

\begin{align}
    E(x) &= \frac{V}{d} \label{eq:4.16} \\
    E(x) &= \frac{V}{d} - \frac{Qx}{\epsilon d} \label{eq:4.17}\\
    E(x) &= \begin{cases}
\frac{V}{d} - \frac{Qx}{\epsilon \delta} & \text{ if } x< \delta \\ 
\frac{V}{d} - \frac{Q}{\epsilon} & \text{ if } x\geq \delta 
\end{cases} \label{eq:4.18}
\end{align}

\noindent The trap population term can be time-dependent. Initially, traps in a grounded device at room temperature are likely depleted, resulting in low conductance upon voltage application. Under bias, trap filling probability, $n_0$, increases. As conductance and current density increase, electron trapping rises, leading to a higher steady-state $n$. The ITAT model similarly accommodates transient effects via time-varying trap population probabilities.\\

\noindent A conductance increase requires the rate of electron trapping to exceed the detrapping rate. Conductance decay may result from an indirect interaction between the local electric field and drifting mobile defects, such as charged oxygen vacancies. Since the Poole-Frenkel mechanism depends exponentially on the electric field, a field reduction would significantly decrease current.\\

\noindent Mobile ions can reduce the electric fields at trap sites. In an ideal insulator without trapped charge, the electric field is constant (\ref{eq:4.16}). Introducing a homogeneous space charge $Q$ creates a field gradient (\ref{eq:4.17}), reducing the field experienced by traps. If charge is distributed across the oxide, the potential drop is shared, minimizing the effect on individual traps. However, if mobile charge accumulates at an interface, the field reduction concentrates there. Consequently, traps in this region experience a lower electric field, reducing overall device current.\\

\noindent Interface-based models, often used to explain resistance switching in memristors, offer an alternative explanation. Rectifying behavior suggests a Schottky-like barrier at the metal-oxide interface. Defective oxide behaving as an n-type \cite{fujii2005hysteretic} or p-type \cite{sawa2004hysteretic} semiconductor creates this barrier. Work function offsets drive charge transfer between the metal and oxide defects, forming a space charge region and electronic barrier. Conductance changes result from modulations in barrier height (affecting thermionic current) or width (affecting tunnelling current).\\

\noindent Interface states \cite{seong2007hpha}, or surface states \cite{bardeen1947surface}, influence interface space charge. Depending on their type (n-type or p-type), these states can modulate barrier height or width \cite{cowley1965surface}. High interface state concentrations can pin the Fermi level, rendering work functions irrelevant. Charge trapping in these states could modulate the barrier. For example, surface states in metal-amorphous silicon Schottky barriers have been shown to decrease barrier height \cite{wronski1977surface}.\\

\noindent Alternatively, oxygen vacancy migration towards the interface may alter barrier height by modifying band alignments \cite{asanuma2009relationship}. Elevated vacancy levels have been linked to increased bulk bandgaps. Several mechanisms could explain current transients. In bulk-limited devices, mobile space charge reducing local electric fields could explain conductance decay, applicable to both Poole-Frenkel and trap-assisted tunnelling models. Charge-driven conductance increases could result from trap population. In interface-limited devices, charge trapping in surface states could reduce barrier height via Fermi-pinning, increasing conductance. Conversely, defect drift towards the contact could modulate band alignments, causing decay. The true mechanism likely involves a combination of bulk and interface effects.

\section[Summary]{Summary}

\noindent This chapter establishes the subthreshold regime as a distinct operational mode for silicon oxide memristors, capable of concurrent potentiation and depression under unipolar voltage pulses. Unlike the binary switching mechanisms detailed in previous chapter, these transient dynamics bridge the gap between pristine and electroformed states. The methodology developed here for inducing and tuning these behaviors provides the experimental basis for the physical modeling framework presented in next chapter.\\

\noindent These characteristics are foundational to the biorealistic computing strategies explored in other chapters. By intrinsically emulating synaptic homeostasis, habituation, and short-term memory, the device simplifies the hardware requirements for Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs). The volatile conductance changes naturally implement forgetting processes \cite{li2020enhanced} and Spike-Rate-Dependent Plasticity (SRDP) \cite{huang2019binary}, reducing circuit complexity by eliminating the need for inhibitory pulses and aligning hardware behavior with biological learning rules.\\

\noindent While the slow dynamics of these transients limit high-frequency operation, they are well-suited for biological timescales and specific monitoring applications \cite{vieluf2022seizure}. Future optimization requires resolving the interplay between interface-limited charge trapping and bulk defect migration. Ultimately, this work validates the subthreshold regime as a robust tool for single-rail neuromorphic circuits, leveraging inherent material physics to achieve efficient, brain-inspired computation.
