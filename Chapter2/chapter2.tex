\section[Biological Neural Computation]{Biological Neural Computation}

\noindent Computational neuroscience uses computational methods to elucidate brain function mechanisms. This involves identifying neural computations and understanding the interactions between neurons and synapses that facilitate them. The brain performs vast computations using neurons and synapses as fundamental units. A synapse facilitates the transfer of electrical or chemical signals from a presynaptic to a postsynaptic neuron. \\

\noindent This section overviews relevant biological details and computational neuroscience concepts employed in this study. These foundations inform the accurate modeling of silicon oxide-based neuromorphic hardware and bio-inspired computing. This section presents fundamental biological details at both neural and network levels. The models outlined here are simplified compared to the extensive biological evidence \cite{kandel2000principles}. Consequently, only biological facts essential to this work are presented.


\subsection[Neuronal Physiology and Signaling]{Neuronal Physiology and Signaling}

\noindent A neuron is a specialized cell that processes and transmits information via electrical and chemical signals \cite{mel1994information}. While one of many brain cell types, neurons are the primary computational entities. Functionally, neurons receive input from other neurons; sufficient stimulation triggers an action potential, or voltage spike, which propagates to connected neurons. Figure \ref{fig:2a} illustrates the basic neuron structure, comprising dendrites, the soma (cell body), and the axon. Dendrites receive input voltage and transmit it to the soma. A spiking neuron sends voltage down its axon, triggering neurotransmitter release at synapses—connections between the axon and the dendrites of other neurons. This release induces dendritic input voltage in connected neurons. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Chapter2/Figs/a.png}
    \caption[Labeled diagram of the neuron.]{Labeled diagram of the neuron. The gap between dendrites and axons are synapses that allow it to accept input from other neurons. The dendrites carry current to the soma, where electrical charge is integrated. If the neuron membrane gets sufficiently polarised, an action potential travels down the axon. This causes neurotransmitters to be released at synapses, resulting in voltage in the dendrites of postsynaptic neurons.}
    \label{fig:2a}
\end{figure}

\noindent Computationally, the soma integrates incoming dendritic voltages \cite{polsky2004computational}, initiating action potential generation (Figure \ref{fig:2b}). At rest, the soma maintains a negative resting voltage via ion pumps that regulate intracellular ion concentrations, predominantly sodium ($Na^+$), potassium ($K^+$), and calcium ($Ca^{+2}$). \\

\noindent Incoming dendritic currents depolarize the cell \cite{johnston1996active}. When somatic voltage reaches a threshold, voltage-activated sodium channels open, allowing sodium influx and further depolarization. This continues until the electrical gradient from accumulated sodium ions equilibrates with the chemical gradient, significantly increasing the neuron's positive charge beyond the resting voltage. \\

\noindent Substantial depolarization activates voltage-gated potassium channels, releasing potassium ions and facilitating repolarization. Concurrently, sodium channels inactivate. Potassium channel opening drives the cell voltage below its resting level, a state termed hyperpolarization. Sodium channels remain inactivated and potassium channels open for a duration following the spike. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Chapter2/Figs/b.png}
    \caption[Spiking dynamics of a neuron.]{The action potential and the underlying conductance and currents with respect to time \cite{squire2012fundamental}. Increased conductance for $Na^+$ is associated with the rising phase of the action potential, whereas the slower increase in conductance for $K^+$ is associated with repolarisation and afterhyperpolarisation. The reduction in $I_{Na}$ before the peak of the action potential is due to inactivation of the Na+ channels.}
    \label{fig:2b}
\end{figure}

\noindent These factors create an absolute refractory period during which the neuron cannot fire. While ionic concentration changes during a single spike are minor, ion pumps maintain optimal sodium and potassium concentrations over numerous spikes. Other currents, notably calcium currents, exist in some neurons. \\

\noindent Action potential-induced rapid depolarization increases somatic voltage and partially depolarizes proximal axon segments. This opens sodium channels in the axon, causing further depolarization and propagating the effect to subsequent sections. Thus, the somatic spike triggers a voltage wave traveling down the axon, eventually releasing neurotransmitters from synaptic vesicles at the axon terminals. \\

\noindent Dale's principle posits that all synapses along a neuron's axon release the same neurotransmitter or combination of neurotransmitters. Originally assuming a single neurotransmitter type per neuron, subsequent evidence revealed cotransmission \cite{burnstock2004cotransmission}. Neurotransmitters exert either excitatory or inhibitory effects on postsynaptic cells.

\subsection[Computational Neuron Models]{Computational Neuron Models}

\noindent Available neuron models have proliferated, ranging from simple binary threshold units \cite{stocks2001information} to complex multi-compartmental models accounting for dendritic morphology \cite{markram2015reconstruction}. For large-scale neural models reproducing high-level behaviors, single-compartment neuron models remain prevalent. Single-compartment models treat the neuron as a unified electrical entity combining dendrites, soma, and axon. Conversely, multi-compartmental models represent the neuron as interacting electrical compartments. Modeling the spike separately from other neural dynamics separates time scales, conserving computational resources by avoiding detailed spike trajectory modeling \cite{abbott1999lapicque}. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Chapter2/Figs/c.png}
    \caption[Hodgkin-Huxley neuron model.]{(a) An equivalent circuit for the Hodgkin-Huxley (HH) models \cite{hodgkin1952quantitative}. (b) An action potential waveform, which demonstrates the resting, threshold, and reset potentials \cite{chua2012hodgkin}.}
    \label{fig:2c}
\end{figure}

\noindent Many influential computational neuroscience findings rely on detailed neuronal models. The Hodgkin-Huxley (HH) model of the squid giant axon \cite{hodgkin1952quantitative} is prominent, employing nonlinear differential equations to accurately approximate neuronal electrical signals \cite{chua2012hodgkin}. Figure \ref{fig:2c}(a) depicts the HH model, where time-varying nonlinear resistors $R_{Na}(G_{Na})$ and $R_K(G_K)$ represent sodium and potassium channels, respectively. A linear resistor $R_L(G_L)$ simulates leak channels, and $C$ models membrane capacitance. The HH model equations are: 
\begin{align}
    C \frac{dV_m(t)}{dt} = I_C(t) + \sum_{k}^{}I_k(t) \label{eq:2.1} 
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $V_m$ represents membrane potential, and $\sum_{k}^{}I_k(t)$ denotes the sum of ionic currents flowing into the neuron, formulated as three currents:
\begin{align}
    \sum_{k}^{}I_k &= C_m \frac{dV_m}{dt} + G_Kn^4(V_m - V_K) + G_{Na}m^3(V_m - V_{Na}) + G_L (V_m - V_L) \label{eq:2.2} \\
    \frac{dn}{dt} &= \alpha_n(V_m)(1-n)-\beta_n(V_m)n \label{eq:2.3} \\
    \frac{dm}{dt} &= \alpha_m(V_m)(1-m) - \beta_m(V_m)m \label{eq:2.4} \\
    \frac{dh}{dt} &= \alpha_h(V_m)(1-h)-\beta_h(V_m)h \label{eq:2.5}
\end{align}

\noindent $V_K$, $V_{Na}$, and $V_L$ are reversal potentials. Rate constants $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$, dependent on membrane potential, describe the $i^{th}$ ion channel behavior. $G_K$, $G_{Na}$, and $G_L$ represent maximal conductance values. Dimensionless quantities $n$, $m$, and $h$ (between 0 and 1) associate with the ion channels. To optimize the fit for human action potentials, the HH model reduces leakage channel conductance to $G_L = 0$ \cite{noble1962modification}. $G_{Na}$ and $G_K$ have been demonstrated to be memristors \cite{chua1976memristive}, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:2c}(b). \\

\noindent The integrate-and-fire (IF) neuron \cite{lapicque1907louis}, one of the earliest computational models, predates the measurement of neuronal electrical and chemical changes. It models the neuron membrane as a capacitor storing charge over time \cite{abbott1999lapicque}. The IF model integrates current over time and fires when voltage reaches a threshold. Incorporating a leak term (a resistor in parallel with the capacitor) allows charge dissipation, designating the model as leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF). \\

\noindent To determine membrane voltage evolution and spike timing, capacitor charge $Q$ is defined as $Q = V \times C$. Differentiating with respect to time yields the neuron's membrane voltage $V(t)$:
\begin{align}
    C \frac{dV(t)}{dt} = J(t) \label{eq:2.6} 
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $J(t)$ represents input current, and $C$ denotes membrane capacitance. Equation (\ref{eq:2.6}) shows the IF neuron integrating input current. Spike timing must then be identified. A threshold voltage, $V_{th}$, defines the firing point. Exceeding this threshold triggers a spike, reflecting the neurophysiological principle that spike initiation is irreversible once the threshold is crossed. After firing, the membrane voltage resets to the resting potential, $V_{rest}$. Physiologically, post-spike ionic currents (typically potassium) restore the membrane voltage.\\

\noindent The leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) model \cite{knight1972dynamics} accounts for membrane leakage. Imperfect capacitance causes current leakage, pulling voltage back to resting potential. Modeling the membrane as a parallel capacitor and resistor enables "forgetting": without input, voltage returns to rest \cite{koch2004biophysics}. LIF dynamics are described by:

\begin{align}
C \frac{dV(t)}{dt} = J(t) - \frac{1}{R} (V - V_{rest}) \label{eq:2.7} 
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $R$ represents membrane resistance; other parameters and the resetting procedure remain consistent with the IF model. The LIF model includes parameters $C, R, V_{rest}$, and $V_{th}$. Normalizing the model reduces parameter count while preserving dynamics. Specifically, voltage can be normalized to the range [0, 1], with zero resting potential and unit firing threshold. Multiplying Equation (\ref{eq:2.7}) by $R$ yields:

\begin{align}
    \tau_{rc} \frac{dV}{dt} &= RJ(t) - V + V_{rest} \label{eq:2.8} \\
    \tau_{rc} &= R \times C \label{eq:2.9} \\
    \bar{V} &= \frac{V - V_{rest}}{V_{th} - V_{rest}} \label{eq:2.10} \\
    \bar{V_{rest}} &= \frac{V_{rest}}{V_{th}} \label{eq:2.11} 
\end{align}

\noindent Substituting $\bar{V}$ and $\bar{V_{rest}}$ into Equation (\ref{eq:2.3}) gives:

\begin{align}
    \tau_{RC}(V_{th} - V_{rest})\frac{d\bar{V}}{dt} &= RJ(t) - \bar{V}(V_{th} - V_{rest})
    \label{eq:2.12} \\
    \tau_{rc}\frac{d\bar{V}}{dt} &= \frac{R}{V_{th} - V_{rest}} J(t) - \bar{V} \label{eq:2.13} \\
    \tau_{rc} \frac{d\bar{V}}{dt} &= \bar{J}(t) - \bar{V} \label{eq:2.14} 
\end{align}

\noindent With $\bar{V_{th}} = 1$, $\bar{V_{rest}} = 0$, and $\bar{J}(t) = \frac{R}{V_{th} - V_{rest}} J(t)$, $\bar{J}(t)$ is a linear transformation of $J(t)$. Consequently, Equation (\ref{eq:2.14}) retains the full dynamics of Equation (\ref{eq:2.7}) for a scaled input, using only parameter $\tau_{RC}$. $\bar{V}$ and $\bar{J}$ are unitless. Conventionally, unitless space is used, referring to these quantities as $V$ and $J$ for mathematical simplicity without loss of generality. \\

\noindent Equation (\ref{eq:2.14}) describes spike conditions for input current $J(t)$. Often, considering only the spike rate—spikes per second for a given input—suffices. For the LIF model, the analytical firing rate for constant input current is determined by calculating the inter-spike interval (ISI). The firing rate is the inverse of the ISI. For constant input $J(t) = j$, Equation (\ref{eq:2.14}) yields the voltage over time: 
\begin{align}
    V(t) = (V(0) - j)e^{\frac{-t}{\tau_{rc}}} + j \label{eq:2.15}
\end{align}

\noindent Without spikes, the time for voltage to rise from $V(0) = 0$ to $V(t) = 1$ is calculated (requiring $j > 1$). Substituting into Equation (\ref{eq:2.15}) and solving for $t$ yields:
\begin{align}
    t = - \tau_{RC} log \left( - \frac{1}{j} \right) \label{eq:2.16}
\end{align}

\noindent Incorporating the refractory period and inverting yields the LIF neuron spike rate $r$:
\begin{align}
    r &= \begin{cases}
    \frac{1}{t_{ref} - \tau_{RC} log \left( 1 - \frac{1}{j} \right)} & \text{ if } j > 1 \\ 
    0 & \text{ otherwise }  
    \end{cases} \label{eq:2.17}
\end{align}


\noindent The LIF model is a widely used simplified neuron model \cite{lapique1907researches}, illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:2d}(a). Here, a resistor $R$ in series with a DC source $V_{rest}/V_{reset}$ connects in parallel with a capacitor $C$ \cite{stein1967frequency}. A postsynaptic neuron receives synaptic current $I(t)$ from presynaptic spikes. Current $I(t)$ flowing into $C$ increases membrane potential $V(t)$, while charge leaks via resistor $R$. When $V(t)$ reaches a threshold, the neuron spikes, and the potential resets. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Chapter2/Figs/d.png}
    \caption[The Leaky Integrate-and-Fire neuron model.]{(a) Schematic diagram of the LIF electrical model. The LIF model allows for the control of spiking behaviour through a comparison of membrane potential and threshold at each time step. Upon the triggering of a spike, a voltage-controlled switch discharges C for a duration corresponding to the refractory period $t_0$ \cite{tal1997computing}. (b) Input current in the form of an alpha function \cite{teeter2018generalized}.}
    \label{fig:2d}
\end{figure}

\noindent Without $I(t)$, voltage across $C$ settles at $V_{rest}$. During the refractory period $t_0$, spiking is disabled. Figure \ref{fig:2d}(c,d) illustrates LIF dynamics for DC input with zero rest and reset potentials ($E_{reset} = E_{rest} = 0$) \cite{tal1997computing}. Input synaptic current $I(t)$ can be described by a time-varying alpha function, or alternatives like instantaneous rise with exponential decay, biexponential functions, sawtooth, or pulse functions. The alpha synaptic current is modeled by Equation (\ref{eq:2.15}) (Figure \ref{fig:2d}(b)). Neurons exhibit considerable heterogeneity in dynamics, morphology, and connectivity. They are categorized as: excitatory neurons, which promote activity; inhibitory neurons, which suppress activity to maintain stability and rhythm; and interneurons, which connect local circuits to enable complex computations.

\subsection[Synaptic Plasticity and Learning Rules]{Synaptic Plasticity and Learning Rules}

Modeling synaptic dynamics is essential. Synapses function as low-pass filters; a presynaptic spike elicits an extended postsynaptic current pulse, effectively a filtered version of the spike. The simplest synapse model is a first-order low-pass filter. The impulse response describes the filter's reaction to an impulse (an idealized spike), representing the postsynaptic current response. The first-order low-pass filter impulse response is:
\begin{align}
h(t) = \frac{1}{\tau_s}e^{\frac{t}{\tau_s}} \label{eq:2.18} 
\end{align}

\noindent The synaptic time constant $\tau_s$ defines the duration of the postsynaptic current spread. As the impulse response is exponential, this is termed the exponential synapse model.
\begin{align}
h(t) = \frac{1}{\tau_s^2}e^{\frac{t}{\tau_s}} \label{eq:2.19} 
\end{align}

\noindent A second-order low-pass filter offers a superior synapse model \cite{mainen1995reliability}. Its impulse response, defined by Equation (\ref{eq:2.19}), is the alpha function, designating it the alpha synapse model. Both are current-based synapse models, describing current generated by a spike.\\

\noindent A primary objective of computational neuroscience is to determine how the brain encodes information. Researchers have proposed various coding schemes, with a key distinction between rate coding and temporal coding. Rate coding considers only the firing rate over a period. For example, peripheral motor neurons use rate coding to control muscle contraction intensity \cite{gerstner1997neural}. Temporal coding considers individual spike timing. In the early auditory system, precise timing facilitates sound localization \cite{chase2006spike}.\\

\noindent Definitions of rate and temporal codes remain contentious \cite{dayan2005theoretical}. For instance, a neuron might fire rapidly then rest, while another fires the same number of spikes evenly over the same period. Rate and temporal codes describe individual neuron encoding. Population coding involves distributed representation across a neuron group, where the value cannot be decoded from a limited subset.\\

\noindent Extrapolating coding to multiple neurons could involve redundancy, where numerous neurons with similar tuning properties exhibit similar firing patterns for a given value. Conversely, population coding entails each neuron representing a distinct aspect of the value \cite{georgopoulos1982relations}. For example, in representing head direction, different neurons encode specific orientations (left, right, centered, intermediate). The direction eliciting maximal activity is the neuron's preferred direction. \\

\noindent Synapses play a dual role: facilitating communication and serving as the locus of learning and memory. Synaptic strength \cite{bastos2022motor} determines the connection magnitude. Synaptic plasticity refers to the ability to modify these strengths. Plasticity is categorized into types: Short-Term Plasticity (STP) involves transient changes lasting milliseconds to seconds. Long-Term Potentiation (LTP) and Long-Term Depression (LTD) involve sustained increases or decreases in synaptic strength. \\

\noindent Hebbian learning, summarized as "neurons that fire together wire together," is a significant plasticity model. Spike-Timing Dependent Plasticity (STDP) \cite{zheng2018learning} offers a more precise, temporally sensitive rule. Mathematical models capture spike timing effects on weight updates. If a presynaptic neuron fires shortly before a postsynaptic neuron, the synapse strengthens; the reverse order weakens it. A common representation is:
\begin{align}
\Delta w = \left\{ \begin{array}{cl}
    A_+ \cdot e^{-\Delta t/\tau_+}, & \ \Delta t > 0 \\
    -A_- \cdot e^{-\Delta t/\tau_-}, & \ \Delta t < 0
    \end{array} \right. \label{eq:2.20}
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $\Delta t = t_{post} - t_{pre}$ is the timing difference, $A_+, A_-$ are learning rates, and $\tau_+, \tau_-$ are time constants. This asymmetric window reflects experimental observations, providing a biologically plausible basis for hardware synaptic learning.\\

\noindent Memristive devices offer an electronic synapse analogue via tunable conductance and history-dependent memory. In crossbar arrays, they implement synaptic weight matrices directly in hardware, with updates governed by local pulses. Memristive STDP implementations exploit device physics, making conductance change contingent on pulse overlap:
\begin{align}
    \Delta G = f(V_{pre}, V_{post}, \Delta t) \label{eq:2.21}
\end{align}
\noindent where $f$ is a device-specific function determined by material properties and pulse shapes. \\

\noindent Memristors inherently facilitate the nonlinear, history-dependent behavior characteristic of biological plasticity rules like STDP. Applying timed voltage pulses to a memristor modulates conductance, mimicking LTP and LTD.

\section[Memristive Neuromorphic Hardware]{Memristive Neuromorphic Hardware}

Extensive research in device physics and materials science explores innovative memory materials and retention techniques \cite{indiveri2021introducing}. The term "neuromorphic" describes technologies and systems that exhibit behaviors comparable to biological synapses, essential for massive AI networks \cite{di2009circuit}. The concept of using nanoscale "memristors" to create synapses in deep neural networks and sustain weights locally gained popularity \cite{jo2010nanoscale}. \\

\noindent This hardware enables "in-memory computing" and advanced non-linear properties, mimicking biological synapse physics \cite{saighi2015plasticity}. Research aims to develop volatile and non-volatile memristive electronics and spike-based control systems to elicit biologically realistic learning in crossbar arrays. Identifying the ideal artificial synapse requires investigating diverse materials, tools, and techniques.

\subsection[Physics of Memristive Devices]{Physics of Memristive Devices}

\noindent Memristive devices, or memory resistors, are foundational to neuromorphic computing, retaining resistive states based on electrical history to emulate biological synapses. These components facilitate unified memory storage and computation, enabling the co-location vital for brain-inspired architectures. Traditional passive circuit components (resistor, capacitor, inductor) inadequately describe characteristics connected by circuit theory symmetry. Leon Chua addressed this by introducing the memristor in 1971 \cite{chua1971memristor}, coupling flux linkage and charge:
\begin{align}
    M(q) = \frac{d\phi}{dq} \label{eq:2.22}
\end{align}

\noindent where $M$ denotes memristance, a value dependent on the device's current history. This creates resistive memory, retaining the previous state. Proof of resistive switching in the memristor model was established in 2008 \cite{strukov2008missing}.

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Chapter2/Figs/e.png}
    \caption[Conceptual symmetries of resistor, capacitor, inductor, and memristor]{Conceptual symmetries of resistor, capacitor, inductor, and memristor \cite{du2017metal}. Four fundamental variables in circuit theory are depicted with their relationships. Each variable can be related to another via either a passive component or a well-known equation.}
    \label{fig:2e}
\end{figure}

\noindent Basic circuit theory involves three fundamental elements and four variables. One component is absent for symmetry: a device interconnecting charge and magnetic flux (Figure \ref{fig:2e}). The link between the mathematical memristive model and a two-terminal resistive switching device is pivotal. Memristance differs from resistance by being charge-dependent rather than constant. Current, defined as charge flow per unit time, is expressed as:
\begin{align}
    q = \int_{-\infty}^{t_0} i(t)dt \label{eq:2.23}
\end{align}

\noindent where charge is the integral of current up to time $t_0$. Thus, memristance is determined by historical currents. If current is interrupted, the memory state persists until flow restores, characterizing the device as a "memristor."Physically, memristors are modeled as two-terminal devices where resistance varies due to ion or vacancy drift in a dielectric. State-dependent resistance is:
\begin{align}
    V(t) = R(w(t)) \cdot I(t) \label{eq:2.24}
\end{align}

\noindent where $V(t)$ and $I(t)$ are voltage and current, and $R(w(t))$ is resistance dependent on internal state $w(t)$. $w(t)$ often represents physical quantities like oxygen vacancy concentration or filament length. The state evolution is defined by $f(w, I) = \frac{dw}{dt}$. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Chapter2/Figs/f.png}
    \caption[Typical I-V characteristic of a memristor]{The typical I-V characteristic of a memristor displays a pinched hysteresis loop resulting from the nonlinear relationship between current and voltage in memristance \cite{wen2012dynamics}.}
    \label{fig:2f}
\end{figure}
    
\noindent Visually, the frequency-dependent pinched hysteresis loop distinguishes memristors \cite{chua2019resistance} (Figure \ref{fig:2f}). As voltage input frequency increases, the loop shrinks; at infinite frequency, the memristor approximates a resistor.\\

\noindent This study focuses on memristor devices among non-volatile memories, including MRAM, PRAM, FeRAM, and RRAM \cite{wang2017memristors}. These devices are characterized by resistive switching—a reversible, non-volatile resistance change driven by electrical signaling and material properties \cite{mehonic2018silicon}. RRAM utilizes reversible resistance switching, cycling between discrete states via repeated stimuli \cite{liu2010controllable}. An RRAM cell comprises an insulating thin film (typically metal oxide) sandwiched between electrodes, where switching occurs. "Memristance" describes RRAM behavior: resistance depends on total charge flow or the time-integral of applied voltage, allowing precise state switching. However, the ideal model often fails to capture real RRAM complexities.

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Chapter2/Figs/g.png}
    \caption[The main RRAM types.]{The main RRAM types, from left to right: electrochemical metallization memory (ECM), valence change memory (VCM), and thermochemical memory (TCM). \cite{goux2016electrochemical}}
    \label{fig:2g}
\end{figure} 

\noindent Despite sharing a metal-insulator-metal (MIM) architecture, RRAM categorization is challenging. Devices are generally classified by mechanism into oxide-RAM (OxRAM) and conductive bridge RAM (CBRAM) \cite{liu2015categorization}. Internal physical behavior varies, complicating a unified view. RRAM cells are classified by switching mechanism: electrochemical metallization (ECM), valence change memory (VCM), or thermochemical memory (TCM) \cite{goux2016electrochemical} (Figure \ref{fig:2g}). \\

\noindent Memristors operate between a high resistance "OFF" state (HRS) and a low resistance "ON" state (LRS). Transitioning from HRS to LRS is "Set"; the reverse is "Reset." An electroforming step typically converts the pristine, high-resistance device to a switchable state. Biorealistic learning requires continuous synaptic strength adjustment. Analog (gradual) switching in memristors is essential, offering incremental conductance changes under controlled pulses, unlike binary transitions. Consider a simplified state evolution model:
\begin{align}
    \frac{dw}{dt} = \mu \cdot I(t) \label{eq:2.25}
\end{align}
where $\mu$ is a material-dependent mobility parameter. Controlled voltage pulses allow precise conductance modulation:
\begin{align}
    \Delta G \varpropto \int I(t) dt = Q \label{eq:2.26}
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $Q$ is the accumulated charge. Shaping input pulses encodes plasticity rules like STDP directly in hardware.\\

\noindent Despite promise, memristive devices exhibit non-idealities \cite{govoreanu2013vacancy}. Variability in conductance range, switching thresholds, and cycle-to-cycle behavior occurs even among nominally identical devices. Conductance updates often exhibit non-linear saturation or asymmetry. Drift and retention loss over time may result from relaxation effects. \\

\noindent Neuromorphic systems employ redundancy, error-tolerant algorithms, or closed-loop calibration to address these issues. Notably, variability may be biologically realistic; synaptic stochasticity can enhance generalization and robustness. Compact models, ranging from physics-based to empirical, are essential for design and testing. The linear ion drift model \cite{cai2011abel} is a popular framework for early $TiO_2$ devices:
\begin{align}
    w(t) = w_0 + \frac{\mu_vR_{ON}}{D} \cdot \int_{0}^{t} I(\tau)   \,d\tau  \label{eq:2.27}
\end{align}

\noindent where $\mu_v$ is ion mobility, $R_{ON}$ is the low resistance state, and $D$ is device thickness. Window functions prevent unrealistic $w(t)$ values in simulations. A modified form is:
\begin{align}
    \frac{dw}{dt} = \mu \cdot I(t) \cdot f(w) \label{eq:2.28}
\end{align}

\noindent where $f(w)$ is a window function such as:
\begin{align}
    f(w) = 1 - (2w - 1)^{2p} \label{eq:2.29}
\end{align}
\noindent with $p$ controlling boundary non-linearity. These models enable software prototyping of algorithms and circuits, facilitating design-space exploration and validation before hardware realization.

\subsection[In-Memory Computing with Crossbars]{In-Memory Computing with Crossbars}

\noindent Neuromorphic computing represents a paradigm shift inspired by biological neural systems. Unlike conventional systems that compartmentalize memory and processing, causing inefficiencies, neuromorphic systems co-locate them using distributed, parallel architectures mimicking brain functionality. Neuromorphic engineering originated with Carver Mead in the 1980s \cite{mead1989analog}, who proposed analog electronics to mimic neurons and synapses. The field now encompasses both analog and digital brain-inspired circuits.\\

\noindent Neuromorphic computing translates neurobiological principles into hardware. Event-driven processing, where circuits activate only when necessary, significantly reduces power consumption. For example, synapses (implemented by resistive memory) function as both computational and memory units. Neuromorphic systems exhibit plasticity and real-time local learning using biologically plausible rules like STDP. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Chapter2/Figs/h.png}
    \caption[The memristor-based crossbar architecture.]{The memristor-based crossbar architecture with a single memristor array and a constant-term circuit \cite{truong2014new}. Resistive components are located at word and bit line connections. When voltages $\mathbf{V}$ are applied to the word line, the resistive element at the junction of the $i^{th}$ word line and the $j^{th}$ bit line generates $V_i \times G_{i,j}$ units of current, assuming zero wire resistance, as per Ohm's law. Currents created by each element are aggregated along the bit lines using Kirchhoff's current law.}
    \label{fig:2h}
\end{figure}


\noindent Neuromorphic architectures fall into two categories \cite{ceolini2020hand}. Digital systems (e.g., IBM TrueNorth, Intel Loihi) use digital circuits to simulate spiking neurons with programmable connectivity. Analog/Mixed-Signal systems, including memristive arrays, more closely mimic continuous biological dynamics, offering a substrate for analog computation. The crossbar array, with intersecting metal lines and memristive devices, is a key unit supporting efficient matrix-vector multiplication. For input voltage vector $V$ and conductance matrix $G$, the output current vector $I$ is:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{I} = \mathbf{G} \cdot \mathbf{V} \label{eq:2.30}
\end{align}

\noindent This single-step analog computation eliminates data movement, offering energy efficiency. Fundamental circuit laws perform the multiplication and addition required for linear algebra. Per Ohm's law, current $I$ through a conductance $G$ with voltage $V$ is $V \times G$, making $G$ a multiplicative factor. Per Kirchhoff's current law, currents $I_i$ from multiple branches sum at an intersection ($I = \sum_{i}^{} I_i$). \\

\noindent Resistive crossbar arrays perform vector-matrix products. Output currents represent the product of a voltage vector and conductance matrix. For $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}^\intercal \mathbf{W}$, mapping $\mathbf{x}$ to voltages $\mathbf{V}$ and $\mathbf{W}$ to conductances $\mathbf{G}$ generates currents $\mathbf{I}$ proportional to $\mathbf{y}$. Crossbar structure enables vector-matrix multiplication. Voltages $\mathbf{V}$ applied to word lines interact with resistive elements $G_{i,j}$ at junctions, and currents $\mathbf{I}$ are measured on bit lines. Applying $V_i$ generates current $V_i \times G_{i,j}$. Currents on the $j^{th}$ bit line sum to $I_j$, the dot product of $\mathbf{V}$ and the $j^{th}$ column of $\mathbf{G}$. Thus, the output current vector is $\mathbf{I}^\intercal = \mathbf{V}^\intercal \mathbf{G}$. \\
    
\noindent Application requirements dictate resistive device choice. Training requires frequent conductance updates, while inference uses fixed weights set after initial programming. Both require programmability. Memristors, with electrically tunable conductance, are ideal for crossbar accelerators. Training requires linear, repeatable programming, a significant challenge. Consequently, research often focuses on inference. \\
    
\noindent Memristors have a restricted conductance range $[G_{off}, G_{on}]$. Since conductance is positive, representing real numbers (including negatives) is challenging.  Differential pairs address this by representing $w$ as the difference between two conductances, $G+$ and $G-$ \cite{joksas2022nonideality}. These are chosen symmetrically around an average value and assigned to different bit lines \cite{kim20214k}. Independent currents $\mathbf{I}+$ and $\mathbf{I}-$ are generated, and the result is $\mathbf{I}+ - \mathbf{I}-$. Arranging positive and negative bit lines proximally mitigates line resistance effects \cite{joksas2020committee}. \\

\noindent Neuromorphic learning approaches include supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning \cite{stone2019artificial}. Unsupervised, local learning aligns best with biological models. Biorealistic learning draws on synaptic plasticity laws like Hebbian Learning \cite{kempter1999hebbian} ("neurons that fire together wire together"), simplified as:\\
\begin{align}
    \Delta w_{ij} = \eta \cdot x_i \cdot y_i \label{eq:2.31}
\end{align}

\noindent where $\Delta w_{ij}$ is the weight change, $x_i$ and $y_i$ are activity levels, and $\eta$ is the learning rate. Hebbian learning enhances synaptic strength via co-activity \cite{frenkel2019morphic}. STDP is a temporally sensitive variant. Homeostatic plasticity ensures neural activity remains within functional bounds, analogous to biological metabolic regulation.\\

\noindent Local circuit rules implement these mechanisms. In memristive STDP, pulse timing determines conductance change, embodying the learning rule physically. Digital learning involves updates like:
\begin{align}
    w_{ij} \leftarrow w_{ij} + \eta \cdot \delta_j \cdot x_i \label{eq:2.32} 
\end{align}

\noindent where $\delta_j$ is the error signal. Memristive learning avoids explicit backpropagation, using local rules where conductance change $\Delta G$ depends on spike timing and voltage:
\begin{align}
    \Delta G \varpropto f(\Delta t_{ij}) \cdot g(V_{pre}, V_{post}) \label{eq:2.33}
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $f(\Delta t_{ij})$ is the STDP window and $g(V_{pre}, V_{post})$ models voltage pulse effects. Memristors act as "plastic synapses," evolving weights in real time based on temporal correlations.

\subsection[Hardware Realization of Plasticity]{Hardware Realization of Plasticity}

\noindent Implementing biorealistic learning is central to memristor-based architectures. This section explores embedding neuroscience-inspired rules like STDP, Hebbian learning, and homeostatic regulation into memristive networks. Pulse-pairing schemes encode spike timing as overlapping voltage pulses to implement plasticity rules. Memristor conductance modulation physically realizes these rules \cite{campbell2016pulse}. \\

\noindent A presynaptic spike triggers a positive pulse, and a postsynaptic spike a negative one. Net voltage depends on temporal alignment. Constructive overlap (pre before post) exceeds the potentiation threshold, increasing conductance; reversed order triggers depression. \\

\noindent For pulse shapes $V_{pre}(t)$ and $V_{post}(t)$, the effective voltage is:
\begin{align}
    V_{mem}(t) = V_{pre}(t) - V_{post}(t) \label{eq:2.34}
\end{align}

\noindent Conductance $G(t)$ changes based on $V_{mem}(t)$ via a windowed integration rule:
\begin{align}
    \Delta G = \int_{-\infty}^{-\infty} \gamma (V_{mem}(t)) \,dt \label{eq:2.35}
\end{align}
\noindent where $\gamma(\cdot)$ maps voltage to conductance change non-linearly. \\

\noindent Memristive synapses form dense connectivity graphs. Configured with spiking neurons, emergent learning occurs. Typical architectures include LIF neuron layers, memristive crossbars for weights, and circuits for spike timing detection and pulse application.\\

\noindent For a neuron with inputs $x_i(t)$ and synapses $G_i(t)$, membrane potential $V_m$ evolves as:
\begin{align}
    C_m \frac{dV_m}{dt} = -\frac{V_m}{R_m} + \sum_{i} G_i(t) \cdot x_i(t) \label{eq:2.36}
\end{align}

\noindent where $C_m$ is capacitance and $R_m$ is leakage resistance. Exceeding threshold $V_{th}$ triggers a spike and reset. Synaptic updates follow:
\begin{align}
    \Delta G_i(t) = f(\Delta t_i) \cdot Pulse_{pairing}(x_i(t), y(t)) \label{eq:2.37}
\end{align}

\noindent with $f(\Delta t_i)$ as the STDP kernel and $Pulse_{pairing}$ as the pulse overlap function. Unsupervised pattern recognition illustrates biorealistic learning. Memristive networks exposed to spiking MNIST digits learn prototypes via STDP. Input pixels connect to neurons via memristive synapses, with intensity converted to spike trains. Winner-take-all mechanisms inhibit simultaneous firing. STDP strengthens synapses with temporally correlated inputs. Neurons specialize in specific patterns, emulating cortical feature selectivity.\\

\noindent Unbounded synaptic growth destabilizes learning. Biological systems use homeostasis for stability; analogous mechanisms are indispensable in memristive networks. Synaptic normalization constrains the sum of synaptic weights:
\begin{align}
    \sum_{i} G_i = G_{max} \label{eq:2.38}
\end{align}

\noindent Alternatively, weight decay models biological forgetting:
\begin{align}
    G_i(t + \Delta t) = (1 - \alpha) \cdot G_i(t) + \Delta G_i \label{eq:2.39}
\end{align}

\noindent where $\alpha$ is a decay factor. These mechanisms ensure stability and enable continual learning.

\section[System Architecture and Integration]{System Architecture and Integration}

\noindent Memristive networks eliminate the von Neumann bottleneck via in-memory learning. Subthreshold operation enables ultra-low-power computation, and physical time integration matches biological timescales. Challenges include variability, inconsistent behavior, write endurance degradation, and non-linear dynamics. Co-designing algorithms and devices adapts learning rules to device characteristics, facilitating practical implementation. Realizing practical systems requires scaling architectural integration. This section explores hierarchical organization, interfacing, and optimization, demonstrating the translation of bio-inspired learning into cohesive hardware.

\subsection[Scalable Hierarchical Architectures]{Scalable Hierarchical Architectures}

\noindent The crossbar array enables massive parallelism and efficient matrix-vector multiplication (MVM), a cornerstone of neural computation. Practical implementations face issues like sneak paths, line resistance, variability, and noise. Selector devices, resistive isolation, and adaptive calibration maintain accuracy and scalability.\\

\noindent Neocortical modularity inspires hierarchical neuromorphic architectures. Each "core" comprises spiking neurons, a local memristive crossbar, peripheral circuitry, and optional learning engines. Cores connect via event-driven networks mimicking axonal signaling, typically using Address-Event Representation (AER). This scales the architecture while preserving sparse, asynchronous activity (Figure \ref{fig:2i}). Cores operate independently, with global integration via spike-based communication.\\

\noindent Neurogrid \cite{khodagholy2015neurogrid} combines analog dendritic trees with digital processors. Memristive implementations replace charge-based memory with conductance-based synapses. The hybrid interface equation resembles:
\begin{align}
    y_j = \sigma \left( \sum_i G_{ij} \cdot V_i + b_j  \right) \label{eq:2.40}
\end{align}
\noindent where $G_{ij}$ is conductance, $V_i$ is input voltage, $b_j$ is digital bias, and $\sigma$ is the digital thresholding function. \\

\noindent Biorealistic learning requires online, local learning, unlike batch-trained deep learning. Memristive systems facilitate this via pulse modulation circuits applying updates based on spike timing. Systems incorporate time-window detectors and plasticity controllers. These engines exhibit high parallelism and low power consumption, facilitating real-time adaptation. Synapse conductance $G$ evolves via:
\begin{align}
    G(t + \Delta t) = G(t) + \Delta G = G(t) + f(\Delta t) \cdot V_{update} \label{eq:2.41}
\end{align}

\noindent where $f(\Delta t)$ is the learning window and $V_{update}$ is the voltage pulse. Experimental systems illustrate full-stack integration. Intel Loihi 2 \cite{orchard2021efficient} exhibits event-driven learning. The IMEC Dot Product Engine uses analog crossbars for pattern recognition \cite{zha2017imec}. NeuroSim+ simulates large-scale architectures \cite{chen2017neurosim+}. These systems facilitate complex tasks like speech recognition and motor control. \\

\noindent Memristive architectures are optimal for edge AI due to compactness and efficiency. On-device learning and inference eliminate cloud dependency. This requires local learning, non-volatile memory, and energy harvesting compatibility. This parallels biological unsupervised, embodied learning, reinforcing the bio-alignment of memristive architectures.

\subsection[Hardware-Software Co-Design Frameworks]{Hardware-Software Co-Design Frameworks}

\noindent Memristive hardware requires software co-design to realize its potential. Biorealistic learning, SNNs, and recurrent systems necessitate reconsidering software stacks to leverage memristive dynamics and constraints. Biorealistic models typically employ spiking neuron formulations like LIF, Izhikevich, or Hodgkin-Huxley, using differential equations for membrane dynamics:
\begin{align}
    \tau_m \frac{dV(t)}{dt} = -(V(t) - V_{rest}) + R_mI(t) \label{eq:2.42}
\end{align}

\noindent where $V(t)$ is membrane potential, $V_{rest}$ is resting potential, $I(t)$ is input current, $\tau_m$ is the time constant, and $R_m$ is resistance. Software frameworks must generate spike trains, emulate conductance changes, encode delays, and schedule computation. Frameworks like Brian2 \cite{stimberg2019brian}, NEST \cite{gewaltig2007nest}, and BindsNET \cite{hazan2018bindsnet} offer abstractions. Translation layers map models to hardware control signals. \\

\noindent Local learning rules must be compiled into device-level protocols. Quantization poses a challenge, as memristive devices have limited resolution (4-8 bits), requiring mapping of continuous gradients to discrete states. Physical constraints necessitate efficient placement and routing. Key constraints include fan-in/fan-out limits, synaptic locality, and event congestion, which requires load balancing.\\

\noindent Mapping tools use graph partitioning and spatial locality heuristics to co-locate interacting neurons and optimize routing. Software compensation algorithms mitigate stochasticity, variability, drift, and degradation. Methods include write-verify loops, adaptive learning rates, and redundancy encoding. Homeostatic plasticity can be implemented as system-level feedback:
\begin{align}
    \theta_i(t + 1) = \theta_i(t) + \eta(r_i - r_{target}) \label{eq:2.43}
\end{align}

\noindent where $\theta_i$ is the threshold, $r_i$ is the firing rate, and $\eta$ is the adjustment rate. This regulates hardware behavior to match desired statistics.Emulation platforms (FPGAs, software models) simulate memristive networks, preserving timing and constraints, reducing prototyping costs. Simulators like NeuroSim, MNSIM, CARLsim, and XNOR-Nets facilitate testing, scalability evaluation, and validation before silicon implementation. Domain-specific languages and toolchains (Nengo, PyNN, NxSDK) bridge algorithm design and hardware execution. Integration with these ecosystems enables a complete workflow: model specification, learning rule assignment, hardware mapping, and runtime adaptation. Future progress depends on co-designed environments abstracting hardware complexity.

\subsection[Benchmarking and Evaluation]{Benchmarking and Evaluation}

\noindent Experimental validation assesses the effectiveness of biorealistic learning algorithms. This section explores empirical studies, benchmarks, and comparisons with digital platforms and biological networks, discussing validation challenges and solutions. Benchmark tasks assess MNN robustness and efficiency, evaluating learning, generalization, and computational efficiency. Primary categories include pattern recognition and classification. \\

\noindent The MNIST dataset \cite{lecun1998gradient}, comprising preprocessed handwritten digits, frequently evaluates fundamental classification capabilities. While no longer challenging for state-of-the-art methods, its small size makes it a common benchmark for new models, enabling efficient training with slower methods. CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 \cite{krizhevsky2009learning} contain small color images from 10 and 100 categories, respectively. These datasets evaluate network performance on complex visual data. CIFAR-100 is particularly challenging due to category diversity and similarity. Memristive networks are also tested on speech recognition tasks for temporal pattern recognition.\\

\noindent Memory and learning tasks evaluate information storage and recall. Sequence learning tests temporal sequence acquisition, while working memory evaluates information retention and manipulation. Reinforcement learning tasks evaluate action optimization based on feedback. Atari games test the acquisition of decision-making strategies from pixel inputs. Benchmarks evaluate accuracy, learning speed, and energy efficiency. Memristive systems are often compared with digital platforms to assess energy consumption.\\

\noindent Empirical studies explore memristive SNN implementations, leveraging crossbar arrays and temporal dynamics. Memristive networks demonstrate competitive results in pattern recognition tasks compared to conventional algorithms.A 4-layer SNN on memristive hardware using STDP achieved 97\% accuracy with 30\% less energy than a GPU-based model.Memristive systems excel in memory tasks. A memristive RNN \cite{wang2018fully} for sequence prediction demonstrated:
\begin{align}
    E = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left| y_i - \hat{y}_i  \right| \label{eq:2.44}
\end{align}

\noindent where $E$ is error, $y_i$ is true output, and $\hat{y}_i$ is predicted output. The memristive RNN outperformed traditional RNNs by 20\% in accuracy and efficiency.Memristive networks show promise in reinforcement learning. A memristive SNN learned Atari 2600 games using reward feedback and a reward-modulated plasticity rule \cite{bohnstingl2019reinforcement}:
\begin{align}
    \Delta w_{ij} = \eta \cdot \left( r_i - \alpha \cdot w_{ij} \right) \label{eq:2.45}
\end{align}

\noindent where $\eta$ is learning rate, $r_i$ is reward, and $\alpha$ is decay factor. This SNN achieved an 85\% success rate, outperforming traditional models by 10\% in performance and efficiency.\\

\noindent Memristive systems offer superior energy efficiency. They excel at low-energy, parallel computation. A 1-watt memristive chip can perform tasks requiring 100 watts on a conventional CPU or GPU \cite{prezioso2015training}. Permanent synaptic weight storage reduces memory refresh needs, enhancing power efficiency for continuous, real-time systems.Scaling challenges exist. Device-to-device variations in conductance change require calibration or redundancy. Increasing device density increases crosstalk risk, requiring advanced routing and encoding. Lower precision necessitates quantization, error correction, and approximate computing.\\

\noindent Future validation will explore complex tasks. Brain emulation research focuses on large-scale networks using memristive chips to model cognitive processes. Future research aims to create sophisticated networks for accurate cognitive modeling, advancing AI and neuroscience. Integrating neuroprosthetics with memristive systems could restore sensory or motor functions. Future studies may explore hybrid systems combining biological neurons and memristive devices. The long-term potential lies in mirroring biological computation for improved efficiency, power, and scalability.
