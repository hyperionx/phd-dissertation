\begin{abstract}
\noindent A synapse learns from its own spike history, yet the consequence of a spike usually arrives after
the spike has gone. Something local must hold what was recently active until that consequence is
known, and spiking networks normally hold it as an abstraction with a chosen lifetime. This thesis
holds it in the material instead, in the slow current transient a memristive silicon oxide device
shows below its switching threshold, a response usually dismissed as degradation. The transient is
measured across gold and indium tin oxide contacts, traced to dispersive trap filling and
discharge, and reduced to a compact model.\\

\noindent Made into a synapse, the device is trained with its own Poole--Frenkel conductance statistics
folded into training rather than compensated for afterwards, and a homeostatic regulariser
recovers part of that cost. Held instead as a volatile state, the same transient carries a
classification across an interval with no input, so spike history stays readable after the input
has gone. How long it stays readable depends on more than the average lifetime of the decay.
Replacing the measured decay with a single exponential of the same average lifetime changes that
interval by a fifth to a third. Clearing the state one layer at a time shows that no single layer
is sufficient. A retention time alone therefore does not specify what a task can still use.\\

\noindent Learning from spike history asks more of the transient, because a late reward must find which
coincidences earned it. The measured discharge is that eligibility term, and the canonical
exponential rule its narrow-dispersion limit, so the credit window is measured rather than
assumed. The material does not settle that kernel by itself, since the same measurements fitted
under different conditions credit opposite events even at equal mean retention. A relaxation
offered as a learning variable must therefore be reported with the conditions that produced it. On
that kernel a signed coincidence rule learns a two-layer policy in simulation. Fixed random
feedback stands in for weight transport, and local homeostasis keeps the learner stable.
Biorealistic learning here means
learning from spike history, so what a material intrinsically remembers sets what a synapse can
learn. A rule then becomes something matter holds rather than something imposed on it.
\end{abstract}
