\chapter{Homeostasis Optimisation}

\section[Challenges in Analogue Optimisation]{Challenges in Analogue Optimisation}

This chapter delves into the intricate challenges and innovative solutions associated with implementing Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) on analogue memristive hardware, ultimately extending their application to real-time biosignal processing. A central hypothesis guiding this work is that neuromorphic robustness under hardware variability can be achieved through biologically inspired regulatory mechanisms and tailored training algorithms. The chapter specifically focuses on mitigating the inherent imprecision and non-idealities of analogue memristive devices, introducing a novel homeostasis-driven dropout mechanism, and demonstrating its efficacy in a memristive convolutional SNN (MCSNN) for end-to-end biosignal classification.

\subsection[Memristor Non-Idealities and Constraints]{Memristor Non-Idealities and Physical Constraints}

\noindent While memristors provide a powerful substrate for biorealistic computing, analogue hardware inherently suffers from limited precision and reliability. Unlike digital systems that mask physical imperfections with binary representations, analogue electronics expose the intrinsic imprecision of continuous systems. Even minimal non-idealities prevent developers from encoding information with exact conductance values. This section details the motivations for optimizing neuromorphic computing systems, focusing on the physical constraints of analogue hardware and their impact on computation accuracy.\\

\noindent Analogue devices, including RRAM, suffer from stuck states, device-to-device variability, and I-V nonlinearity \cite{mehonic2017intrinsic}. These imperfections force devices into fixed conductance states, cause conductance drift, and produce non-linear voltage responses. Although advanced fabrication and circuit-level optimizations mitigate some issues, algorithmic techniques provide an effective supplementary defense.\\


\noindent Analogue computing provides fundamentally lower precision than standard 16-bit digital systems. While this limitation disqualifies analogue circuits for precise numerical tasks, machine learning naturally tolerates reduced precision and minor noise \cite{cheney2017robustness}. Nevertheless, severe hardware imperfections substantially degrade accuracy, requiring robust mitigation strategies.\\


\noindent Linear algebra applications demand Ohmic behavior—proportional voltage and current—to accurately perform multiplication. High-resistance devices, however, frequently violate this linearity. Device and circuit-level modifications can correct this. For instance, hot-forming during RRAM initialization creates a denser, metallic-like conductive filament that yields more linear characteristics \cite{sung2018effect}. Furthermore, a 1T1R (one-transistor-one-resistor) architecture precisely tunes memristor conductance despite I-V nonlinearities \cite{li2018analogue}. Alternatively, encoding inputs into pulse widths via charge accumulation bypasses the I-V curve shape entirely \cite{amirsoleimani2020memory}.\\


\noindent Memristors frequently stick in specific conductance states after electroforming or programming cycles \cite{joksas2022nonideality}. Larger discrepancies between the intended and actual conductance exacerbate network errors. However, adjusting the complementary device in a differential pair can counteract a stuck device's negative impact \cite{liu2017rescuing}. If engineers identify faulty devices prior to programming, they can deploy sophisticated mapping strategies, assigning the most critical weights to crossbar regions with the fewest stuck devices \cite{gaol2021reliable}. Evaluating weight sensitivity, $\Delta w_{i,j} := - \eta \frac{\partial E}{\partial \Delta w_{i,j}}$, identifies these critical parameters, where $E$ represents the back-propagated loss and $\eta$ denotes the learning rate.\\


\noindent A limited dynamic range, defined by a small $\frac{G_{on}}{G_{off}}$ ratio, decreases effective precision. Combined with device variability, a narrow range restricts the number of distinguishable resistance states. While inference tasks can occasionally tolerate small dynamic ranges—naive mapping schemes function with a $\frac{G_{on}}{G_{off}}$ ratio as low as 3 \cite{mehonic2019simulation}—a larger range improves state differentiation and network resilience when compounding non-idealities arise.\\

\noindent Line resistance from non-zero interconnects introduces errors into crossbar vector-matrix products. Physical and algorithmic techniques minimize these discrepancies. Engineers can reduce interconnect resistance by widening wires \cite{li2017three} or deploying highly conductive 2nm platinum nanofins \cite{pi2019memristor}. Circuit-level strategies like double biasing symmetrically distribute electric potentials to attenuate line resistance \cite{hu2016dot}. Splitting large arrays into smaller blocks \cite{xia2016technological} or utilizing 3D structures \cite{xia2019memristive} also mitigates the issue. Algorithmically, nonlinear mapping or sensitivity analysis places critical weights near the applied voltages \cite{agrawal2019x}. Finally, in situ training relies on linear conductance adjustments \cite{burr2015experimental}, achieved by structural modifications \cite{woo2016improved, wu2018methodology} or pairing memristors with CMOS transistors \cite{ambrogio2018equivalent}.\\


\noindent Memristors, particularly high-resistance devices, frequently exhibit random telegraph noise (RTN), randomly switching between discrete voltage levels \cite{puglisi2016guidelines}. Adopting non-filamentary architectures effectively eliminates this noise \cite{chai2018impact}. However, rather than addressing individual physical flaws, developers can employ technology-agnostic algorithmic strategies to optimize high-level metrics like classification accuracy. Averaging outputs across parallel networks or over time suppresses stochastic noise like RTN \cite{wan2020voltage}. Furthermore, training models with generic noise \cite{ye2023improving} or targeted non-ideality noise \cite{huang2021method} substantially fortifies network robustness.

\subsection[Existing Neuromorphic Training Algorithms]{Existing Neuromorphic Training Algorithms}


\noindent Spike-based optimization techniques minimize network error by adjusting continuous spike times. However, the threshold-driven nature of spiking neurons renders the problem highly non-linear; minor input variations can push a neuron over its threshold and radically alter its output \cite{gutig2014spike}.\\

\noindent SpikeProp pioneered supervised deep learning via spike time optimization \cite{bohte2002error}. It simplifies the problem by optimizing only a single spike per neuron per interval and models synapses with multiple delayed terminals. While SpikeProp solved the XOR problem and matched backpropagation on small datasets, its single-spike constraint and complex synaptic structure hindered scalability. Subsequent research accelerated convergence \cite{mckennoch2006fast}, yet practical applications remained scarce.\\

\noindent Mostafa proposed an alternative for non-leaky integrate-and-fire neurons \cite{mostafa2017supervised}, relaxing the discrete-delay constraint and utilizing a standard architecture with one exponential-synapse connection per neuron pair. These networks achieved 2.45\% and 2.86\% error rates on MNIST with one and two hidden layers, respectively. However, standard dropout regularization failed; it forced neurons to completely cease firing, causing severe generalization errors despite near-zero training errors.\\

\noindent Other researchers optimize multiple spikes per neuron. Stromatias et al. applied a genetic algorithm to multi-spike firing but tested it only on networks with fewer than 10 hidden neurons, raising scalability concerns for datasets like CIFAR-10 \cite{stromatias2015supervised}. Conversely, Lee et al. treated neuron output as a linear function of filtered inputs, enabling backpropagation over multiple spikes by bypassing spiking discontinuities \cite{lee2016training}. By ignoring the refractory period and adding lateral inhibition, their model achieved 1.30\% and 1.23\% error rates on MNIST using SGD and ADAM optimizers. Although scalability requires further proof, this approach introduced vital concepts for SNN training.\\


\noindent Huh and Sejnowski introduced a gating function, $g(V)$, applied to the membrane voltage, $V$, to continuously approximate the spiking process \cite{huh2018gradient}. This function remains positive near the firing threshold, evaluates to zero elsewhere, and integrates to one. Rather than receiving discrete pulses, synapses continuously draw current proportional to $g(V)\frac{dV}{dt}$. This term evaluates to zero outside the active zone, integrates to one if the voltage crosses the threshold (a full spike), and produces a fractional value if the voltage enters but fails to cross the threshold (a partial spike). This induced current mirrors the traditional $\delta (t - t_k)$ spiking current at extremes while preserving continuity in intermediate regions.\\

\noindent This continuity permits gradient computation via backpropagation through time, facilitating full-network optimization. Huh and Sejnowski evaluated this method on temporal predictive coding rather than static classification. By making neural nonlinearity differentiable, this strategy supports diverse neuron models and effectively trains recurrent spiking networks. However, the extensive time-series optimization required for each input restricts its utility for large-scale, static object classification.\\


\noindent Researchers rarely apply spike-based optimization to deep architectures like convolutional neural networks (CNNs), restricting their use beyond MNIST. Spike-based methods demand immense computational resources by requiring iterative simulation for each stimulus, a problem compounded by the scarcity of specialized SNN software. Consequently, large-scale SNN training typically relies on rate-based optimization.\\


\noindent Rate-based optimization assumes neurons use rate coding, evaluating the total spike count over a time window rather than precise spike times. These techniques replace the discrete spiking process with a continuous rate approximation. Derivative-based methods differentiate this rate approximation to compute gradients. Conversely, derivative-free algorithms, such as Contrastive Divergence, completely bypass differentiation. Finally, function approximation methods select a continuous nonlinearity to train the network, subsequently substituting spiking neurons to emulate that trained function.

\section[Homeostasis-Driven Regularization]{Homeostasis-Driven Synaptic Regularization}

This section introduces homeostasis as a biologically inspired mechanism for regularization in SNNs, particularly when implemented on memristive hardware. By dynamically adjusting synaptic strength based on neuronal firing activity, homeostasis can stabilize learning and mitigate the impact of faulty or overactive devices. The section explores how this mechanism can be modeled mathematically and implemented to improve robustness and energy efficiency.

\subsection[Variability Modelling and Dropout Mechanism]{Variability Modelling and the Dropout Mechanism}

\noindent Memristive devices frequently stick at either $G_{off}$ or $G_{on}$. Previous studies often modeled this phenomenon by assigning a predefined stuck-state probability \cite{joksas2020committee, xia2017stuck}. This data-agnostic model integrates easily with other non-idealities, such as the empirically modeled $SiO_x$ nonlinearities.\\

\noindent This study adopts a continuous probabilistic model to simulate stuck device behavior in large memristor arrays. The model classifies a device as "stuck" if its maximum conductance range falls below half the median range ($G_{on} - G_{off}$), where $G_{off}$ and $G_{on}$ represent the median minimum and maximum conductances. By treating partially stuck devices as fully stuck, this approach intentionally overestimates variability to ensure robust simulation.\\

\noindent For stuck devices, kernel density estimation (KDE) approximates the conductance distribution from empirical measurements \cite{turlach1993bandwidth}. A truncated normal distribution with reflection at zero models the probability density function, $p(G)$, preventing unphysical negative conductances:
\begin{equation}
    p(G) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ \mathcal{N}(G_i, \sigma ^2) + \mathcal{N}(-G_i, \sigma ^2) \cdot 1_{G_i < 0} \right] \label{eq:7.1}
\end{equation}
\noindent Here, $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ denotes a normal distribution, and the second term handles the zero-reflection \cite{jones1993simple}. Scott's rule estimates the standard deviation \cite{scott2015multivariate}.\\


\noindent Inaccurate programming introduces device-to-device (D2D) variability. Resistance deviations adhere to a lognormal distribution, exhibiting more pronounced variations in the high-resistance state (HRS) than the low-resistance state (LRS). A lognormal distribution models these conductance deviations:
\begin{equation}
    G_{observed} = G_{target} \cdot exp(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_g^2)) \label{eq:7.2}
\end{equation}
\noindent This formulation ensures multiplicative variations and preserves positive-definite conductance values.\\

\noindent Integrating an $L_1$ sparsification regularizer into the loss function promotes sparse, low-power conductance states and prevents overfitting \cite{han2015learning}. Because synaptic weights map linearly to physical conductances, the total loss is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
    L_{total} = L_{CE} + \lambda \sum_{i}|G_i| \label{eq:7.3}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $L_{CE}$ is the cross-entropy loss and $\lambda$ is a tunable regularization coefficient \cite{feurer2019hyperparameter}. This regularizer biases the network toward energy-efficient states, aligning it with biological constraints and improving overall robustness.\\

\noindent Recent research explores various strategies to mitigate memristor non-idealities in SNNs \cite{morshed2023choose}. Advanced STDP implementations enhance robustness via adaptive learning rates and probabilistic weight updates \cite{burr2017neuromorphic, pehle2022brainscales}, while hybrid analogue-digital designs improve weight storage consistency \cite{friedmann2016demonstrating}. A promising alternative exploits the subthreshold conduction regime, where low-power current transients encode precise temporal information \cite{mannion2023unipolar}, directly benefiting spike-based tasks like neuromorphic vision \cite{mannion2020memristor}. This transient behavior improves the biological fidelity of learning mechanisms like eligibility traces and homeostasis \cite{demiraug2021pcm, john2022reconfigurable}. Furthermore, homeostasis-driven dropout dynamically modulates synaptic conductance to prevent overfitting and stabilize learning \cite{vu2024spiking}.\\

\noindent To implement dropout regularization in SNNs, the proposed homeostasis mechanism dynamically adjusts synaptic conductance based solely on the presynaptic neuron's firing rate \cite{kim2021spiking, stoffel2024spiking}. The steady-state conductance, \( G_{\text{steady}} \), exhibits a non-monotonic response to the input firing rate, \( r_{\text{in}} \). Conductance remains high at low firing rates but decreases once the firing rate surpasses a specific threshold, inducing synaptic depression. This response effectively suppresses the influence of individually overactive or faulty neurons. Because it requires neither complex bipolar presynaptic pulses nor access to postsynaptic variables, this approach significantly simplifies circuit design while enhancing regularization. Equation \ref{eq:7.4} defines this homeostatic dropout:
\begin{equation}
G_{\text{steady}} = \frac{G_{\text{max}}}{1 + \left(\frac{r_{\text{in}}}{r_{\text{threshold}}}\right)^n} \label{eq:7.4}
\end{equation}


\noindent Here, \( G_{\text{max}} \) represents the maximum conductance, \( r_{\text{threshold}} \) marks the firing rate where depression begins, and \( n \) dictates the transition's sharpness. By suppressing synapses connected to hyperactive neurons, this model effectively implements dropout. The product of the synaptic conductance \( G_{\text{steady}} \) and the input voltage \( V_{\text{in}} \) determines the synapse's output current \( I_{\text{out}} \):
\begin{equation}
I_{\text{out}} = G_{\text{steady}} \cdot V_{\text{in}} \label{eq:7.5}
\end{equation}


\noindent This dynamic conductance adjustment selectively dampens the influence of overactive neurons during training, mirroring traditional dropout. Modulating synaptic conductance based on firing rates ensures that only relevant connections remain active, thereby improving network robustness and preventing overfitting. Figure \ref{fig:7a} illustrates a proposed architecture that incorporates this dropout mechanism into a convolutional memristive SNN (MCSNN) using crossbar arrays, highlighting the targeted suppression of faulty neurons.\\

\noindent The homeostasis-dropout method leverages the SNN time-step mechanism by evaluating outputs from a recent window of time steps rather than averaging across the entire sequence. By dynamically adjusting synaptic conductance based on localized firing rates, the system identifies periods of intense activity and selectively suppresses overactive neurons. This targeted approach reduces computational costs by bypassing redundant evaluations, fortifies network robustness, and prevents overfitting by emphasizing the most critical temporal features during both training and inference.

\subsection[Validation of Homeostatic Dropout]{Validation of Homeostatic Dropout}


\noindent To evaluate performance on the spike-encoded MNIST dataset, this study designed a two-layer memristive Spiking Neural Network (MSNN) and an enhanced Memristive Convolutional Spiking Neural Network (MCSNN). The MSNN features 100 hidden Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) neurons and a 10-neuron output layer. The MCSNN incorporates a first convolutional layer with 12 filters ($5\times5$ kernel, $2\times2$ pooling), a second convolutional layer with 64 filters ($2\times2$ pooling), and a final memristive fully connected 10-neuron output layer. Both networks utilize LIF activation and digital implementations for hardware compatibility. Training employed identical hyperparameters: a batch size of 64, random weight initialization, a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-4}$, and 50 steps per epoch over 20 epochs, using PyTorch's cross-entropy loss function with automatic softmax activation. The architecture integrates kernel density estimation (KDE) to simulate probabilistic memristor failures and device-to-device variations. Crucially, a homeostasis mechanism temporarily disables hyperactive neurons to maintain stable dynamics. Figure \ref{fig:7b} illustrates feature maps from the second convolutional layer containing intentionally hyperactive neurons. Activating the homeostasis dropout selectively suppresses only the hyperactive channels, leaving normal channels unaffected. This targeted suppression proves the system can regulate unstable dynamics locally without disrupting the entire network.\\

\section[Application to Biosignal Processing]{Application to Real-Time Biosignal Processing}

This section explores memristive SNNs for processing time-sensitive biosignals like EEG and MEG. By combining event-driven SNNs with convolutional layers, the architecture efficiently extracts spatiotemporal features. Highlighting the advantages of SNNs for real-time edge computing, this section demonstrates end-to-end signal classification directly within the spiking model.

\subsection[Memristive CSNN Architecture]{Memristive CSNN Architecture for EEG and MEG}

\noindent Biosignals like Electroencephalogram (EEG) and Magnetoencephalogram (MEG) contain complex, time-sensitive information that challenges traditional deep learning models. By processing data as discrete spikes, Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) closely mimic biological signaling, offering significant improvements in temporal precision and energy efficiency over conventional Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs).\\

\noindent Convolutional SNNs (CSNNs) harness the spatial feature extraction power of CNNs while maintaining the sparse, event-driven dynamics of spiking neurons. CSNNs consistently outperform standard architectures in biosignal tasks, achieving over 99\% accuracy in real-time anticipatory EEG potential detection \cite{lutes2024convolutional} and 98.7\% accuracy in EEG-based stress detection \cite{Joshi2025-sa}.\\

\noindent However, implementing large-scale, low-power SNNs on conventional hardware remains challenging. Memristive hardware resolves this bottleneck by deploying synaptic weights directly within crossbar arrays for native in-memory computing. Combining event-driven spiking dynamics, convolutional spatial extraction, and memristive hardware yields a highly efficient architecture. These characteristics position memristive CSNNs as a potent solution for real-time, on-device biosignal processing, making them ideal for battery-sensitive applications like brain-computer interfaces and ambulatory diagnostics.\\

\noindent This study utilized open-source EEG and MEG data from the MGH/HMS/MIT Athinoula A. Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging \cite{Gramfort2020-jm}. Researchers recorded 60-channel EEG synchronized with MEG while subjects received auditory (left/right ear tones) and visual (left/right visual field checkerboards) stimuli at 750 ms intervals. Subjects responded to unpredictable visual targets (smiley faces) via a button press. The dataset encoded specific trigger codes for each stimulus and response (e.g., auditory left = 1, button press = 32), embedding these events directly into the recordings for precise epoching and analysis.\\

\noindent Data preprocessing relied on the MNE-Python library \cite{gramfort2014mne}. The pipeline extracted event triggers, applied a 1--40 Hz band-pass filter to remove noise, and epoched the data relative to stimulus onset. After removing drift via baseline correction and eliminating artifacts using Independent Component Analysis, the pipeline z-score normalized and resampled all signals to a uniform temporal resolution. The final preprocessed EEG segments formed tensors of shape [64, 1, 376, 71], representing batch size, input channel, timepoints, and EEG channels, respectively.\\

\noindent The proposed memristive CSNN architecture seamlessly integrates the spatial pattern recognition strengths of CNNs with the temporal sensitivity of SNNs. Unlike conventional recurrent models such as BiLSTMs \cite{malviya2022novel}, the CSNN bypasses sequential memory components. Instead, it relies on event-driven temporal processing via spiking neurons, substantially reducing inference latency and energy consumption without sacrificing modeling fidelity.\\

\noindent The model utilizes threshold-based latency encoding, converting signal amplitudes into inversely proportional spike latencies. These encoded spike trains then pass through a convolutional layer that applies 2D spatial filters over the spatiotemporal input. The spiking dynamics govern the output of each convolutional layer:
\begin{equation} h_{ij}^{l} = \sigma \left( \sum _{k} w_{k}^{l} \cdot x_{(i+k)(j+k)} + b^{l} \right) \label{eq:7.6}
\end{equation}
\noindent Here, $h_{ij}^{l}$ represents the output at position $(i,j)$, $w_{k}^{l}$ denotes the filter weight, and $\sigma$ is the spiking activation function \cite{tavanaei2019deep}. Each convolutional stage feeds into Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) spiking neurons. The architecture also incorporates homeostatic dropout layers to regularize firing activity based on the dynamic conductance principles of oxide-based memristors.\\

\noindent Stacking multiple convolution-LIF blocks with max pooling captures hierarchical feature representations \cite{shrestha2018slayer}. The network flattens the final spiking representation and passes it to fully connected LIF output layers. To train the network across multiple timesteps, the system employs Backpropagation-Through-Time (BPTT) equipped with surrogate gradients. This strategy approximates the non-differentiable Heaviside step function with smooth activations, ensuring stable and effective gradient descent despite the binary nature of the spike outputs.

\subsection[Classification Performance and Resilience]{Classification Performance and Network Resilience}

\noindent The MCSNN incorporates a homeostatic dropout layer between the convolutional and fully connected stages to regulate synaptic conductances based on presynaptic firing activity. In biosignal processing, this adaptation creates a robust, biorealistic system capable of suppressing the severe noise and inter-subject variability inherent to EEG and MEG signals. By dynamically dampening unstable connections, homeostasis mitigates overfitting to spurious features. This ensures the network learns meaningful patterns and achieves the stable temporal integration required to classify short-epoch event-related potentials, such as visual or auditory triggers.\\

\noindent Furthermore, adjusting the synaptic drive indirectly modulates the neuronal spike threshold, preventing saturation during transient bursts and preserving sensitivity to relevant temporal features. As Equation (\ref{eq:7.4}) defines, synaptic conductance peaks at moderate presynaptic firing rates and sharply drops during hyperactivity. This mechanism tunes the system to extract meaningful mid-range activations—analogous to biological filters discarding extreme values—and optimizes feature extraction from complex biosignals.\\

\noindent Training the MCSNN utilized cross-entropy loss computed directly on the time-averaged spike rate of the output neurons. This approach enables end-to-end classification entirely within the spiking model, eliminating the need for external classifiers and enhancing the system's biological plausibility and real-time autonomy. The Adam optimizer, paired with weight decay, minimized loss over 20 to 50 epochs. Following evaluation on a held-out test set, dimensionality reduction techniques (PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP) visualized class separability directly from the final convolutional layer's feature maps (Figure \ref{fig:7f}).\\

\noindent From a cognitive neuroscience perspective, this architecture models both the information processing and regulatory homeodynamics observed in neocortical circuits. Just as the human brain reduces sensitivity to constant stimuli to remain responsive to environmental changes, SNN homeostasis downregulates overactive synapses to maintain sensitivity to new input patterns. This prevents saturation and ensures a dynamic response to evolving biosignals. While biological systems rely on neurotransmitters like dopamine and serotonin to modulate excitability, the MCSNN emulates this intrinsic regulation through spike-frequency-dependent conductance modulation, achieving stability without requiring complex, global feedback mechanisms.\\

\noindent Ultimately, homeostasis distributes spike activity across the neuronal population, preventing a small subset of hyperactive neurons from dominating the encoding process. This distributed representation proves critical for capturing rich, high-dimensional biosignals like EEG, as it broadens the representational capacity and significantly enhances fault tolerance. This convergence of bioinspired function and rigorous empirical validation confirms the feasibility of biorealistic computation, demonstrating that co-designing hardware and algorithms yields machines capable of emulating biological intelligence in both form and adaptive function.

\section[Summary]{Summary}

\noindent Building upon the hardware-aware frameworks from Chapter 6, this chapter addressed resilient optimization strategies for analogue memristive SNNs. To mitigate device variability and failures, it introduced a biologically inspired homeostasis-driven dropout mechanism. By dynamically decreasing synaptic conductance in response to hyperactive presynaptic firing, this regularization suppresses overactive pathways. Simulation results confirmed its efficacy across fully connected and convolutional architectures, achieving over 20\% accuracy improvements despite 50\% simulated device failure. Integrating biological regulatory mechanisms provides a robust algorithmic defense against severe hardware imperfections.\\

\noindent Extending these architectures to real-world tasks, the chapter demonstrated end-to-end classification of complex biosignals using a memristive CSNN. Evaluated on EEG data, the CSNN leveraged spatial filters and temporally encoded spikes to achieve up to 98.44\% classification accuracy, verified via t-SNE and UMAP visualizations. The synergy between hardware-aware homeostatic dropout and event-driven processing creates a resilient, energy-efficient system. These findings establish a foundational framework for deploying memristive SNNs in real-time edge-computing applications, such as cognitive monitoring and brain-computer interfaces.
