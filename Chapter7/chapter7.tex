\chapter{Memristive Reinforcement Learning}\label{ch:learning}

\noindent Delayed reward requires each active synapse to retain a local state until the outcome of an action becomes
known. In a canonical three-factor rule, local activity deposits eligibility, a decay kernel carries it towards reward,
and the reward gates the persistent weight update. This chapter takes the normalised SiO$_x$ relaxation
measured in Chapter~\ref{ch:model} as that decay kernel, derives how it weights events at different delays, and tests
the resulting predictions in closed-loop reinforcement learning.\\

\noindent The author developed the kernel identification, learning rules, simulations, and analyses reported in this
chapter. The same measured decay also drives a two-layer learner without weight transport on a task that requires
credit to be divided between layers. The mesoporous-SiO$_2$ relaxations \cite{zhang2024mesoporous} refitted for the
external check were measured and deposited by another group, and the reanalysis is the author's. Only the decay
profile comes from the device measurement: activity-dependent
deposition, signed eligibility, reward gating, and persistent weight updates remain simulated, and no integrated
learning device is claimed.

\section[Measured Eligibility-Kernel Identification]{Measured Eligibility-Kernel Identification}
\label{sec:7p1}

\noindent Before the measured current can enter a learning rule, it must be converted into a dimensionless decay, and
that conversion requires a background current, an amplitude, and a fitted observation interval. The first subsection
identifies the kernel and places it in the canonical three-factor equation. Fit reliability follows in the second, which
tests how the fitted interval and background-current definition alter the inferred kernel and, consequently, its
temporal-credit prediction.

\subsection[Measured Decay in the Canonical Rule]{Measured Decay in the Canonical Rule}
\label{sec:7p1p1}

\noindent An eligibility trace is a synapse-local state written by recent activity and read when reward arrives.
Within the learning model, each deposited contribution carries unit amplitude, while the measurement supplies its
subsequent decay. Writing $q(\ell)$ for the fraction remaining after lag $\ell$, the normalised post-drive relaxation
of the \abbrev{ITO}-contacted SiO$_x$ device provides the kernel directly instead of imposing a chosen
time constant.\\

\noindent Ten relaxations were available from one randomly selected \abbrev{ITO}-contacted pad in one session, measured
by the near-zero-bias protocol of Section~\ref{sec:3pulse}. The kernel screen of Section~\ref{sec:3analysis} retained nine
measurements; the excluded measurement returned $R^2=0.88$.
These nine measurements quantify repeatability within one session on one contact instead of variation between devices,
and the drive history immediately preceding each of them was not documented.\\

\noindent Equation~\ref{eq:3.1}, a stretched exponential, gives the fraction of one deposited contribution that
survives to lag $\ell$, and its mean retention
follows from the fitted scale and shape parameters:
\begin{align}
    q(\ell) &= \exp\!\left[-\left(\frac{\ell}{\tau_{leak}}\right)^{\beta_{leak}}\right], \qquad
    \langle t \rangle = \int_{0}^{\infty} q(\ell)\, d\ell = \frac{\tau_{leak}}{\beta_{leak}}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{leak}}\right). \label{eq:7.1}
\end{align}
\noindent Here $\tau_{leak}$ is a retention scale in seconds, and $\beta_{leak}$ sets the decay shape. Each measurement
was fitted separately as a decaying current above a settled background, fixed at the median current over the final
tenth of the complete measurement. Subtracting that background and dividing by the fitted amplitude gives the
dimensionless kernel, with $q(0)=1$.\\

\noindent The arithmetic mean of the nine fitted parameter pairs gives $\tau_{leak}=16.0$\,s and
$\beta_{leak}=0.622$, with mean $R^2=0.995$ and no measurement below $0.992$. The fitted amplitudes span a factor of
$1.87$, while the normalised decays closely overlap, so averaging parameter pairs gives each measurement equal
weight.\\

\noindent Across the nine measurements, $\tau_{leak}$ ranges from $14.8$ to $17.5$\,s, and $\beta_{leak}$ ranges
from $0.604$ to $0.647$. Every fit is dispersive and remains below the exponential value
$\beta_{leak}=1$, consistent with the close overlap after normalisation, and Equation~\ref{eq:7.1} gives a mean
retention of $23.0$\,s for the aggregate kernel
(Figure~\ref{fig:7r}).\\

    post-drive currents from one contact, each with its stretched-exponential fit. \textbf{(b)} The same measurements after

    $\beta_{leak}=0.622$.}
    \label{fig:7r}
\end{figure}

\noindent Within the canonical three-factor rule, the normalised decay weights every locally generated
eligibility contribution according to its age when reward arrives:
\begin{align}
    e_{ij}(t_R) &= \int_{0}^{\infty} d_{ij}(t_R - \ell)\, q(\ell)\, d\ell, \label{eq:7.2} \\
    \Delta w_{ij} &= \eta \left( R - b \right) e_{ij}(t_R). \label{eq:7.3}
\end{align}
\noindent Here $d_{ij}$ is the signed drive produced by local input and output activity at synapse $ij$,
$e_{ij}(t_R)$ is the eligibility surviving at reward time $t_R$, $R$ is the received reward, $b$ is a running reward
expectation, and $\eta$ is the learning rate. This product $(R-b)e_{ij}$ sets the update sign: agreement between reward
prediction error and eligibility potentiates the synapse, while disagreement depresses it
\cite{fremaux2016neuromodulated, florian2007reinforcement}.\\

\noindent For the exponential member of this function, $q(\ell)=e^{-\ell/\tau_{leak}}$, the convolution in
Equation~\ref{eq:7.2} reduces exactly to the first-order equation
\begin{align}
    \dot{e}_{ij}(t) &= d_{ij}(t) - \frac{e_{ij}(t)}{\tau_{leak}}, \label{eq:7.4}
\end{align}
\noindent which is the canonical exponential eligibility equation
\cite{izhikevich2007solving, florian2007reinforcement}. Setting $\beta_{leak}=1$ therefore recovers the standard rule,
whereas the measured $\beta_{leak}=0.622$ replaces its single-timescale decay with a dispersive kernel inside the same
update. Equation~\ref{eq:7.1} remains defined for every positive exponent, including the values above unity returned
by the truncated fits below. For $\beta_{leak}<1$, equal increments of time remove progressively smaller fractions of
what remains, so the exponent sets how the surviving fraction changes with age. Equal mean retention can therefore
conceal different weighting of newer and older events. Because $q(0)=1$, eligibility is greatest when deposited and
then decays without an internal rising phase.\\

\noindent The measurement identifies the decay kernel and not the microscopic mechanism behind it, while the remaining
functions of the learning rule, namely activity-dependent deposition, signed storage, eligibility sensing, reward
gating, and weight updates, are simulated. The kernel uses the \abbrev{ITO} relaxation alone, because combining
it with the conductance rise measured in Ti/Au-contacted devices would construct a transient that no single device
family produced. Figure~\ref{fig:7s} shows how $\tau_{leak}$ and $\beta_{leak}$ change the eligibility surviving at
reward.

    working interpretation links coincident activity to trap filling and subsequent emission; no trap distribution is

\subsection[Fit Reliability and Reporting]{Fit Reliability and Reporting}
\label{sec:7p1p2}

\noindent Primary identification uses each complete $226$\,s measurement and fixes the background from its final
tenth. For the truncation comparison, the same selected-interval-tail rule applies to both the $30$\,s and $226$\,s
intervals; over the complete interval the two definitions coincide and return the same exponent. This distinction
matters because the truncation comparison changes observation length alone, while the broader sensitivity analysis
varies observation length and background treatment independently.\\

\noindent Fitted retention and dispersion depend on what part of the relaxation is observed and which current is
treated as settled. Each measurement was therefore refitted over intervals ending at $20$, $30$, $60$, $120$, $160$,
and $226$\,s. The background was fixed from the final tenth of the complete measurement, estimated freely from
the selected interval, or fixed from the final tenth of that interval. This last convention treats the end of every
selected interval as settled, even when the current may still be decreasing.\\

\noindent The nine measurements, six intervals, and three background treatments produced $162$ fits.
Across individual measurements, $\beta_{leak}$ ranged from $0.598$ to $1.268$, and the means for each fitting
condition ranged from $0.616$ to $1.210$, so both ranges cross the exponential value of unity. This dependence arises
from the slow tail: assigning the end of a short interval to the background raises the estimated settled current,
steepens the apparent early decay, and increases the fitted exponent. Longer intervals reveal more of the tail and
reduce the dependence.\\

\noindent A robust-loss sensitivity analysis on a subsampled grid reproduced the same dependence on interval and
background treatment, including the crossing of unity. This reproduction attributes the effect to the observed portion
of the decay and not to a small number of large residuals. At $\beta_{leak}=1$ the kernel is exponential; below unity
the fractional loss slows with age, and above unity it accelerates. Moving the fitted exponent across one therefore
reverses the age dependence assigned to the same measurement (Figure~\ref{fig:7kernel}).\\

\noindent Variation between repeated measurements describes repeatability, and the uncertainty in a single fitted
parameter pair requires a separate calculation. A joint $95\,\%$ profile-likelihood region was therefore calculated
for each measurement, with amplitude and background profiled out and the sample count replaced by an autoregressive
effective size to account for correlated
residuals \cite{wilks1938large, bayley1946effective}. Each pointwise region contains the
$(\tau_{leak},\beta_{leak})$ pairs compatible with that measurement under the stated approximation.\\

\noindent The later measurements test the short fit directly: under the selected-interval-tail rule, the $30$\,s and
$226$\,s fits return $\beta_{leak}=1.07$ and $0.622$, respectively, and both exceed mean $R^2=0.99$ within their
own intervals. Beyond $30$\,s the short fit departs from the subsequently measured decay
(Figure~\ref{fig:7kernel}a), so its exponent above unity reflects incomplete observation and not a second
physical description of the complete relaxation.\\

\noindent An external check asks whether shortening the observation also increases the fitted exponent in independent
measurements. Five deposited decays from mesoporous-silica diffusive memristors last $19.9$\,s
\cite{zhang2024mesoporous}, and the comparison uses the complete interval and its first $30/226$, approximately
$2.64$\,s, which preserves the fractional observation used above. Fitting the background freely in both intervals keeps
the short fit free of any information from the later decay.\\

\section[Kernel Shape Changes Temporal Credit]{Kernel Shape Changes Temporal Credit}
\label{sec:7p2}

\noindent The fitted kernel weights each event by the eligibility that remains when reward arrives, and a threshold
derived from that kernel predicts the relative strength at which credit changes between an older and a newer event. A
role-swap task then tests whether the predicted ordering persists after repeated reward-gated updates, and the same task
compares the complete $226$\,s identification with the kernel inferred from its first $30$\,s.

\subsection[Credit Prediction and Role-Swap Test]{Credit Prediction and Role-Swap Test}
\label{sec:7p2p1}

\noindent The role-swap task separates event age from event identity: each trial contains a predictive event and a
competing event at fixed delays before reward. One input line carries each event, and both lines project to both action
neurons. Output spikes produced during the predictive event select the action, and while the competing event does not
enter that count, it deposits eligibility on the opposing pathway. Target labels vary across trials, so a pathway that
competes on one trial can become predictive on a later trial. These two events therefore compete for reward-gated updates
to future choices, not for the action already selected on the current trial.\\

\noindent Each action channel contains a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron with a $20$\,ms membrane time constant, a unit
firing threshold, and Gaussian membrane noise of scale $0.15$. Older and newer events are each $0.3$\,s spike trains
at $200$\,Hz. The two input lines and two action neurons form four plastic weights, each initialised at $0.5$ and
bounded within $[0,1.5]$. Selection takes the channel with the larger output spike count during the predictive event,
with ties broken at random, and the selected action then remains fixed until reward.\\

\noindent At each $5$\,ms step containing an input spike, the two synapses from the active line receive separate signed
drives: a simultaneous spike from the corresponding output neuron contributes $+1$, while its absence contributes
$-0.6$. Reward is $1$ when the selected action matches the target and $0$ otherwise, and at reward the prediction error
$R_n-b_n$ gates the eligibility on all four synapses. This running baseline $b_n$ starts at the chance rate,
$b_1=0.5$, and follows $b_{n+1}=b_n+0.02(R_n-b_n)$; eligibility is cleared between trials.\\

\noindent Signed drive, event duration, membrane noise, time step, and weight bounds are simulation choices. The
$+1$ and $-0.6$ contributions balance coincident potentiation against non-coincident depression, preventing unrelated
activity from accumulating only positive eligibility. One global learning rate of $10^{-3}$ is used throughout the
role-swap experiment, and Figure~\ref{fig:7task} shows the complete sequence from eligibility deposition to action,
reward, and weight update.\\

\begin{figure}[htbp!]
    \centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Chapter7/Figs/role_swap_task.pdf}}
    \caption[The closed-loop role-swap task.]{\emph{Schematic}: green denotes the predictor and grey the competitor.
    \textbf{(a)} The older and newer events exchange roles at fixed times. \textbf{(b)} Predictor-period output spikes
    select the action, while each input spike adds $+1$ or $-0.6$ to the corresponding eligibility.
    \textbf{(c)} At reward, $\delta_n=R_n-b_n$ gates the eligibility surviving on both pathways.
    \textbf{(d)} The role-swap effect $g$ compares $500$-trial mean reward between the two assignments.}
    \label{fig:7task}
\end{figure}

\noindent Dividing one reward between two events makes the ratio of their surviving eligibility the relevant quantity.
Each nominal delay is the age of an event's onset when reward arrives. Because deposition continues throughout the
$0.3$\,s event, the simulated eligibility sums contributions that are slightly younger than the nominal onset delay. A
point-event calculation predicts the ordering before the learner is run. Writing
$t_{\mathrm{newer}}<t_{\mathrm{older}}$ for the two delays, the threshold is
\begin{align}
    r^{\ast} &= \frac{q(t_{\mathrm{newer}})}{q(t_{\mathrm{older}})} \label{eq:7.6}
\end{align}
\noindent where $r^{\ast}$ is the older-to-newer deposit ratio that leaves equal eligibility from the two events at
reward. Since $q$ decreases with age, $r^{\ast}>1$: equal deposits favour the newer event, and the older event contributes
more only when its initial deposit is greater by more than $r^{\ast}$. A larger threshold therefore means stronger
relative weighting of the newer event, and integrating over the finite event duration changes the numerical value
slightly while preserving the ordering.\\

\noindent Both the kernel and the event delays enter the threshold, so it is a task-specific prediction, not an
intrinsic device credit window, and $\tau_{leak}$ alone cannot determine it. Kernels with different
$\beta_{leak}$ can share the same mean retention yet return different thresholds, assigning the same pair of deposits
to different events.\\

\noindent Five example kernels hold the mean retention at $23.0$\,s while varying $\beta_{leak}$ and solving for the
corresponding $\tau_{leak}$. Their thresholds at event delays of $1$ and $10$\,s span $1.332$ to $1.874$
(Figure~\ref{fig:7w}). At an older-to-newer deposit ratio of $1.60$, two kernels assign more surviving eligibility to
the newer event, and three assign more to the older event; equal mean retention therefore leaves temporal credit
undetermined. These five kernels provide an analytical example, and the learning comparison uses the calibrated
exponential defined below.\\

\begin{figure}[htbp!]
    \centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{Chapter7/Figs/credit_threshold_witness.png}}
    \caption[Equal mean retention does not fix temporal credit.]{Five kernels share
    $\langle t\rangle=23.0$\,s while $\beta_{leak}$ varies and $\tau_{leak}$ is adjusted accordingly. Their thresholds
    at event delays of $1$ and $10$\,s span the marked range. At a deposit ratio of $1.60$, two kernels favour the newer
    event, and three favour the older event.}
    \label{fig:7w}
\end{figure}

\noindent Summarising the learning outcome takes two levels: within one kernel, the role-swap effect $g$ is the
difference in $500$-trial mean reward between the assignment with the newer predictor and the assignment with the older
predictor. A second quantity, the contrast $\Delta g$, compares that role-swap effect between the
SiO$_x$ kernel and the exponential,
\begin{align}
    g(q) &= \bar{R}_{\mathrm{newer}}(q) - \bar{R}_{\mathrm{older}}(q), \qquad
    \Delta g = g(q_{\mathrm{SiO}_x}) - g(q_{\exp}). \label{eq:7.6b}
\end{align}
\noindent A positive $g$ means that mean reward is higher when the newer event is predictive. A positive $\Delta g$
means that this newer-predictor advantage is larger with the SiO$_x$ kernel; a negative value means that it
is larger with the exponential. These three quantities therefore form a sequence: $r^{\ast}$ predicts the relative
weighting before learning, $g$ measures the role-swap outcome within one kernel, and $\Delta g$ compares the two
kernels.\\

\noindent The control kernel is the canonical single-timescale exponential
$q_{\exp}(\ell)=\exp(-\ell/\tau_{r^{\ast}})$, whose time constant is chosen to approximate the
SiO$_x$ thresholds jointly at the calibration timings $(1,10)$ and $(3,20)$\,s, giving
$\tau_{r^{\ast}}=19.86$\,s. One time constant cannot reproduce both thresholds exactly: the control returns $1.573$
against $1.765$ at $(1,10)$\,s, and $2.354$ against $2.215$ at $(3,20)$\,s. This control therefore tests whether an
exponential tuned at known delays preserves the SiO$_x$ ordering at new delays.\\

\noindent Equation~\ref{eq:7.6} selected the evaluation timings, $(1.5,11)$ and $(3.5,19)$\,s, on opposite
sides of the predicted crossover, and both timings were excluded from control calibration and learning-rate selection.
Because the exponential was fixed before these simulations, a change in the sign of $\Delta g$ was a prospective
prediction.\\

\noindent Different kernels can also produce different total update magnitudes, which would confound a comparison of
temporal weighting. Each kernel therefore received one fixed normalisation factor, estimated from $200$ balanced trials
at each calibration timing with prescribed actions and learning disabled, and these trials supplied the unscaled signal
$(R-b)e(t_R)$. A common learning rate was then chosen as the smallest prespecified value at which both kernels learned
without saturating their weights, and the factors and that learning rate remained fixed for evaluation.\\

\noindent The measurement and learning analyses use different evidence units: Section~\ref{sec:7p1} fits nine repeated
measurements, while the learner uses one aggregate kernel formed from their mean parameter values.
Measurement-to-measurement variation is therefore not propagated through the learning simulations. Replication on the
learning side comes from $16$ paired simulations at each evaluation timing, with the two kernels receiving identical
target labels and input spike trains within each pair.\\

\noindent An exact paired sign-flip test on the within-pair difference
$\Delta g(1.5,11)-\Delta g(3.5,19)$ tests the predicted reversal
\cite{ernst2004permutation}. This planned contrast asks whether the relative ordering of the two kernels changes with
timing. The three truncation contrasts reported below address a separate comparison and are adjusted together using
Holm's procedure \cite{holm1979simple}.

\label{sec:7p2p2}

\noindent Thresholds evaluated at the two timings predict opposite kernel orderings. At $(1.5,11)$\,s, the
SiO$_x$ kernel has the larger threshold, $1.755$ against $1.613$, and therefore predicts $\Delta g>0$.
At $(3.5,19)$\,s, the exponential has the larger threshold, $2.182$ against $2.062$, and predicts $\Delta g<0$.
This prediction concerns which kernel produces the larger newer-predictor advantage; it does not require either kernel
to favour the older event in absolute terms.\\

\noindent Both kernels produced positive $g$ at both timings, so mean reward was higher when the newer event served as
the predictor (Figure~\ref{fig:7l}a). This advantage changed in size between kernels as predicted: mean
$\Delta g$ was $+0.0133\pm0.0082$ at $(1.5,11)$\,s and $-0.0356\pm0.0094$ at $(3.5,19)$\,s, with standard errors over
the $16$ paired simulations (Figure~\ref{fig:7l}b). Their planned within-pair difference was
$+0.0489\pm0.0123$, with $p=0.00269$, supporting the predicted change in kernel ordering
(Table~\ref{table:7credit}).\\

\noindent In the truncation comparison, $q_{30}$ denotes the kernel fitted over the first $30$\,s and $q_{226}$ the
kernel fitted over the complete $226$\,s. Both fits take the background from the final tenth of their selected interval,
so the observation length is the only changed analysis choice. The comparison tests the learning consequence of
identifying the same relaxation before its slow tail has been observed.\\

    \centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,trim=0 0 0 174,clip]{Chapter7/Figs/kernel_reliability_lower.pdf}}

    \label{fig:7trunc}

\noindent Mapping each profile-likelihood region through Equation~\ref{eq:7.6} gives an interval for the credit
threshold. At $(3,20)$\,s, the intervals from the complete and truncated fits do not overlap in eight of the nine
measurements (Figure~\ref{fig:7trunc}a). This uncertainty analysis estimates the background within each fitting interval,
whereas the learning comparison fixes it from the interval tail. The two analyses therefore ask related but distinct
questions: whether the fitted thresholds are distinguishable, and whether the declared truncation convention changes
learning.\\

\noindent Replacing $q_{226}$ with $q_{30}$ increased the newer-predictor advantage at both evaluation timings.
$\Delta g_{\mathrm{trunc}}=g(q_{30})-g(q_{226})$ was $+0.0636\pm0.0077$ at $(1.5,11)$\,s and
$+0.0951\pm0.0110$ at $(3.5,19)$\,s (Figure~\ref{fig:7trunc}b), and all $16$ paired values were positive at both
timings. Between timings, the truncation effect differed by $-0.0315\pm0.0130$, showing that the shift was larger at
$(3.5,19)$\,s (Table~\ref{table:7credit}). Truncating the observation therefore changed both the fitted age dependence
and the learned distribution of temporal credit.\\

\noindent The threshold compares two surviving deposits at one reward, while $g$ emerges after $500$ trials of feedback
between weight updates and later choices. Agreement between the predicted and learned ordering therefore shows that the
kernel's relative temporal weighting survives the closed loop. The SiO$_x$ kernel loses a decreasing
fraction of its surviving state as it ages, whereas the exponential loses a constant fraction over equal intervals, so
their ordering changes with event timing, and neither kernel is uniformly larger.\\

\noindent The truncation comparison addresses identification rather than kernel superiority: fitting only the early
decay raises the inferred exponent, places more relative weight on newer events, and shifts the closed-loop learning
outcome in the same direction. These results use one material, one aggregate kernel per fit, and a small two-action
task. They establish that the declared measurement and fitting choices can alter learned temporal credit, without
showing an advantage over a separately tuned generic trace or the same effect in another device family.

\section[From Local Eligibility to Learning Systems]{From Local Eligibility to Learning Systems}
\label{sec:7p3}

\noindent Within one plastic layer, the role-swap task isolates temporal credit; a multilayer network adds spatial
credit, because the reward signal must also distinguish the hidden neurons and layers that contributed to the action.
The learning rule below addresses that requirement, using direct feedback alignment to distribute the
learning signal without transporting the forward weights, and a two-layer exclusive-or task then tests whether the
measured decay still supports learning once credit must be divided between layers.

\subsection[Coincidence Drive and Projected Credit]{Coincidence Drive and Projected Credit}
\label{sec:7p3p1}

\noindent Eligibility must distinguish reward-correlated activity from input spikes that fail to drive the
postsynaptic neuron. Section~\ref{sec:7p2p1} introduced a signed drive for that purpose, giving a positive contribution
to same-step pre- and postsynaptic spikes and a smaller negative contribution when only the presynaptic spike occurs
\cite{izhikevich2007solving}. In indexed form, with $\lambda_d=0.6$, the drive is
\begin{align}
    d_{ij}(t) = x^{pre}_i(t) \left[ \mathbf{1}\!\left( x^{post}_j(t) \right) - \lambda_d \left( 1 - \mathbf{1}\!\left( x^{post}_j(t) \right) \right) \right], \label{eq:7.7}
\end{align}
\noindent where $x^{pre}_i$ and $x^{post}_j$ are the pre- and postsynaptic spike trains, and
$\mathbf{1}(x^{post}_j)$ indicates a postsynaptic spike in the same $5$\,ms step, within which the rule does not resolve
spike order. Reward-correlated input produces more coincidences and a net-positive eligibility, while uncorrelated
input produces more negative contributions. This signed drive supplies selectivity between the two cases without
normalising the weights.\\

\noindent The postsynaptic units are \abbrev{LIF} neurons on this task's own time step, with Gaussian membrane
noise providing exploration. Their spikes supply the coincidence term in Equation~\ref{eq:7.7}, the measured kernel
decays the resulting eligibility, and the broadcast prediction error commits the surviving value through
Equation~\ref{eq:7.3}. Figure~\ref{fig:7b} translates this sequence into a proposed physical process at one synapse, of
which only the post-drive decay has been measured; coincidence-dependent deposition and reward-gated programming remain
circuit functions.\\

    \label{fig:7n}

\noindent In the proposed crossbar mapping, state inputs drive the rows, action neurons read the bitline currents, and
each signed weight is stored as the differential conductance pair $(G^{+}_{ij},G^{-}_{ij})$ of
Equation~\ref{eq:6.33}. A separate volatile state at the same logical crosspoint carries eligibility
(Figure~\ref{fig:7n}). Discretising
Equation~\ref{eq:7.3} gives the complementary conductance update
\begin{align}
    \Delta G^{\pm}_{ij} = \pm\, \eta\, \big( R - b \big)\, e_{ij}(t_R), \label{eq:7.10}
\end{align}
\noindent applied as potentiation and depression pulses. This mapping avoids a transposed weight matrix and a
per-synapse error signal \cite{bellec2020solution,zhou2022memprop}, but it still requires coincidence generation,
eligibility sensing, reward-gated programming, and selective access to each conductance pair. Material relaxation could
remove the need to store and numerically decay one eligibility value per synapse. Whether that saving outweighs the
peripheral circuitry requires a fabricated implementation and is not resolved by the present simulation.\\

\noindent A single broadcast prediction error gives hidden synapses no information about which hidden neuron
contributed to the chosen action. Direct feedback alignment (\abbrevdef{DFA}) instead projects the output learning
signal through a fixed random matrix \cite{lillicrap2016random,nokland2016direct}; since this matrix is independent of
the forward weights, the update avoids weight transport. Eligibility remains synapse-local, while all synapses entering
one hidden neuron share its projected signal; the resulting credit is neuron-local, not fully synapse-specific.\\

\noindent Direct feedback alignment supplies spatial credit but does not regulate hidden-neuron activity, so a separate
homeostatic term adjusts each hidden neuron's incoming weights according to its own firing-rate estimate and contains no
reward information. The hidden-layer update combines the projected reward term with this local stabiliser,
\begin{align}
    \Delta w_{ij} = \underbrace{\eta\,(R-b)\,[B\mathbf{L}]_j\, e_{ij}(t_R)}_{\text{per-neuron credit (\abbrev{DFA})}} \;+\; \underbrace{\eta_h\,(\rho^{\ast} - \bar{\rho}_j)\, w_{ij}}_{\text{local homeostasis}}, \label{eq:7.11}
\end{align}
\noindent where $[B\mathbf{L}]_j$ is the fixed-random projection onto hidden neuron $j$, $\bar{\rho}_j$ is that
neuron's firing-rate estimate, $\rho^{\ast}$ is the shared target, and $\eta_h$ is the homeostatic rate. Each action
neuron contributes one component to the output learning signal,
$L_a=\mathbf{1}(a=a_{\mathrm{chosen}})-\pi_a$, with $\pi$ obtained from a softmax over output spike counts.
Output-layer synapses retain Equation~\ref{eq:7.3} and hidden-layer synapses use Equation~\ref{eq:7.11}, so the measured
decay supplies eligibility in both layers, while the homeostatic target, gain, and weight dependence remain modelling
choices.

\subsection[Multilayer Learning and the Homeostatic Requirement]{Multilayer Learning and the Homeostatic
Requirement}
\label{sec:7p3p2}

\noindent A two-layer exclusive-or (XOR) contextual bandit tests temporal and spatial credit together. Two binary
features are encoded one-hot onto four input lines, with exactly two active lines on every trial. Action $1$ is correct
when the features differ, and action $0$ when they agree. Because XOR is not linearly separable
\cite{rumelhart1986learning}, the learner contains eight hidden \abbrev{LIF} neurons and two action neurons, connected by
$4\times8$ and $8\times2$ weight matrices, both of which carry eligibility through the measured kernel.\\

\noindent The predictive cue precedes reward, while an independent $0.3$\,s competing spike train appears closer to
reward on randomly chosen cue lines. This competing input contains no information about the correct action, so a learner
that follows recency alone credits the wrong pathway. Inputs use $200$\,Hz spike trains on the $5$\,ms step, and each
condition comprises $20$ paired simulation runs of $2{,}000$ trials, with each pair sharing its cue sequence, membrane
noise, feedback matrix, and weight initialisation. Final reward rate is the mean over the last $100$ trials.\\

\noindent The simulations retain $\lambda_d=0.6$, membrane-noise scale $0.15$, and weight bounds $[0,1.5]$. Each hidden
neuron also tracks its firing rate over a $200$\,s window against the shared target $\rho^{\ast}=0.35$, and the
feedback matrix
$\mathbf{B}$ is drawn once for each simulation and then held fixed. These settings are common to every condition, and
no parameter is selected using the reported final reward rate.\\

\noindent Without homeostasis, several simulations converged on one action and failed to solve the XOR task. Adding the
local firing-rate term prevented individual hidden neurons from dominating the response and raised final reward rate
from $0.436$ to $0.876$ over $20$ paired runs. The mean paired increase was $+0.4402$, with an exact sign-flip
$p<0.0001$. This dependence on activity regulation is consistent with the need to stabilise associative plasticity
\cite{abbott2000taming,zenke2013homeostasis,zenke2015orchestrated}, and the present evidence for that stabilisation
extends only to this learner and task.\\

\noindent A zero-eligibility control retained the architecture and homeostatic term while preventing reward from using
the measured decay. Its final reward rate was $0.536$, giving a paired difference of $+0.3403$ from the full learner,
again with $p<0.0001$ (Figure~\ref{fig:7m}), so homeostasis on its own does not account for the learned performance.
These two terms are held as separate eligibility and homeostatic states in the simulation, and autonomous physical
implementation of both in one device has not been demonstrated.\\

    \caption[Homeostasis stabilises multilayer local credit.]{\emph{Simulated}: the two-layer XOR learner with a
    competing event and the measured decay kernel. \textbf{(a)} Final reward rate against chance.
    \textbf{(b)} Paired effects of adding homeostasis and restoring eligibility. Bars are means over $20$ paired runs,

\noindent This experiment moves the measured decay out of the single-layer setting. It places the kernel within a
weight-transport-free learning rule and shows that the tested network also needs an independent local stabiliser to
reach the task. The learner remains a simulation throughout, so it tests system compatibility without observing
learning in biological tissue.

\section[Summary]{Summary}

\noindent The normalised \abbrev{ITO} relaxation supplies the decay kernel of a canonical three-factor learning rule,
and its measured exponent gives a dispersive extension of the standard exponential eligibility equation. This
identification holds for the complete measurement, while the $30$\,s fit departs from the later measured tail.
Observation length and background-current treatment moved the fitted exponent across the exponential value of unity,
which reverses the age dependence assigned to one measurement and shifts the temporal credit predicted from it. A device
proposed for this role must therefore be reported by its complete decay profile and measurement conditions, not by one
retention time.\\

\noindent In the learning simulations, the role-swap learner preserved the kernel's predicted timing-dependent ordering,
and the truncated identification shifted learned credit towards newer events. That same measured decay supported a
two-layer learner without weight transport when combined with a separate local stabiliser. Within the thesis's
definition of biorealistic learning, the material provides the fading local state,
while simulated circuitry provides activity-dependent deposition, reward gating, and persistent weight updates. An
integrated device implementation remains outside the evidence presented here.
