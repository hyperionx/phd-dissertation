\chapter{Fabrication and Characterisation Methodologies}\label{ch:fab}

\noindent With the device physics of Chapter~\ref{ch:foundations} in place, this chapter grounds it in fabricated hardware. It details how the silicon-rich \abbrev{SiOx} memristors are made and measured. It characterises their resistive switching across unipolar, bipolar, multilevel, and threshold modes. It fits the measured current--voltage data to identify trap-assisted tunnelling as the dominant transport channel. The result is the empirical foundation on which the modelling and neuromorphic chapters that follow depend: the stable, state-dependent conductance changes and their underlying mechanism. \\

\noindent The devices were fabricated within the UCL Electronic Materials and Devices group. The full process is described here for completeness, although group members other than the author carried out some of the fabrication steps. The electrical characterisation and the conduction-mechanism fitting are the author's own.

\section[Fabrication and Measurement]{Fabrication and Measurement}

\noindent This section sets out the device stack and geometry, the sputter-deposition process that produces it, and the electrical probing setup used for every measurement reported in the thesis.

\subsection[Material Stack and Geometry]{Material Stack and Geometry}
\label{sec:matstack}

\noindent The device is a metal-insulator-metal (\abbrev{MIM}) structure fabricated on a silicon wafer. It comprises a molybdenum bottom electrode, a silicon oxide (SiO$_x$) switching layer, and a gold top contact with a thin titanium adhesion layer. Commercially pre-oxidised wafers bear a thick thermally grown silicon dioxide layer, which electrically isolates the bottom electrode from the underlying silicon substrate. Sputtering deposits the molybdenum bottom electrode and SiO$_x$ thin film unpatterned across the sample. A contact mask then patterns the gold top contacts into squares with sides of $200\mu m$ to $800\mu m$ during deposition (Figure \ref{fig:3a}), avoiding photolithography. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/device_structure.png}
    \caption[Device Structure]{Contact (shadow) mask (left) and the top electrical contacts (right), patterned as square pads of $200\times200$, $400\times400$, $600\times600$, and $800\times800$\,$\mu$m.}
    \label{fig:3a}
\end{figure}

\noindent A titanium buffer layer between the top metal contact and the oxide improves adhesion. This layer is non-ideal: by scavenging oxygen at the interface it can introduce additional oxygen-vacancy defects in the oxide. However, electron energy loss spectroscopy (\abbrevdef{EELS}) and transmission electron microscopy (\abbrevdef{TEM}) studies show no evidence of titanium interface migration in these devices \cite{mehonic2017intrinsic}.\\

\noindent Gold forms the top contact. Although gold can diffuse into amorphous SiO$_x$, this occurs only at elevated temperatures (for example at $390^{\circ}$C over four hours, with no migration below $370^{\circ}$C \cite{madams1974migration}). The present devices undergo no such thermal treatment, so gold diffusion through the film is not a concern here. Gold therefore behaves as an inert electrode, as it does in electrochemical metallisation cells (a form of conductive-bridge \abbrev{RRAM}, \abbrevdef{CBRAM}). There it is paired with an active electrode, and only the active species migrates \cite{kozicki2016electrochemical}.\\

\noindent Prior studies on high-performance resistance switching memory guided this asymmetric pairing of a reactive molybdenum bottom electrode with an inert gold top contact. As Cox et al.~\cite{cox2021nanoscale} show, electrode selection directly influences conductive filament formation, stability, oxygen exchange dynamics, and device endurance. The asymmetry employed here enables the controlled redox reactions at the active electrode that reliable resistive switching requires. A profilometer measures layer thickness. The molybdenum bottom electrode is 300 nm thick to ensure high conductivity, the SiO$_x$ thin film is 35 nm thick, and the gold top contact is 110 nm thick. Two variants of this stack are studied (Figure \ref{fig:3stack}). The first is a symmetric Mo/SiO$_x$/Au device, which exhibits unipolar switching. The second is an asymmetric Mo/SiO$_x$/Ti/Au device, in which a thin (3 nm) titanium adhesion layer beneath the gold top contact yields bipolar switching.\\

    \caption[Cross-section of the MIM device stacks.]{Cross-sections of the two metal--insulator--metal device stacks studied in this chapter: (a) the symmetric Mo/SiO$_x$/Au stack used for unipolar switching and (b) the asymmetric Mo/SiO$_x$/Ti/Au stack used for bipolar switching, with the thin titanium adhesion layer between the oxide and the gold top contact. Layer thicknesses are as labelled; the vertical scale is compressed so that the thin titanium and oxide layers remain visible, so the schematic is not to scale.}

\subsection[Sputter Deposition Process]{Sputter Deposition Process}

\noindent Radio-frequency (\abbrev{RF}) sputtering, a physical vapour deposition process, deposited all device layers. Deposition occurs at low pressure in an inert gas, where plasma bombards the target material and expels atoms. These expelled atoms reach the sample along a direct ballistic path or by a random walk, depending on chamber gas pressure. Higher pressure increases collisions and random walks, whereas lower pressure favours a direct ballistic trajectory. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/rf_sputtering.png}
    \caption[RF Sputtering]{Basic configuration for depositing thin films by \abbrev{RF} sputtering. A strong radio-frequency electric field ionises argon gas to produce a plasma. High-energy collisions between the ionised argon and the target eject target atoms with high kinetic energy. These atoms traverse the chamber and deposit onto the sample over time, forming an amorphous thin film.}
    \label{fig:3b}
\end{figure}

\noindent Figure \ref{fig:3b} illustrates a simplified sputtering system. The sample connects to the \abbrev{RF} power source anode, the target sits before the cathode, and the sputtering gas is injected into the chamber. The plasma consists of positively charged argon ions, which the negatively charged cathode (target) attracts. High-energy collisions between these ions and the target surface eject target atoms.\\

\noindent The solid target provides the deposition material, and a strong electric field applied to the sputtering gas (argon) creates the plasma. The target's electrical properties dictate the choice between direct-current (\abbrevdef{DC}) and alternating-current (\abbrevdef{AC}) fields. Conductive targets, such as metals, require only a \abbrev{DC} field. For insulating targets like $SiO_2$, however, a \abbrev{DC} field causes charge buildup that quenches the plasma. An \abbrev{AC} field, oscillating at radio frequency (typically 13.56 MHz), alternates polarity to prevent charge accumulation and sustain continuous sputtering of dielectrics. Stable plasma and efficient deposition therefore follow for both conductive and insulating targets.\\

\noindent Reactive sputtering introduces a reactive gas such as oxygen alongside argon, so the target reacts to form an oxide film on the substrate. This gives precise control over film composition and stoichiometry. Sputtering often yields amorphous films with sub-stoichiometric oxides. The devices' SiOx oxide has a stoichiometry of $SiO_{1.9}$, and the root mean square (\abbrevdef{RMS}) roughness of the underlying molybdenum layer (0.9 to 1.5 nm) sets the film's roughness \cite{kenyon2019interplay}.\\


\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/stylus_profilometer.png}
    \caption[Stylus profilometer for thin-film thickness measurement.]{Schematic of a contact stylus profilometer (Dektak). A weighted diamond stylus is dragged laterally across the sample at constant force; its vertical deflection is transduced by a linear variable differential transformer (\abbrevdef{LVDT}) and recorded against position. Masking part of the substrate during deposition leaves a step at the film edge, whose height the stylus reads off directly as the film thickness. This is distinct from an atomic force microscope (\abbrevdef{AFM}), which uses a microcantilever and optical-lever feedback for nanometre-scale surface imaging.}
    \label{fig:3c}
\end{figure}

\noindent Each SiOx sputtering run includes two Si substrate specimens for thickness measurement. A patterned photoresist masks a region of the first specimen. Removing it after deposition leaves a step at the SiOx/Si interface, which a Dektak XT contact profilometer (Figure \ref{fig:3c}) profiles to a few nanometres' resolution. Ellipsometry measures the second specimen. It infers film thickness from changes in the polarisation of reflected light, independently verifying the deposited layer.

\subsection[Electrical Probing Setup]{Electrical Probing Setup}

\noindent Current magnitude is the primary measured quantity, reporting on oxide-layer conductivity and interface barrier heights. The challenge is to minimise measurement artefacts such as probe contact resistance. The device electrodes must first be connected, either by wire bonding inside a chip carrier or by direct probing with micromanipulators. Direct probing with tungsten probes was adopted, because the small exposed electrode pads and thin oxide layers make the devices vulnerable to wire-bonding damage. Ultrasonic bonding melts gold or aluminium wire onto the contact under pressure, which can compress the internal layers and short the electrodes. The probe tip is lowered carefully, with a low test voltage applied to detect contact.\\

\noindent Contact is detected by monitoring the current: before contact no current flows, and upon contact the applied voltage drives a measurable current of matching polarity. The gentle approach and low test voltage prevent damage to the sample.\\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/kelvin_four_wire_measurement.png}
    \caption[Two-wire versus four-wire (Kelvin) measurement.]{Two-wire versus four-wire (Kelvin) measurement. (a) In the two-wire configuration the same pair of leads both sources the current and senses the voltage, so the measured voltage includes the drop across the contact resistance $R_c$ at each probe, $V_{meas} = I\,(R_{dev} + 2R_c)$. (b) In the four-wire (Kelvin) configuration a separate force pair carries the current while a high-impedance voltmeter senses the voltage on a second pair of leads tapped at the device terminals; negligible current flows in the sense leads, so the drop across their contact resistance is negligible and $V_{meas} \approx I\,R_{dev}$.}
    \label{fig:3d}
\end{figure}

\noindent The choice between 2-wire and 4-wire measurement (Figure \ref{fig:3d}) is set by sample conductivity. The 2-wire method places a voltage source, ammeter, and device in series and applies Ohm's law to the set voltage and total current. However, it assumes all resistance resides in the device, neglecting the wire, instrument, and contact resistances in series with it. When device resistance dominates these parasitics the method is valid. For low-resistance devices a 4-wire (Kelvin) measurement is needed, in which two probes force a constant current while two high-impedance sense probes read the voltage drop, so negligible current flows through the sensing contacts. The studied devices are high-resistance (kilo- to mega-ohms), so the parasitics are negligible and 2-wire measurement suffices, with a picoammeter to reach the sub-nanoamp range. \\

\noindent A digital multimeter (\abbrevdef{DMM}) ammeter reads the voltage drop across a shunt resistor. This suffices for milliamp currents but fails at smaller ones, where the larger shunt then needed adds thermal noise and a voltage burden that grows comparable to the applied voltage. Picoammeters avoid this by using a transimpedance amplifier to convert input current to voltage. \\


\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/transimpedance_amplifier.png}
    \caption[Transimpedance amplifier circuit.]{Transimpedance amplifier circuit. The input current ($I_{in}$) is amplified and converted into an output voltage ($V_{out}$) via the op-amp. The gain is set by the feedback resistor ($R_f$). The voltage/current ratio ($V_{out}/I_{in}$) equals the negative of the measured resistance ($-R_f = V_{out} / I_{in}$).}
    \label{fig:3e}
\end{figure}

\noindent In the transimpedance amplifier (Figure \ref{fig:3e}), grounding the non-inverting input holds the inverting input at a virtual ground. The op-amp then drives the input current through the feedback resistor $R_f$ to give $V_{out} = -R_f \cdot I_{in}$. With the input held at virtual ground no series shunt is needed. This removes the voltage burden that limits a \abbrev{DMM} and enables the small-current measurements required here. The Keithley 6430 sourcemeter, combining a picoammeter with a low-noise voltage source, performed the current--time measurements and current--voltage sweeps. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/spike_train_setup.png}
    \caption[Experimental setup of spike-train measurements.]{Experimental setup of spike-train measurements. An arbitrary signal generator applies the voltage spike train $V(t)$ across the memristive device under test (DUT). Because the Stanford Research Systems SR570 current preamplifier holds its input at a virtual ground ($\approx 0$\,V), essentially the full applied voltage drops across the device, and the resulting through-current $I_{in}$ is converted by the SR570 to an output voltage $V_{out}$ captured by the oscilloscope. All instruments share a common ground return.}
    \label{fig:3f}
\end{figure}

\noindent The Keithley's limited sampling frequency means that complex voltage transients (pulses, spike trains) require an arbitrary signal generator. In this arrangement (Figure \ref{fig:3f}), a series Stanford Research Systems SR570 current preamplifier amplifies the device current, and an oscilloscope (Rigol DS4024) captures the output.

\section[Resistive Switching Behaviour]{Resistive Switching Behaviour}

\noindent Resistive switching is a reversible, non-volatile change in resistance induced by electrical stimuli. A large resistance difference distinguishes the high resistance state (\abbrev{HRS}, "OFF") from the low resistance state (\abbrev{LRS}, "ON"). "Set" transitions from \abbrev{HRS} to \abbrev{LRS}, and "Reset" transitions back. Two sample sets are studied: symmetric Mo/SiO$_x$/Au devices, which exhibit unipolar switching, and asymmetric Mo/SiO$_x$/Ti/Au devices, which exhibit bipolar switching. The fundamental I-V characteristics of both sets were measured across all electrode pad sizes using the dual-sweep functionality of a Keithley 4200-SCS with two 10\,$\mu$m diameter tungsten tip probes. \\

\noindent Both sample sets must first be electroformed. This one-time preconditioning step is common to unipolar and bipolar operation, and it converts the pristine, high-resistance film into a reversibly switchable state. Electroforming is a controlled, current-limited electrical breakdown. The source-measure unit's current compliance bounds the breakdown to preserve switching functionality, although a slow analyser response can produce transient overshoot spikes. It is applied once to each fresh device, and the resulting structural change is permanent, so the pristine state is never recovered. Only the forming sweep amplitude and polarity differ between the two device types, as shown for the unipolar case in Figure \ref{fig:3g} and the bipolar case in Figure \ref{fig:3l}.

\subsection[Unipolar Switching]{Unipolar Switching}

\noindent The symmetric Mo/SiO$_x$/Au devices are electroformed under a positive voltage sweep (Figure \ref{fig:3g}), after which the device switches reversibly between two stable states. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/electroforming_double_sweep.png}
    \caption[Initial electroforming step via a double sweep curve.]{Initial electroforming step via a double sweep curve. Double sweeping refers to applying a voltage sweep in both forward and reverse directions to capture the device's switching behaviour during electroforming.}
    \label{fig:3g}
\end{figure}

\noindent For these symmetric devices, forming proceeds by programming the Keithley-SCS to sweep up to 18\,V (Figure \ref{fig:3g}). Current peaks and an unstable I-V curve appear during the sweep, and at approximately 14\,V the device abruptly switches to \abbrev{LRS}. Subsequent sweeps are smoother. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/unipolar_nonvolatile_switching.png}
    \caption[Non-volatile switching behaviour for unipolar device under current compliance.]{Non-volatile switching behaviour for a unipolar device. Current compliance is applied only during Set to limit the current and preserve switching functionality.}
    \label{fig:3h}
\end{figure}

\noindent The structural change during forming, likely oxygen removal creating vacancies, drives the conductance change. The resulting \abbrev{LRS} is stable and non-volatile. Figure \ref{fig:3h} shows the unipolar switching mechanism in symmetrical \abbrev{MIM} devices. The blue plot marks the Set process (Off to On) at a threshold of approximately 2.3\,V, and a 3\,V sweep with 3\,mA compliance suffices to switch. Reducing the voltage below the threshold does not reverse the state \cite{mehonic2016silicon}.\\

\noindent Reset requires a critical current. Unlike the voltage-triggered Set, it depends on exceeding a current level, and the orange plot shows the Reset current to be an order of magnitude greater than the Set compliance. This current induces localised Joule heating that ruptures the conductive filament and restores \abbrev{HRS}. Without current compliance, hard breakdown or multiple transitions may occur. The switching sequence is repeatable. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/set_reset_sweeps.png}
    \caption[Observation of the Set and Reset process under forward and reverse sweeps.]{Observation of the Set and Reset process under forward and reverse sweeps.}
    \label{fig:3i}
\end{figure}

\noindent \abbrev{HRS} conductance lies between that of the \abbrev{LRS} and the pristine state. Transitions are abrupt and independent of the sweeping parameters. An elevated electric field sets the device (\abbrev{LRS}), whereas high Joule heating resets it (\abbrev{HRS}). Figure \ref{fig:3i} shows an alternative unipolar mode in which the device sets at a lower voltage ($\sim$1.15\,V) than it resets ($\sim$2.85\,V). This mode requires no current compliance. Reset occurs once the current exceeds the Joule-heating threshold and ruptures the filament, so switching is self-limiting. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/unipolar_cycling_stress.png}
    \caption[Cycling stress test for unipolar device.]{Cycling stress test for unipolar device.}
    \label{fig:3j}
\end{figure}

\noindent Figure \ref{fig:3j} illustrates unipolar device cycling under stress. The blue spikes are positive bias voltage pulses. A 100 ns, 4.5 V pulse sets the device, and a longer 2 ms, 2.5 V pulse resets it to accommodate Joule heating. A 1 ms, 0.7 V reading pulse follows each set or reset. The orange plot shows the read resistances separating into two well-defined bands spanning roughly two orders of magnitude, the \abbrev{LRS} reading near 50\,k$\Omega$ and the \abbrev{HRS} near 3\,M$\Omega$ (a median \abbrev{HRS}/\abbrev{LRS} ratio of about 60, with individual cycles reaching beyond 200). \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/unipolar_electrode_size.png}
    \caption[Switching in unipolar devices across different electrode sizes.]{Switching in unipolar devices across different electrode sizes.}
    \label{fig:3k}
\end{figure}

\noindent Unipolar switching is independent of electrode size. Figure \ref{fig:3k} shows comparable switching for contacts from 200 x 200 $\mu m$ to 800 x 800 $\mu m$: devices consistently switch at ~3.5 V (3 mA compliance) and reset at ~5 mA.

\subsection[Bipolar Switching]{Bipolar Switching}

\noindent The asymmetric Mo/SiO$_x$/Ti/Au devices, whose stack is shown in Figure \ref{fig:3stack}(b), yielded bipolar switching. They undergo the same one-time electroforming step described above, but under a negative dual voltage sweep to $-10$\,V at 0.1\,mA compliance (Figure \ref{fig:3l}). The sample spikes unstably as it transitions from the pristine \abbrev{HRS} to \abbrev{LRS} \cite{mehonic2012resistive}.

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/bipolar_electroforming.png}
    \caption[Electroforming step for the bipolar sample via a double sweep curve.]{Electroforming step for the bipolar sample via a double sweep curve.}
    \label{fig:3l}
\end{figure}


\noindent After electroforming, the device transitions reliably between stable states. Figure \ref{fig:3m} illustrates switching by opposite-polarity stimuli: a negative voltage sweep to $-2$\,V sets the device. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/bipolar_switching_asymmetric.png}
    \caption[Observation of bipolar switching in an asymmetric device.]{Observation of bipolar switching in an asymmetric device with $-2$\,V Set and $+2$\,V Reset sweeps.}
    \label{fig:3m}
\end{figure}

\noindent The 100 mA compliance used here is a deliberately high ceiling, chosen to display a clear transition with two orders of magnitude of conductance change rather than for routine operation. At this current the device endures only a few tens of cycles before irreversible breakdown (Figure \ref{fig:3n}), so endurance testing uses a much lower compliance, trading conductance contrast against device lifespan. A positive 2\,V dual sweep resets the device. This intrinsic bipolar switching belongs to the valence change mechanism class. Electroforming causes local reduction, forming a conductive pathway of oxygen vacancies, and an applied electric field then drives oxygen ion migration \cite{waser2007redox}. Local redox at the filament-electrode interface likely defines the resistance state by modulating the effective tunnelling barrier height \cite{waser2009redox}. \\

    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/bipolar_cycling_stress.png}

Over repeated cycling, the \abbrev{LRS} gradually converges to a narrower, lower resistance range (middle), indicating that the conductive filament strengthens and stabilises with cycling. Eventually, after extensive cycling, the device undergoes hard breakdown (bottom), where the resistance irreversibly increases and the device fails to switch, marking the end of operational endurance.}
    \label{fig:3n}

\noindent Suitable voltage pulses drive the transitions reliably. Figure \ref{fig:3n} shows switching under $-2$\,V Set and $+2$\,V Reset pulses, each followed by five $\pm0.1$\,V reading pulses chosen to measure the resistance without triggering switching. Here the \abbrev{HRS} is $\sim$300\,$\Omega$ and the \abbrev{LRS} $\sim$100\,$\Omega$. In a separate long-duration endurance run (not plotted here), the device sustained over 4500 cycles at 10 mA compliance, the \abbrev{LRS} converging from 200\,$\Omega$ towards 150\,$\Omega$. At 100 mA compliance it failed after $\sim$40 cycles, locking into a 200\,k$\Omega$ \abbrev{HRS}. \\


\noindent Figure \ref{fig:3o} demonstrates bipolar switching across contact sizes (200 x 200$\mu m$ to 800 x 800$\mu m$). Set sweeps used $-2$\,V (5\,mA compliance) and reset used $+2$\,V (100\,mA compliance). The switching voltages and contrast ratios vary, suggesting a need for statistical analysis, but consistent switching confirms that the mechanism is largely independent of contact area.

\subsection[Multilevel and Threshold Modes]{Multilevel and Threshold Modes}

\noindent Reliable switching occurs under specific conditions. The \abbrev{LRS} current does not scale with contact area, indicating filamentary transport. Some devices nonetheless switch gradually and at multiple levels, as parallel conductive filaments open multiple independent switching channels and yield gradual I-V behaviour. Figure \ref{fig:3p} illustrates this multilevel switching. The initial transition to \abbrev{LRS} can be followed by a stable transition to a lower \abbrev{LRS}, both \abbrev{LRS} states being stable. The 3\,V and 5\,V sweeps generated the primary and secondary \abbrev{LRS} respectively. \\

\noindent In bipolar samples, multilevel reset occurs at 1 V and 1.8 V over a narrower range, switching remaining stable with significant contrast. Sweeping the set voltage from -2.0 V to -2.3 V with 100 mA compliance produces a gradual conductance increase, the controlled current limiting filament growth and enabling intermediate states. A 2 V bias produces a set spike followed by exponential decay (reset). The decay time constant $\tau$ decreases with higher bias (7 s at 1.4 V vs. 1 s at 1.8 V), indicating faster filament rupture. \\

\noindent Constant current induces volatile spikes through repeated filament formation and rupture (Figure \ref{fig:3s}). Higher current (for example $10\,\mu$A) accelerates formation and Joule heating, raising spike amplitude and frequency. Stochastic trap filling, thermally assisted rupture, and ionic motion produce these voltage bursts, which persist until the bias triggers a transition. At -10 mA, the bipolar device switches after a large spike, reducing subsequent spiking activity.

\section[Conduction and Switching Mechanisms]{Conduction and Switching Mechanisms}

\noindent This section applies the charge-transport framework of Section \ref{subsec:transport} to the measured current--voltage characteristics. It identifies the dominant conduction mechanism in the fabricated silicon-rich silica devices and builds a phenomenological picture of resistance switching that distinguishes the unipolar and bipolar modes \cite{zhuge2013advances}. Unipolar switching is polarity-independent, typical of the symmetric devices, and relies on current compliance to prevent hard breakdown. Bipolar switching requires opposing polarities for Set and Reset, and it is driven by the field-induced migration of charged species such as oxygen vacancies, the valence change mechanism (\abbrev{VCM}) \cite{waser2007redox}. Ionic drift then controls filament formation and rupture, often localised at an electrode interface \cite{waser2009redox}. The unipolar reset is therefore current-driven, through Joule heating, whereas the bipolar reset is field-driven. \\

\noindent Silicon oxide ($SiO_x$) is the primary switching material. Doping with mobile metal ions (Ag, Cu) enables switching \cite{mehonic2012resistive}, the conductive-bridge (\abbrev{CBRAM}) mechanism introduced in Section~\ref{sec:matstack}, but compromises complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (\abbrevdef{CMOS}) compatibility. This research focuses on intrinsic switching in silicon-rich silica, which avoids problematic metal ions and suits \abbrev{CMOS} technology. Conduction may proceed either through a localised filament in the bulk oxide or through a switching layer at the electrode interface (Figure \ref{fig:3t}). A single dominant filament makes switching largely independent of electrode size and oxide thickness, whereas interface-limited transport, governed by a Schottky barrier or by bulk oxygen-vacancy redistribution, scales with electrode area. \\

\end{figure}

\subsection[Dominant Mechanism Identification]{Dominant Mechanism Identification}

\noindent To identify the dominant transport channel, the candidate mechanisms set out in Section \ref{subsec:transport} (ohmic, Poole--Frenkel hopping, thermionic (Schottky) emission, Fowler--Nordheim and trap-assisted tunnelling) were each fitted to the measured I--V data (Figures \ref{fig:3u}--\ref{fig:3w}). Each mechanism predicts a straight line in its own linearised coordinates (for example $\ln(J/E)$ versus $\sqrt{E}$ for Poole--Frenkel, $\ln J$ versus $1/E$ for trap-assisted tunnelling). The quality of each fit was therefore judged by how closely the transformed data followed that line. The slope and intercept then yield the corresponding parameter: the relative dielectric constant $\epsilon_r$ (Poole--Frenkel, thermionic) or the trap barrier height $\phi_t$ (trap-assisted tunnelling). \\

\noindent In Table \ref{table:3a}, the relative dielectric constants extracted for Poole--Frenkel conduction exceed the theoretical silicon-dioxide value ($\epsilon_r \approx 3.9$) and approach, in two cases modestly exceeding, that of pure silicon ($\epsilon_r \approx 12$). This is consistent with conduction through silicon-rich regions within the sub-stoichiometric film. The thermionic-emission fits, by contrast, return relative permittivities of order unity, two below unity ($\epsilon_r = 0.2$--$1.9$). Since a physical dielectric constant cannot fall below unity, these values are unphysical. They show that thermionic emission does not describe the transport, rather than competing with Poole--Frenkel hopping. Each branch reflects a fixed resistance state (the \abbrev{HRS} or \abbrev{LRS} held during a Set or Reset sweep) rather than the transition itself. The extracted parameters therefore characterise steady-state transport in each state, not the switching event. \\

\noindent Across all 25 samples the trap-assisted tunnelling model gave the closest linear agreement for both unipolar and bipolar devices, particularly at high fields. This indicates that carrier tunnelling through defect states is the dominant channel, and the other mechanisms may coexist but fit less well. The original analysis recorded only the slope and intercept of each linearised plot, without a goodness-of-fit statistic, so a coefficient of determination cannot be reconstructed for the full sample set. To illustrate the procedure, refitting a representative bipolar device for which the raw sweep is available gives a coefficient of determination of $R^2 \approx 0.98$ for the trap-assisted tunnelling linearisation. The thermionic linearisation of the same data also reaches $R^2 \approx 0.98$. This shows that a high $R^2$ alone does not validate a mechanism: the fit is statistically good yet returns the unphysical sub-unity permittivity noted above. Trap-assisted tunnelling is therefore preferred on the combined grounds of fit quality \emph{and} a physically meaningful extracted parameter.

\subsection[Proposed Switching Model]{Proposed Switching Model}

\noindent The dominance of trap-assisted tunnelling places the conduction path in a discontinuous chain of oxygen vacancies. This microscopic picture, together with the state-dependent parameters of Table \ref{table:3a}, supports the phenomenological switching model set out below. The starting point is silicon-rich silica, in which the applied field phase-separates oxygen vacancies into silicon dioxide and silicon, with oxygen drift driving the separation. Figure \ref{fig:3x} contrasts the resulting bipolar and unipolar modes schematically. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/siox_switching_mechanism.png}
    \caption[Schematic of switching mechanism in SiOx devices.]{Schematic of bipolar switching in asymmetric devices (left) and of unipolar switching in symmetric devices (right).}
    \label{fig:3x}
\end{figure}

\noindent Field-driven oxygen ion migration describes bipolar switching (Figure \ref{fig:3x}), with intrinsic structural defects providing the conduction path without externally induced damage. Sputtered silicon suboxides grow with a columnar microstructure, and oxygen vacancies are expected to concentrate at the column boundaries and oxide--electrode interfaces, where the defect density is highest \cite{munde2017intrinsic}. Vacancy diffusion is slow at ambient temperature. Under bias, oxygen anions drift towards and accumulate at the interface, and the displaced oxygen has been proposed to form molecular $O_2$ that, under sufficiently aggressive stressing, can nucleate gas pockets there \cite{mehonic2017intrinsic}. The controlled breakdown of electroforming self-terminates once a conductive filament shunts the field, preventing the runaway hard breakdown that would otherwise destroy the device. \\

\noindent Unipolar switching shares this vacancy filament but differs in its reset. Unipolar filaments are denser, resembling metallic nanowires, and their near-ohmic conduction matches the low-resistance, weakly voltage-dependent \abbrev{LRS} current. The high currents this sustains drive the Joule heating that ruptures the filament once a critical current is exceeded. The two modes therefore share a filament but differ in drive: the electric field alone resets the bipolar device (oxygen anions returning to the vacancies), while field-driven Joule heating resets the unipolar one. \\

\noindent Silicon-rich silica ($SiO_x$) is the primary insulator \cite{mehonic2012resistive}. Wide-angle $O-Si-O$ bonds trap electrons, weakening the bonds and creating Frenkel defects (vacancies) \cite{gao2016mechanism}. Figure \ref{fig:3y} summarises the switching modes: unipolar, bipolar, and threshold. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/schematic_iv_curves.png}
    \caption[Schematic I-V curves.]{Schematic I-V curves in: a) non-volatile bipolar memory switching mode, b) non-volatile unipolar memory switching mode; and c) volatile threshold switching mode. In the SET process, a compliance current is required to avoid hard breakdown \cite{li2017resistive}.}
    \label{fig:3y}
\end{figure}

\noindent Bipolar switching uses opposing polarities \cite{wei2008highly}. Current compliance is required for Set but not Reset, which simplifies the system. The voltages typically fall within digital integration limits ($\pm 1$\,V) \cite{menke2009separation}. Unipolar devices allow single-supply-rail operation but need complex compliance to manage high reset currents \cite{yun2007random}. Threshold switching exhibits hysteresis \cite{adler1980threshold} shaped by heat dissipation and electrode thickness \cite{chang2008effects}. \\

\noindent \abbrev{RRAM} development faces several challenges. OxRAM offers good retention but lower conductance ratios \cite{ielmini2010resistance}, whereas \abbrev{CBRAM} offers high ratios but limited endurance \cite{ambrogio2014statistical1}. Stochastic switching and filament variability hinder \abbrev{RRAM} cell design \cite{ambrogio2014statistical2}, limiting commercial density. High-density devices show promise, with low power and high endurance and retention \cite{yang2013memristive, hu2014review, cha2013nanoscale}, but resistance variation remains a barrier \cite{moore2006lithography}. Deep learning tolerates this variability better than standard memory applications, by virtue of its stochastic nature \cite{malik2013governing}. This positions \abbrev{RRAM} as a candidate for neural technologies.

\section[Summary]{Summary}

\noindent This chapter established the empirical foundation of the thesis. It detailed the fabrication of silicon-rich \abbrev{MIM} devices by \abbrev{RF} sputtering and their electrical characterisation. The characterisation revealed coexisting unipolar and bipolar non-volatile switching alongside multilevel and volatile threshold modes. Curve fitting identified trap-assisted tunnelling through conductive oxygen-vacancy filaments as the dominant transport mechanism. The observed device-to-device stochasticity and variability were framed not as mere defects but as properties the later chapters exploit for probabilistic and neuromorphic computing. These operational boundaries and physical mechanisms provide the basis for the modelling and learning frameworks developed in the following chapters.
