\chapter{Fabrication and Characterisation Methodologies}


\section[Device Fabrication and Experimental Setup]{Device Fabrication and Experimental Setup}

Demonstrating the devices' capability to exhibit distinct and stable state-dependent conductance changes is a prerequisite to designing novel neuromorphic systems. Neuronal spiking systems model these conductance changes. This chapter details the experimental methodology and presents the results. The devices exhibit various non-volatile switching properties. Measurements focus on the samples' electrical characteristics and switching mechanisms. The Electronic Materials and Devices group developed the devices investigated in this thesis. This chapter details the full fabrication process for completeness, acknowledging that the research group performed specific tasks.

\subsection[Device Architecture and Materials]{Device Architecture and Materials}

\noindent The device features a metal-insulator-metal (MIM) structure fabricated on a silicon wafer. Thermal oxidation creates a thick silicon dioxide layer on the wafer before bottom electrode deposition to prevent interactions between the bottom metal contact and the wafer. Sputtering deposits the bottom electrode and thin film oxide unpatterned across the sample. A contact mask patterns the top electrical contacts into squares with sides ranging from $200\mu m$ to $800\mu m$ during deposition (Figure \ref{fig:3a}), eliminating the need for photolithography. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/a.png}
    \caption[Device Structure]{Photolithographic mask (left) and dimensions of the top electrical contacts (right).}
    \label{fig:3a}
\end{figure}

\noindent A titanium buffer layer between the top metal contact and the oxide increases adhesion. This adhesive layer is non-ideal, potentially causing additional defect migration within the oxide. However, electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) studies show no evidence of titanium interface migration in these devices \cite{mehonic2017intrinsic}.\\

\noindent While not fully observed in the studied devices, using gold as a primary electrical contact may result in gold atom migration into the oxide layer and subsequent diffusion. For instance, gold diffusion through amorphous SiOx occurs at $390^{\circ} C$ over four hours \cite{madams1974migration}. Heating gold to $500^{\circ} C$ for two hours causes uniform diffusion through a 500 nm oxide layer. No migration occurs below $370^{\circ} C$. Although documented at high temperatures, researchers often overlook gold diffusion in resistance switching memory devices. In electrochemical metallisation, where metallic filaments form between electrodes, gold acts as an inert electrode \cite{kozicki2016electrochemical}. This role persists in valence change memories \cite{ge2014electrode}. For example, in a device with gold and silver electrodes separated by an $As_2S_3$ film, only silver migrates.\\

\noindent Distinct materials form the top and bottom electrodes. Prior studies on high-performance resistance switching memory guided this selection to optimize device performance. As shown by Cox et al.~\cite{cox2021nanoscale}, electrode material selection—particularly using an active electrode like molybdenum and an inert electrode like gold—directly influences conductive filament formation, stability, oxygen exchange dynamics, and device endurance. This deliberate asymmetry enables controlled redox reactions at the active electrode, essential for reliable resistive switching. A profilometer assesses layer thickness. The bottom electrode is 300 nm thick to ensure high conductivity. The oxide thin film is 35 nm thick. The top electrical contact thickness varies by material; gold is 110 nm thick.\\

\subsection[Thin Film Deposition and Patterning]{Thin Film Deposition and Patterning}

\noindent RF sputtering, a physical vapour deposition process, deposited all device layers. Deposition occurs at low pressure in an inert gas environment by bombarding the target material with plasma, expelling atoms. Depending on chamber gas pressure, expelled atoms follow a direct ballistic path or a random walk to the sample. Higher gas pressure increases collisions and random walks, whereas lower pressure produces a direct ballistic trajectory. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/b.png}
    \caption[Radio Frequency Magnetron Sputtering]{Depiction of the fundamental configuration for the deposition of thin films by means of sputtering \cite{jeong2008origin}. A substantial radio frequency electric field is required to ionise argon gas, thereby producing a plasma. The process of deposition is initiated by high-energy collisions between the ionised argon and the target material. The collisions result in target atoms being ejected from the source with high kinetic energy. These atoms traverse the chamber and deposit onto the sample over time, thereby forming an amorphous thin film.}
    \label{fig:3b}
\end{figure}

\noindent Figure \ref{fig:3b} illustrates a simplified sputtering system. The configuration comprises the sample connected to the RF power source anode, the target material before the cathode, and the sputtering gas injected into the chamber. The plasma consists of positively charged argon ions attracted to the negatively charged cathode (target). High-energy collisions between argon ions and the target surface eject target atoms.\\

\noindent The solid target material provides the deposition substance. Applying a strong electric field to the sputtering gas (argon) creates the plasma. The target material's electrical properties dictate the choice between DC and AC fields. Conductive targets, such as metals, require only a DC field. However, for insulating targets like $SiO_2$, a DC field causes charge buildup, quenching the plasma. An AC field, oscillating at radio frequency (RF, typically 13.56 MHz), alternates polarity, preventing charge accumulation and allowing continuous sputtering of dielectric materials. This enables stable plasma generation and efficient deposition for both conductive and insulating targets.\\

\noindent Reactive sputtering introduces a reactive gas, such as oxygen, alongside argon, allowing the target material to react and form an oxide film on the substrate. This process enables precise control over film composition and stoichiometry. Sputtering often results in amorphous films with sub-stoichiometric oxides. The devices' SiOx oxide has a stoichiometry of $SiO_{1.9}$, while the underlying molybdenum layer's RMS roughness (0.9 to 1.5 nm) determines the film's roughness \cite{kenyon2019interplay}.\\


\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/c.png}
    \caption[Contact AFM for thin film thickness measurements.]{Schematic of a AFM \cite{mabilleau2008vitro}. The probe is utilised to scan the surface of the sample, with the height of the probe being adjusted accordingly in order to ensure that a constant force is maintained.     \label{fig:3c}

\noindent Each SiOx sputtering run includes a pair of Si substrate specimens for thickness measurement. A patterned photoresist masks a region of the first Si substrate. Removing the mask after SiOx deposition creates a step on the SiOx/Si interface. A Dektak XT profilometer measures this step, resolving heights of a few nanometres. Ellipsometry measures the second Si substrate covered with SiOx. This optical technique determines thin film thickness by analysing reflected light polarization changes, verifying the deposited layer's thickness. A contact profilometer with 0.5 nm precision measures film thickness after sputtering. A diamond tip scans the sample surface. A feedback loop adjusts the tip's height to maintain consistent force, providing the sample height measurement. The sample's surface height changes proportionally to the tip height. A staircase-like pattern created during production facilitates relative layer thickness measurement.

\subsection[Electrical Characterisation Methodology]{Electrical Characterisation Methodology}

\noindent Current magnitude is the primary metric, providing information about oxide layer conductivity and interface barrier heights. The challenge lies in minimizing measurement equipment artifacts, such as probe contact resistance. Connecting device electrodes precedes current measurement. Options include wire bonding inside a chip carrier or direct probing with micromanipulators. Direct probing with tungsten probes was adopted due to the devices' design and susceptibility to wire bonding damage. Small, exposed electrode pads and thin oxide layers make the devices vulnerable to mechanical stress. The probe tip requires careful lowering to prevent damage. A low voltage test signal confirms contact.\\

\noindent Monitoring the current for a spike determines contact. Initially, the current oscillates around 0 amps. Upon contact, the applied voltage causes a detectable current matching the polarity. Gentle probe lowering and low test voltage prevent sample damage. Wire bonding offers advantages over probing: it avoids sample damage, maintains electrical connection positions between experiments, does not affect contact during temperature measurements, and reduces risk during characterisation. However, the bonding process risks damaging the component. Ultrasonic pulses melt gold or aluminium wire to the contact under pressure. This pressure can compress internal layers, causing shorts between metal contacts and damaging the device. 

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/d.png}
    \caption[Two wire or Four wire (Kelvin) testing.]{Two wire or Four wire (Kelvin) testing. A schematic of the 2-wire measurement setup is provided herewith. The voltage is applied to the sample using two probes. It is important to note that each probe introduces a series resistance with the sample's resistance. The device current is measured by an ammeter in series. Conversely, a constant current is supplied through the sample by two probes. The voltage induced across the device by the current is measured with a voltmeter in parallel with the sample.}
    \label{fig:3d}
\end{figure}

\noindent Following contact method selection, the current monitoring technique is determined. Options typically include 2-wire and 4-wire measurements. Sample conductivity dictates the choice. Applying a set voltage and tracking the total current offers the simplest resistance measurement (2-wire). Ohm's Law determines resistance by connecting a voltage source, ammeter, and device in series. This measurement often proves inaccurate as it assumes electrical resistance resides solely in the test device. In reality, series resistances exist, including wires, internal source/ammeter resistance, and contact resistance. \\

\noindent Contact resistance is crucial in thin film characterization. Fortunately, device resistance usually outweighs electrical resistance, validating this method. However, for low resistance, parasitic resistances become significant, necessitating 4-wire measurement.  This configuration still uses Ohm's law. Instead of sourcing voltage, two probes supply a constant current. Two additional parallel probes measure the induced voltage drop. While contact and wire resistances persist, the voltmeter's high impedance minimizes current through the measuring contacts. \\

\noindent The voltmeter reading accurately represents the device voltage drop because the drop across parasitic resistance is negligible. Reduced current through the voltage-sensing probes minimizes errors induced across contact resistances. Thus, device resistance determines the use of 2-wire or 4-wire measurement. The high resistance (kilo-ohms to mega-ohms) of the studied devices renders parasitic resistances negligible. A picoammeter is required to measure currents in the 100 nA to 1 mA range, as well as lower currents (picoamps to nanoamps). \\

\noindent A digital multimeter (DMM) ammeter provides a basic current measurement method. It uses a shunt resistor to measure voltage drop. While valid for milliamp currents, noise from the shunt resistor complicates lower current measurements. Larger shunt resistors needed for smaller currents introduce thermal noise and voltage burden. Voltage burden becomes problematic when comparable to the applied voltage. This limits DMM current measurements. Picoammeters minimize current readings using transimpedance amplifiers to convert input current to voltage. \\


\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/e.png}
    \caption[Transimpedance amplifier circuit.]{Transimpedance amplifier circuit. The input current ($I_{in}$) is amplified and converted into an output voltage ($V_{out}$) via the operational amplifier. The gain is defined by the feedback resistor (RF). The voltage/current ($V_{out}/I_{in}$) is equivalent to the negative of the measured resistance ($-R_f = V_{out} / I_{in}$).}
    \label{fig:3e}
\end{figure}

\noindent Figure 3.5 illustrates the general operation. The transimpedance amplifier converts input current ($I_{in}$) to output voltage ($V_{out}$). The operational amplifier adjusts output voltage to minimize the voltage difference between input pins. Grounding the non-inverting input (+) forces the inverting input (-) to zero volts. Input current creates a transient voltage offset. The op-amp adjusts output voltage, inducing an equal and opposite current through the feedback resistor, cancelling the input current. This feedback maintains a virtual ground at the inverting pin. \\

\noindent Ohm's law defines the output voltage: $V_{out} = -R_f \cdot I_{inv}$. The feedback resistor, $R_f$, defines amplification. The virtual ground significantly reduces voltage burden by removing the series shunt resistor, facilitating small current measurements impossible with a DMM.  Consequently, a picoammeter is the optimal instrument for current-time measurements or current-voltage sweeps. The Keithley 6430 sourcemeter, combining a picoammeter and low noise voltage source, served this purpose. \\

The device current is amplified and converted into an output voltage via the preamplifier, which is connected in series with the device. \section[Resistive Switching Characteristics]{Resistive Switching Characteristics}

\noindent Resistive switching is a reversible, non-volatile resistance change in two-terminal elements induced by electrical stimuli. In ReRAM devices, a local redox process dictates this mechanism. Repeated application of suitable stimuli achieves reversibility. Describing the memristor states simplifies analysis. A large resistance difference distinguishes "OFF" (High Resistance State, HRS) and "ON" (Low Resistance State, LRS). "Set" transitions from HRS to LRS, and "Reset" transitions back. Initial electroforming changes the device from a pristine state to a switchable state. The pristine state typically exhibits higher resistance than the HRS. Most metal oxide devices exhibit unipolar or bipolar switching; the silicon oxides studied here exhibit both.  Preliminary characterization established the fundamental I-V characteristics. The Keithley 4200-SCS dual sweep functionality, using two 10 $\mu m$ diameter tip probes, performed the tests on both sample sets across all electrode pad sizes.

\subsection[Unipolar Switching Dynamics]{Unipolar Switching Dynamics}

\noindent Switching in these devices requires initial electroforming. Fresh samples typically exist in a high resistance state, necessitating a significant electrical stimulus to transition into LRS. Subsequently, the device reversibly switches between two stable states. \\

    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/g.png}
    \caption[Initial electroforming step for unipolar switching.]{Initial electroforming step for unipolar switching via a double sweep curve. Double sweeping refers to applying a voltage sweep in both forward and reverse directions to capture the device's switching behaviour during electroforming.}
    \label{fig:3g}
\end{figure}

\noindent Electroforming constitutes a controlled electrical breakdown requiring current limitation to preserve switching functionality. The SCS's current compliance provides this limitation. However, slow analyser response may cause overshoot spikes. This step occurs only once on pristine devices. Programming the Keithley-SCS to sweep up to 18V (Figure \ref{fig:3g}) executes the operation. Current peaks and an unstable I-V curve appear during sweeping. At approximately 14V, the device abruptly switches to LRS. Subsequent sweeps are smoother. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/h.png}
    \caption[Non-volatile switching behaviour for unipolar device under current compliance.]{Non-volatile switching behaviour for unipolar device under current compliance.}
    \label{fig:3h}
\end{figure}

\noindent Structural changes during forming, likely oxygen removal forming vacancies, cause the conductance change. Following electroforming, LRS is stable and non-volatile. The initial pristine state is never recovered due to permanent structural alteration. Figure \ref{fig:3h} demonstrates the unipolar switching mechanism in symmetrical MIM devices. The blue plot indicates the Set process (Off to On) at a threshold voltage of approximately 2.3V. A 3V sweep with 3 mA compliance suffices for switching. Reducing voltage below the threshold does not reverse the state \cite{mehonic2016silicon}.\\

\noindent Resetting requires a critical current. Unlike the voltage-triggered Set process, Reset depends on exceeding a current level. The orange plot shows the Reset current is an order of magnitude greater than the Set compliance. This induces Joule heating, rupturing the conductive filament and restoring HRS. High current flow induces Joule heating, causing localised heating and device reset. Without current compliance, hard breakdown or multiple transitions may occur. The switching sequence is repeatable. \\

    \label{fig:3i}
\end{figure}

\noindent HRS conductance lies between LRS and the pristine state. Transitions are abrupt and independent of sweeping parameters. An elevated electric field sets the device (LRS), whereas high Joule heating resets it (HRS). Figure \ref{fig:3i} shows an alternative unipolar switching mechanism. Devices with a set voltage lower than the reset voltage transition to LRS at a lower voltage (1.15V) and return to HRS at a higher voltage (2.85V). This mode requires no current compliance; reset occurs when current exceeds the Joule heating threshold, rupturing the filament. Switching is self-limiting. \\

\section[Physical Mechanisms of Switching]{Physical Mechanisms of Switching}

\noindent This section proposes a phenomenological model of resistance switching in silicon-rich silica RRAM devices, distinguishing between unipolar and bipolar modes. Oxide ReRAM devices typically exhibit unipolar or bipolar switching \cite{zhuge2013advances}. Unipolar switching is polarity-independent, typical of symmetrical devices, and uses current compliance to prevent breakdown. Bipolar devices require opposing polarities for Set and Reset. Field-driven migration of charged species (e.g., oxygen vacancies) drives this valence change mechanism (VCM) \cite{waser2007redox}. Ionic drift controls filament formation/rupture, often localised at an electrode interface \cite{waser2009redox}. Unlike current-driven unipolar reset, the bipolar mechanism is field-driven.


\subsection[Charge Transport Models]{Charge Transport Models}

\noindent Silicon oxide ($SiO_x$) serves as the primary switching material. While metal ion doping (Ag, Cu) enables switching \cite{mehonic2012resistive}, it compromises CMOS compatibility. This research focuses on intrinsic switching in silicon-rich silica, which avoids problematic metal ions and suits CMOS technology. \\

    \label{fig:3t}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection[Direct Tunnelling]{Direct Tunnelling}

\noindent Electroforming typically creates a conductive filament in bulk silicon oxide. A single filament usually dominates, making switching independent of electrode size and layer thickness. Interface switching, driven by Schottky barrier formation or bulk oxygen vacancy conductivity changes, depends on electrode dimensions. This forms a switching layer at the interface. \\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{Chapter3/Figs/u.png}
    \caption[Energy-band diagrams showing different conduction mechanisms.]{Energy-band diagrams showing different conduction mechanisms: (a) direct tunnelling, (b) Fowler-Nordheim tunnelling, (c) thermionic emission, (d) Poole-Frenkel emission, (e) trap-assisted tunnelling \cite{sze2021physics}}
    \label{fig:3u}
\end{figure}

\noindent Electrical conductivity depends on conditions like temperature and electric field. Equation (\ref{eq:3.1}) defines the electric field $E = V/d$. However, assumptions of negligible oxide charges and flat-band voltage may not hold. \\

\begin{align}
    E = \frac{V}{d} \label{eq:3.1}
\end{align}

\begin{align}
    J &\propto E^{2} exp\left[ -\frac{4\sqrt{2m^{*}}(q\phi _{B})^{^{3/2}}}{3q\hslash E_{i}} \right] \label{eq:3.2} \\
    J &\propto V^2exp \left( -\frac{b}{V}\right) \label{eq:3.3}
\end{align}

\noindent Equation (\ref{eq:3.2}) describes tunnelling current density. Tunnelling dominates in strong fields for thin oxides (< 1 nm). Direct tunnelling traverses the full barrier; Fowler-Nordheim tunnelling penetrates a field-deformed triangular barrier. \\

\begin{align}
    J &\propto E \cdot exp\left( -\frac{\Delta E_a}{kT} \right) \label{eq:3.4} \\
    J &\propto \frac{V}{T} \cdot exp \left( -\frac{c}{T}\right) \label{eq:3.5}
\end{align}

\noindent Equation (\ref{eq:3.4}) describes ohmic current density. Ohmic conduction dominates at low fields and high temperatures. Thermally excited carriers move under the electric field. \\

\begin{align}
    J &\propto \frac{E}{T} \cdot exp\left( -\frac{\Delta E_a}{kT} \right) \label{eq:3.6} \\
    J &\propto \frac{V}{T} \cdot exp \left( -\frac{d}{T}\right) \label{eq:3.7}
\end{align}

\noindent Ionic conduction involves mobile ion movement (e.g., oxygen vacancies). An electric field drives these defects through the oxide, creating ionic current. Accumulation at the interface creates a space-charge region and internal field, driving current even after external field removal. \\

\begin{align}
    J &= \frac{9\varepsilon _i \mu V^2}{8d^3} \label{eq:3.8} \\
    J &\propto V^2 \label{eq:3.9}
\end{align}

\noindent The Mott–Gurney law (\ref{eq:3.8}) describes space-charge-limited current from electrode injection. Analysis requires models for interface- and bulk-limited transport. Schottky emission (interface-limited) and Poole-Frenkel conduction (bulk-limited) are the primary candidates. Schottky emission aligns with rectifying I-V curves; Poole-Frenkel aligns with defect-rich amorphous silicon oxide.

\subsubsection[Fowler-Nordheim Tunnelling]{Fowler-Nordheim Tunnelling}

\noindent Quantum mechanical tunnelling dominates in strong fields. Probability depends on field strength and barrier thickness. In Fowler-Nordheim tunnelling, a strong field creates a triangular barrier. Band-bending reduces the tunnelling distance for electrons to enter the conduction band. \\

\begin{align}
    J &= \frac{q^2E^2}{8\pi\hslash\phi _B} exp \left[  -\frac{4\sqrt{2m^{*}}(q\phi _{B})^{^{3/2}}}{3q\hslash E_{i}} \right] \label{eq:3.10} \\
    J &\propto \frac{4\pi q m^* kT}{\hslash^3} \label{eq:3.11}
\end{align}

\noindent A Fowler-Nordheim plot ($ln(J/E^2)$ vs $1/E$) linearizes Equation (\ref{eq:3.10}). The gradient yields barrier height $\phi_B$. The y-intercept indicates electron emission efficiency, accounting for surface roughness field enhancement. This mechanism requires $V > \phi_B$. If $V < \phi_B$, direct tunnelling dominates.


\subsubsection[Poole-Frenkel Hopping]{Poole-Frenkel Hopping}

\noindent Structural defects (traps) in insulators constrain carrier movement. Traps capture and release carriers. In Poole-Frenkel conduction, thermal fluctuations allow trapped electrons to escape to the conduction band. The electric field and thermal energy drive this process. \\

\begin{align}
    J &\propto E_i \cdot exp \left[ -\frac{q(\phi _{B} - \sqrt{qE_i/\pi \varepsilon _i} )}{kT} \right] \label{eq:3.12} \\
    J &\propto V \cdot exp \left[ \frac{q}{kT} \left( 2a\sqrt{V} - \phi _{B} \right) \right] \label{eq:3.13} 
\end{align}

\noindent Equation (\ref{eq:3.12}) describes the current, dependent on trap depth $\phi_B$, permittivity $\epsilon$, and temperature $T$. A non-ideality factor $m$ accounts for fabrication variations.

\subsubsection[Thermionic Emission]{Thermionic Emission}

\noindent The Schottky diode model explains thermionic emission. Metal-insulator junctions provide low forward voltage drop and rapid switching. \\

\begin{align}
    J &= A^{**}T^2exp \left[ -\frac{q(\phi _{B} - \sqrt{qE_i/4\pi \varepsilon _i} )}{kT} \right] \label{eq:3.14} \\
    J &\propto exp \left[ \frac{q}{Kt} \left( a\sqrt{V} - \phi _{B} \right) \right] \label{eq:3.15}
\end{align}

\noindent Equation (\ref{eq:3.14}) describes thermally induced current. The effective Richardson constant $A^{**}$ quantifies electrical properties. \\

\begin{align}
    A^{**} = \frac{4\pi q m^* k^2}{h^3} \label{eq:3.16}
\end{align}

\begin{align}
    a = \sqrt{\frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon _i d}} \label{eq:3.17}
\end{align}

\noindent Thermally excited carriers flow over a potential barrier. In these devices, electrons inject from the metal cathode into the oxide. Thermal energy must exceed the work function. Contact between n-type semiconductor and metal forms a Schottky barrier. Electron flow equilibrates Fermi levels, creating a depletion region and potential barrier. Forward bias reduces the barrier, enhancing thermionic emission. Reverse bias increases the barrier, limiting current to tunnelling thermal electrons until breakdown. Ohmic contacts allow bidirectional flow via tunnelling. High dopant concentration reduces depletion width. Metal evaporation and diffusion can achieve the necessary doping \cite{sze2021physics}.

\subsubsection[Trap Assisted Tunnelling]{Trap Assisted Tunnelling}

\noindent Defects facilitate multi-step tunnelling. Traps divide the energy barrier, increasing tunnelling probability. \\

\begin{align}
    J \propto exp \left[ -\frac{8\pi\sqrt{2qm^*}}{3\hslash E} \phi_t^{\frac{3}{2}} \right] \label{eq:3.18}
\end{align}

\noindent Trap-assisted tunnelling can be elastic or inelastic. Equation (\ref{eq:3.18}) relates current density to trap barrier height $\phi_t$.

\noindent Current-voltage characteristic analysis identifies the trap-assisted tunnelling model as the most accurate description for both unipolar and bipolar devices. This model provides superior fits, particularly at high fields, suggesting carrier tunnelling through defect states dominates. While mechanisms may coexist, trap-assisted tunnelling dominates. Silicon-rich silica contains oxygen vacancies that separate into silicon dioxide and silicon under electric fields. Oxygen drift drives this phase separation. \\

\noindent Field-driven oxygen ion migration describes bipolar switching (Figure \ref{fig:3y}). Intrinsic defects enable this without external damage. Oxygen vacancies concentrate at column boundaries. Diffusion is slow at ambient temperature. Oxygen anions accumulate at the interface, forming $O_2$. Under extreme conditions, bubbles may form. Oxygen ion movement facilitates electrical properties. Controlled breakdown terminates when a conductive filament shunts the field, preventing hard breakdown. \\

\noindent Trap-assisted tunnelling dominance implies a discontinuous filament of oxygen vacancies. Oxygen vacancy diffusion regulates bipolar switching via local redox. Unipolar switching shares mechanisms but differs in reset.  Unipolar filaments are denser, resembling metallic nanowires. This supports high currents for Joule heating. Exceeding a critical current ruptures the filament via thermally activated diffusion. Conductive filaments of oxygen vacancies drive resistive switching. The electric field drives bipolar switching; the field and Joule heating drive unipolar switching. Oxygen anions drift to the electrode during Set \cite{waser2007redox}, forming vacancies. Reset displaces ions back into vacancies or ruptures the filament via Joule heating. \\

\noindent Silicon-rich silica ($SiO_x$) is the primary insulator \cite{mehonic2012resistive}. Wide-angle $O-Si-O$ bonds trap electrons, weakening bonds and creating Frenkel defects (vacancies) \cite{gao2016mechanism}. Switching modes include unipolar, bipolar, and threshold. Figure \ref{fig:3z} summarizes these. \\

    \label{fig:3z}

\noindent Bipolar switching uses opposing polarities \cite{wei2008highly}. Current compliance is required for Set but not Reset, simplifying the system. Voltages are typically within digital integration limits ($\pm 1V$) \cite{menke2009separation}.  Unipolar devices allow single supply rail operation but require complex compliance to manage high reset currents \cite{yun2007random}. Threshold switching exhibits hysteresis \cite{adler1980threshold}, influenced by heat dissipation and electrode thickness \cite{chang2008effects}. \\

\noindent RRAM development faces challenges. OxRAM offers good retention but lower conductance ratios \cite{ielmini2010resistance}. CBRAM offers high ratios but limited endurance \cite{ambrogio2014statistical1}. Stochastic switching and filament variability hinder RRAM cell design \cite{ambrogio2014statistical2}, limiting commercial density.  High-density devices show promise (low power, high endurance/retention) \cite{yang2013memristive, hu2014review, cha2013nanoscale}, but resistance variation remains a barrier \cite{moore2006lithography}. Deep learning's stochastic nature tolerates this variability better than standard memory applications \cite{malik2013governing}, positioning RRAM as a candidate for neural technologies.

\section[Summary]{Summary}

\noindent This chapter establishes the thesis's empirical foundation by detailing the fabrication and electrical characterization of silicon oxide-based memristive devices. It describes the development of metal-insulator-metal (MIM) structures using radio frequency magnetron sputtering with silicon-rich silica. Systematic investigation demonstrates the non-volatile resistive switching behaviors essential for emulating synaptic plasticity. \\

\noindent Characterization reveals coexisting unipolar and bipolar switching modes. Analysis identifies trap-assisted tunnelling as the dominant transport mechanism, supported by conductive oxygen vacancy filaments. This phenomenological insight informs the modeling of device behaviors in subsequent chapters. \\

\noindent Finally, the chapter addresses memristive technology's stochasticity and variability. Rather than detriments, these characteristics are contextualized within the thesis's exploration of RRAM for probabilistic computing and deep learning, where variability can be tolerated or leveraged. Defining operational boundaries and physical mechanisms lays the groundwork for the neuromorphic modeling frameworks and biorealistic learning strategies presented hereafter.
