\chapter{Hardware-Aware Spiking Networks}\label{ch:bio}

\noindent Chapter~\ref{ch:model} condensed the current transient into a compact \abbrev{SPICE} model of its full dual-phase dynamics. This chapter puts the device to work inside a trained spiking network, and finds that whether the transient helps or hinders depends on the network architecture. The chapter is organised around that principle, using two faces of the device in turn. In a static classifier, where the same input is presented at every time step, the evolving conductance of a stuck or overactive synapse is a variability to be managed. Here the network draws on the steady-state Poole--Frenkel I--V map, because time-domain integration through the diode-mediated relaxation suits gradient-based training poorly: each forward pass would demand nested ordinary-differential-equation solves at every synapse and a computation graph awkward to differentiate and unroll. A firing-rate homeostatic regulariser suppresses the variability and improves generalisation on fully connected networks, though the benefit falls away once the network is temporal.\\

\noindent In a temporal classifier, where the network streams a signal as it unfolds, the same relaxation is instead a resource. The slow trap-discharge time $\tau_{leak}$ measured in Chapter~\ref{ch:model} becomes the leak of a leaky integrate-and-fire membrane, and its retention sets how long a decision survives a stimulus-free delay, a working-memory hold-time fixed by fabrication rather than tuned. The same measured $\tau_{leak}$ reappears in Chapter~\ref{ch:learning} as the eligibility trace of reward-modulated learning; here it sets a classifier's temporal memory, and its identification as a learning substrate is the concern of that later chapter. The hardware-aware training platform this chapter builds on was originally developed within the group in TensorFlow \cite{joksas2022nonideality}; the author reimplemented it in PyTorch and extended it from static classifiers to spiking networks, adding the homeostatic gain regulariser, the device-grounded temporal membrane, and the benchmarks reported here. In outline, the results are these: nonideality-aware training retains roughly $80\%$ accuracy against a $90\%$ ideal ceiling; the regulariser adds $+2.6$ to $+4.4$\,pp on fully connected networks but costs $8.5$\,pp once the network is temporal; and the device-set membrane gives a retention-controlled hold-time, $D_{max}\approx6.1\,\tau_{leak}$, that clears recognised neuromorphic benchmarks. The foundations below are only what these results require; the spiking neuron, plasticity rules, and crossbar of Chapter~\ref{ch:foundations} are taken as given.

\section[Spiking Neural Networks]{Spiking Neural Networks}\label{sec:6p1}

This section takes the spiking neuron, its plasticity, and the memristive crossbar of Chapter~\ref{ch:foundations} as given, and recasts only what the rest of the chapter acts on directly: the discrete-time neuron equations the training loop integrates, and the device-facing forms of the local learning rules. The biological and circuit background, from the leaky integrate-and-fire neuron through spike coding to the crossbar vector--matrix product, is established in Chapter~\ref{ch:foundations} (Sections~\ref{subsec:neuronal-modelling}, \ref{subsec:plasticity}, and \ref{sec:neuro-hardware}).

\subsection[Device-Facing Neuron Models]{Device-Facing Neuron Models}

\noindent A spiking neural network builds the event-driven biology of Section~\ref{subsec:neuronal-modelling} in hardware, with \abbrev{CMOS} circuits as neurons and memristors as the synapses whose conductance stores each weight (Figure~\ref{fig:6b}) \cite{saighi2015plasticity, chang2011short}. The current reaching a neuron is the sum of the currents through its synapses, the in-memory vector--matrix multiplication of Section~\ref{sec:neuro-hardware}; when it crosses threshold the neuron fires, and the array adapts its conductances locally through the pulse-overlap \abbrev{STDP} of Section~\ref{sec:neuro-hardware}. The chemical signalling that generates the excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials underlying this process is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:6a} \cite{burr2017neuromorphic}.\\

\subsection[Spike-Based Computation on Memristive Hardware]{Spike-Based Computation on Memristive Hardware}

\noindent The training loop acts on the discrete-time leaky integrate-and-fire (\abbrev{LIF}) neuron of Section~\ref{subsec:neuronal-modelling}, not its continuous form. Figure~\ref{fig:6c} shows the neuron over time: it accumulates weighted input spikes as charge, fires and resets when the charge crosses threshold, and enters a brief refractory period \cite{wu2017enabling}. Equation~\ref{eq:6.1} is the unit-step ($\Delta t = 1$) forward-Euler discretisation of the continuous \abbrev{LIF} equation $\tau_m\,dV_m/dt = -V_m + \sum_i w_{ij} x_i$, the same explicit scheme used for the synaptic dynamics below \cite{eshraghian2023training}, so the membrane relaxes towards rest with time constant $\tau_m$. This constant is normally a free hyperparameter; Section~\ref{sec:6p5} instead sets it from a measured device relaxation, so the neuron's temporal memory becomes the device's own.
\begin{align}
    V_m(t) = V_m(t-1) + \sum_{i} w_{ij}\, x_i(t-1) - \frac{V_m(t-1)}{\tau_m} \label{eq:6.1}
\end{align}

\begin{figure}[htbp!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{Chapter6/Figs/snn_schematic.png}
    \caption[Schematic illustration of a single-layer spiking neural network (SNN).]{Schematic illustration of a single-layer spiking neural network (\abbrev{SNN}). (a) Input spike trains $\{x_1, \ldots, x_N\}$ are weighted by synaptic efficacies $w_{ij}$ and integrated by a postsynaptic leaky integrate-and-fire (\abbrev{LIF}) neuron. (b) The membrane potential $V(t)$ evolves by leaky integration, jumps at each weighted input spike, and on crossing the threshold $V_{th}$ emits an output spike, resets to $V_{reset}$, and enters a refractory period during which subsequent inputs have reduced effect. The trajectory comes from a forward-Euler \abbrev{LIF} simulation driven by the input spikes shown.}
    \label{fig:6c}
\end{figure}

\noindent Equation~\ref{eq:6.2} is the firing rule: the neuron spikes when the voltage exceeds threshold and the refractory period $T_R$ has passed.
\begin{align}
    x(t) = \begin{cases}
        1, & V_m(t) > V_{th} \text{ and } t - t_{spike} > T_R \\
        0, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases} \label{eq:6.2}
\end{align}

\noindent Real-valued inputs are converted to spikes by rate-coding \cite{gerstner1997neural}: at each step a spike $X(t)$ is emitted with probability equal to the normalised input amplitude $\hat{x}\in[0,1]$, compared to a fresh uniform draw $u\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]$,
\begin{align}
    X(t) = \begin{cases}
        1, & u < \hat{x} \\
        0, & \text{otherwise,}
    \end{cases} \label{eq:6.3}
\end{align}
so the expected spike count over the window is proportional to $\hat{x}$. Because the discrete spike is non-differentiable, the network is trained by surrogate-gradient descent through time, unrolling the neuron dynamics so gradient descent can optimise the memristive synapses against them \cite{neftci2019surrogate, zhou2022memristive, pfeiffer2018deep, wang2020supervised}; the surrogate is detailed with the training loop below. Such event-driven networks reduce energy by encoding information in spike timing rather than high-precision numbers \cite{wunderlich2019demonstrating, zhou2020towards}, and mature simulators support their acceleration on \abbrev{GPU}s \cite{pehle2021norse}. Mapping the result onto physical crossbars then requires modelling the devices' imperfections, the nonideality simulations that follow.\\

\subsection[Device-Facing Plasticity Rules]{Device-Facing Plasticity Rules}

\noindent The synaptic plasticity that underlies learning is introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:plasticity}: the persistence-based taxonomy of \abbrev{LTP}, \abbrev{LTD}, and \abbrev{STP}, the Hebbian principle, and the spike-timing-dependent plasticity (\abbrev{STDP}) learning window. The supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning paradigms are introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:learning-paradigms}. This subsection takes those rules as given and reformulates them in the device-facing forms used by the rest of the chapter. It contrasts the long-term potentiation that non-volatile memristors emulate (long-term facilitation, \abbrevdef{LTF}, or \abbrev{LTD}) with the short-term plasticity that volatile devices express.\\

\noindent The Hebbian rate model \cite{hebb2002organization, gerstner2014neuronal} for a weight $w_{ij}$ between presynaptic and postsynaptic firing rates $v_j^{prev}$ and $v_i^{post}$, gated by a neuromodulator $M$, admits a Taylor expansion whose coefficients separate the distinct activity-dependent contributions:
\begin{align}
\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = a_0(w_{ij}, M) + a_1^{prev}(w_{ij}, M)\, v_j^{prev} + a_1^{post}(w_{ij}, M)\, v_i^{post} + a_2^{corr}(w_{ij}, M)\, v_j^{prev} v_i^{post} + \cdots \label{eq:6.5}
\end{align}

\noindent Here $a_0$ is background weight decay, the first-order coefficients $a_1^{prev}$ and $a_1^{post}$ scale isolated presynaptic and postsynaptic activity, and the second-order $a_2^{corr}$ carries the Hebbian correlation between simultaneous firing. These coefficients matter because the pair-based \abbrev{STDP} rule populates exactly $a_1^{prev}$ and $a_2^{corr}$, as shown below.\\

\noindent The pair-based \abbrev{STDP} learning window of Section~\ref{subsec:plasticity} (Chapter~2, Equation~\ref{eq:2.12}) is $W(\Delta t) = A_+(w)\,e^{-\Delta t/\tau_+}$ for $\Delta t = t_{post}-t_{prev} > 0$ and $A_-(w)\,e^{\Delta t/\tau_-}$ for $\Delta t < 0$, with $A_+(w)>0$ and $A_-(w)<0$ setting the magnitude of \abbrev{LTF} and \abbrev{LTD}. It can be recast as a pair of decaying biochemical traces, a form that foreshadows the eligibility trace of Section~\ref{subsec:learning-paradigms} and Chapter~\ref{ch:learning}. Expressing the presynaptic and postsynaptic spike trains as $S_j = \sum_{f} \delta( t - t_j^f )$ and $S_i = \sum_{n} \delta( t - t_i^n )$, the rule reformulates as:
\begin{align}
\frac{dx_j}{dt} &= S_j(t) - \frac{x_j}{\tau_+} \label{eq:6.8} \\
\frac{dy_i}{dt} &= S_i(t) - \frac{y_i}{\tau_-} \label{eq:6.9}
\end{align}

\noindent The variables $x_j$ and $y_i$ act as biochemical traces left by pre- and postsynaptic spikes, corresponding to the exponential decay terms $e^{-|\Delta t| / \tau_+}$ and $e^{-|\Delta t| / \tau_-}$, yielding:
\begin{align}
\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = A_+(w_{ij})\, x_j(t)\, S_i(t) + A_-(w_{ij})\, y_i(t)\, S_j(t) \label{eq:6.10}
\end{align}

\noindent The right-hand terms denote the pre-before-post and post-before-pre effects, respectively. For independent Poisson inputs, \abbrev{STDP} aligns directly with rate models \cite{gerstner2014neuronal}:
\begin{align}
\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = v_{j}^{prev} \int_{0}^{\infty} W(-s)\, \varepsilon(s)\, ds + v_{j}^{prev} v_{i}^{post} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} W(s)\, ds \label{eq:6.11}
\end{align}

\noindent The first term is linear in the presynaptic rate $v_j^{prev}$ alone because each presynaptic spike correlates with the postsynaptic potential $\varepsilon(s)$ it itself drives, so the learning window $W$ is sampled through the neuron's own causal response ($\varepsilon(s)$ for $s>0$); the second, bilinear term collects the rate-independent window overlap $\int W(s)\,ds$ between uncorrelated pre- and postsynaptic firing. Comparing Equations \ref{eq:6.5} and \ref{eq:6.11} shows that \abbrev{STDP} defines the $a_1^{prev}$ and $a_2^{corr}$ coefficients, nullifying the rest. Non-volatile memristors natively emulate \abbrev{LTP} synapses by retaining updated memristance states. Arranged in a crossbar, they perform the analogue vector-matrix multiplication introduced in Section~\ref{sec:neuro-hardware}, formalised for this chapter in Equations~\ref{eq:6.17}--\ref{eq:6.18}.\\

\noindent Gradient backpropagation, a global optimisation algorithm updating weights via backward error propagation, dominates DNNs, while \abbrev{STDP} dominates SNNs. Memristive crossbars support both training and inference paradigms \cite{hasan2017chip, ankit2019puma}.\\

\noindent Backpropagation requires complex external digital circuitry to calculate gradients and sequence update pulses, introducing substantial overhead. Conversely, \abbrev{STDP} naturally maps to memristor physics \cite{maranhao2021low, yakopcic2013generalized}. Figure \ref{fig:6e} illustrates a memristor connecting two neurons. Pre- and postsynaptic spikes generate a precise voltage difference across the device, updating its memristance strictly based on the spike timing interval.\\

\noindent While most research focuses on non-volatile memristors for \abbrev{LTP}, volatile memristors effectively emulate short-term plasticity (\abbrev{STP}), which governs critical functions like motion detection, speech recognition, and working memory \cite{ghanbari2017estimating}. Unlike \abbrev{LTP}, \abbrev{STP} depends heavily on presynaptic spiking activity. Defining $P_{release}$ as the fraction of neurotransmitter released lets both short-term facilitation (\abbrevdef{STF}) and \abbrev{STD} be modelled through the standard phenomenological dynamics \cite{markram1997regulation, dayan2005theoretical}:
\begin{align}
\frac{dP_{release}}{dt} = -\frac{P_{release} - P_0}{\tau_F} + f_F (1 - P_{release}) \sum_{f} \delta\left( t - t^f \right) \label{eq:6.12}
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $t^f$ is the presynaptic spike time, $P_0$ is the resting value, $\tau_F$ governs the recovery timescale, and $f_F$ controls facilitation. Similarly, \abbrev{STD} is defined as:
\begin{align}
\frac{dP_{release}}{dt} = -\frac{P_{release} - P_0}{\tau_D} - f_D\, P_{release} \sum_{f} \delta\left( t - t^f \right) \label{eq:6.13}
\end{align}

\noindent Volatile memristors naturally emulate \abbrev{STF} and \abbrev{STD} by retaining conductance changes only briefly. Paired-pulse facilitation (\abbrevdef{PPF}) and paired-pulse depression (\abbrevdef{PPD}) metrics measure these effects. The index $\frac{A_2}{A_1}$ gives \abbrev{PPF}/\abbrev{PPD} strength, where $A_1$ and $A_2$ are the absolute amplitudes of the excitatory postsynaptic current (\abbrevdef{EPSC}) or inhibitory postsynaptic current (\abbrevdef{IPSC}) from successive spikes. A single-layered molybdenum disulfide ($MoS_2$) memristor, for instance, exploits Joule heating to achieve this volatile conductance modulation \cite{sun2018synaptic}.\\

\noindent Alternatively, the supervised spike-rate-dependent plasticity (\abbrev{SRDP}) rule trains SNNs with high hardware compatibility. Supervised \abbrev{SRDP} overcomes the accuracy limitations of unsupervised methods by incorporating global error signals \cite{pfeiffer2018deep}:
\begin{align}
\Delta_{ij} = \begin{cases}
    +1, & f_{r_j} < f_{t_j} \text{ and } f_{s_i} > \theta \\
    -1, & f_{r_j} < f_{t_j} \text{ and } f_{s_i} < \theta \\
    -2, & f_{r_j} > f_{t_j} \text{ and } f_{s_i} > \theta
\end{cases} \label{eq:6.14}
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $f_s$, $f_r$, and $f_t$ represent the output frequencies of the sensory, relay, and teaching neurons, respectively, and $\theta$ is the activity threshold. The discrete update command $\Delta_{ij}$ dictates the voltage pulses applied to the synapses connecting the $i$-th sensory and $j$-th relay neurons.\\

\noindent The rule evaluates the relay neuron's output against the teaching frequency. If the relay neuron fires too slowly ($f_{r_j} < f_{t_j}$), the network strengthens pathways from highly active sensory neurons ($f_{s_i} > \theta$) via $\Delta_{ij} = +1$ and weakens pathways from insufficiently active neurons ($f_{s_i} < \theta$) via $\Delta_{ij} = -1$. If the relay neuron fires too fast ($f_{r_j} > f_{t_j}$), it has learned the wrong features, and the network aggressively erases them by decreasing the weight ($\Delta_{ij} = -2$).\\

\noindent Because physical memristors strictly possess positive conductance, hardware implementations use differential pairs to encode signed weights. A $\Delta_{ij} = +1$ command pulses the 'negative' memristor to increase the effective weight, while a $-1$ or $-2$ command pulses the 'positive' memristor to decrease it. A global loss function evaluates overall training progress by measuring the normalised discrepancy between actual and target frequencies. For a single training sample $k$, the error $l_k$ across all $j$ relay neurons is:
\begin{align}
l_k       &= \frac{\sum_{j} \left( f_{r,j} - f_{t,j} \right)^2}{\sum_{j} f_{t,j}^2} \label{eq:6.15} \\
\text{Loss} &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} l_k \label{eq:6.16}
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $f_{r,j}$ and $f_{t,j}$ are the actual and target output frequencies for the $j$-th relay neuron. The system computes the individual error $l_k$ and averages it across all $N$ training samples to give the total loss. The algorithm applies local \abbrev{SRDP} updates continually and terminates once the loss falls below a set threshold.

\section[Nonideality-Aware Training]{Nonideality-Aware Training}

\noindent With the spiking primitives and local learning rules of Section~\ref{sec:6p1} in place, the focus turns to training networks whose synapses are physical memristors. This section maps weights onto a crossbar, characterises the device's I-V non-idealities, folds them into the forward and backward passes, and validates the resulting hardware-aware training on \abbrev{MNIST}.

\subsection[Crossbar Arrays and Weight Mapping]{Crossbar Arrays and Weight Mapping}

\noindent Nanoscale non-volatile memory devices arranged as \abbrev{RRAM} synapses enable highly parallel, brain-inspired computing at low state-transition energy and a compact footprint \cite{zamarreno2011spike, querlioz2012bioinspired, yu2011electronic}. Spiking convolutional architectures encode inputs as spatiotemporal spike patterns and extract features by local, competitive (winner-take-all) \abbrev{STDP} \cite{kheradpisheh2018stdp, markram2012spike}. Figure~\ref{fig:6f} illustrates an \abbrev{RRAM} crossbar array connecting two neuronal layers, in which back-propagated spikes modulate the conductances in situ \cite{wu2018dendritic}.\\


\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Chapter6/Figs/rram_crossbar_snn_layer.png}
    \caption[An SNN layer utilising a crossbar array of RRAM synapses.]{An \abbrev{SNN} layer utilising a crossbar array of \abbrev{RRAM} synapses. Multiple parallel \abbrev{RRAM} devices connect each pre-synaptic and post-synaptic neuron. Following dendritic processing, back-propagated spikes enable in-situ modulation of the \abbrev{RRAM} conductance according to the \abbrev{STDP} learning rule \cite{wu2018dendritic}.}
    \label{fig:6f}
\end{figure}

\noindent This dense 2D array mimics standard memory layouts and forms a repeatable motif that scales to deeper SNNs, extensible to 3D through-silicon-via integration \cite{jo2009programmable}. Realising \abbrev{STDP} locally in such arrays is limited by the devices themselves: experimental \abbrev{RRAM} switches abruptly and stochastically between resistance states, restricting stable synaptic resolution \cite{prakash2016multilevel, suri2013bio, soni2011stochastic, yu2012switching, li2014stochastic}, which compound multi-device synapses partly mitigate \cite{bill2014compound, gaba2013stochastic}. The same \abbrev{STDP}-like switching appears across nanoscale memristor families, including phase-change and spin-transfer-torque devices \cite{kang2015emulation, mandal2014novel, sengupta2015spin, krzysteczko2012memristive, li2013ultrafast, chen2016review}, and supports unsupervised local learning schemes such as Bayesian and stability-regularised \abbrev{STDP} \cite{nessler2013bayesian, kappel2014stdp, sprekeler2007slowness, toyoizumi2004spike}. Because purely local \abbrev{STDP} nonetheless lacks a global credit-assignment signal, scaling to deep networks instead applies gradient descent directly to the nonlinear state evolution of the memristive components \cite{zhou2022memristive, neftci2019surrogate}, the approach taken here. Physically, the neuron's voltage response drives the memristive synapses through the crossbar vector--matrix product of Section~\ref{sec:neuro-hardware}:
\begin{align}
\begin{bmatrix} I_1 \\ I_2 \\ \vdots \\ I_M \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
    w_{1,1} & w_{1,2} & \cdots & w_{1,M} \\
    w_{2,1} & w_{2,2} & \cdots & w_{2,M} \\
    \vdots  & \vdots  & \ddots & \vdots  \\
    w_{N,1} & w_{N,2} & \cdots & w_{N,M}
\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}
\begin{bmatrix} V_1 \\ V_2 \\ \vdots \\ V_N \end{bmatrix} \label{eq:6.17}
\end{align}


\noindent Here $\mathbf{I}$ is the output-current vector, with one entry per output neuron $j=1,\dots,M$; $\mathbf{V}$ is the input-voltage vector, with one entry per input line $i=1,\dots,N$; and the weight $w_{ij}$, mapped onto device conductances, connects input line $i$ to output $j$, so that
\begin{align}
I_j = \sum_{i=1}^{N} V_i\, w_{ij} \label{eq:6.18}
\end{align}

\noindent The output current vector $\textbf{I}$ accumulates along the bitlines and drives the next \abbrev{LIF} neuron layer, with buffers or scaling factors mitigating resistive loading attenuation \cite{wang2020high, wang2022low}.
\begin{align}
\tau_{syn} \frac{dI_j}{dt} &= a_j - I_j \label{eq:6.19} \\
\tau_{syn} \frac{da_j}{dt} &= -a_j + \tau_{syn}\sum_{i} \sum_{n} w_{ij}\, \delta\left( t - t_i^n \right) \label{eq:6.20}
\end{align}

\noindent The double sum in Equation (\ref{eq:6.20}) runs over the spikes $\{t_i^n\}_n$ emitted by each presynaptic neuron $i$ and over all presynaptic neurons projecting to postsynaptic neuron $j$. The trace $a_j$ is the synaptic activation aggregated at $j$ from weighted input spikes, decaying with time constant $\tau_{syn}$. The current $I_j$ is the alpha-shaped postsynaptic current, obtained as a low-pass filter of $a_j$ that integrates onto the downstream LIF membrane potential. The continuous alpha waveform accurately approximates biological graded potentials in the sensory periphery, effectively translating discrete spikes into analogue physical dynamics \cite{eshraghian2018formulation}.
\begin{align}
I_j^{t+1} &= I_j^t + \frac{a_j^t - I_j^t}{\tau_{syn}} \label{eq:6.21} \\
a_j^{t+1} &= a_j^t - \frac{a_j^t}{\tau_{syn}} + \sum_{i} \sum_{n} w_{ij}\, \delta\left( t - t_i^n \right) \label{eq:6.22}
\end{align}


\noindent To train this network by gradient descent, the continuous dynamics are discretised (Equations \ref{eq:6.21} and \ref{eq:6.22}), unfolding the memristive state in a computational graph akin to a recurrent neural network (\abbrev{RNN}). Standard \abbrev{SPICE} simulators use complex solvers like backward Euler. Compatibility with backpropagation through time (\abbrev{BPTT}), however, requires the forward Euler method, which computes the next time step explicitly. The unrolled memristive SNN (\abbrevdef{MSNN}) lets gradient descent optimise memristive synapses against \abbrev{LIF} evolution, with a loss function evaluating the output layer's continuous membrane potential $v$ at each time step. \abbrev{SNN} training must retain intermediate states to guide learning accurately, making \abbrev{BPTT} preferable to memory-efficient alternatives like the continuous-time adjoint sensitivity method \cite{chen2018neural}.\\

\noindent During supervised learning, the algorithm maximises the target neuron's spike frequency while suppressing incorrect outputs, applying error backpropagation directly to the continuous membrane voltages \cite{neftci2019surrogate}. \abbrev{BPTT} applies the chain rule backward through time and layers to compute synaptic gradients \cite{eshraghian2023training}, and treating mathematical weights $w$ as physical conductances maps these updates directly to the \abbrev{RRAM} crossbars. The optimiser here is backpropagation through time rather than a biological rule. Biorealism, in the sense fixed in Chapter~2, resides in the physically grounded device-to-synapse mapping the training acts on, not in the training algorithm.\\

\noindent Together, these elements define one full spiking time step on the array. It is worth stating explicitly, because every result in this chapter and the reinforcement-learning chapter that follows runs on this same loop. At time step $t$, the presynaptic layer presents its spikes as a vector of row (wordline) voltages $\mathbf{V}(t)$, a spike encoded as a read pulse and no-spike as zero. The crossbar performs the analogue vector--matrix product of Equation~(\ref{eq:6.18}) in place. Each column (bitline) sums the Ohmic currents $I_j = \sum_i G_{ij} V_i(t)$ by Kirchhoff's law, so the conductance matrix is the weight matrix and the multiply costs no explicit arithmetic. Each bitline current is low-pass filtered into the alpha-shaped synaptic current of Equations~(\ref{eq:6.21})--(\ref{eq:6.22}) and integrated onto the postsynaptic membrane potential of Equation~(\ref{eq:6.1}). When that potential crosses threshold, the neuron emits a spike per Equation~(\ref{eq:6.2}), resets, and enters its refractory window. The emitted spikes become the wordline voltages driving the next layer's crossbar, and the cycle repeats.\\

\noindent Signed weights are realised, as throughout this chapter, by the differential conductance pair of Equation~(\ref{eq:6.28}), $w_{ij} \propto G^{+}_{ij} - G^{-}_{ij}$, read on paired bitlines whose currents are subtracted at the neuron input, so the in-memory spiking inference and the gradient-based training share one description.\\

\noindent Mathematical optimisation of \abbrev{MSNN}s typically assumes ideal components. Mapping weights onto physical crossbars is harder, because real memristors exhibit stuck-at faults, random telegraph noise (\abbrevdef{RTN}), and highly nonlinear current-voltage (I-V) characteristics. Hardware fixes for these non-idealities, such as compensation circuitry \cite{ambrogio2018equivalent, li2018analogue} or complex programming schemes \cite{xia2017stuck, chen2017accelerator, jain2019cxdnn, wang2020ssm, li2019build}, add substantial overhead \cite{amirsoleimani2020memory, cai2019fully}. \\

\noindent Hardware-agnostic algorithmic solutions instead neutralise noise without device changes. Committee machines (CMs), for example, average outputs across parallel ensembles \cite{joksas2020committee}. Ex-situ training also builds resilience by modifying loss functions \cite{zhu2020statistical} or injecting noise \cite{he2019noise, joshi2020accurate}, and recent work extends these schemes to recover adversarial robustness even when test-time non-idealities are only partially known \cite{joksas2025robust}. Simple noise injection, however, cannot model complex I-V nonlinearity adequately. End-to-end compute-in-memory evaluation tools such as SpikeSim further account for time-step-accumulated crossbar non-idealities, IR drop, and \abbrevdef{ADC} quantisation \cite{moitra2023spikesim}. To develop a reliable ex-situ methodology, this research embeds empirical characteristics from a physical $SiO_x$ memristor directly into the training loop.\\

\noindent The modelled $SiO_x$ device features a 35 nm amorphous silicon oxide layer between a 280 nm $Mo$ bottom electrode and a 110 nm $Au$ top electrode, with a 3 nm $Ti$ wetting layer. After electroforming and initial cyclic stress tests, positive voltage sweeps generated diverse resistance states spanning multiple orders of magnitude. Integrating these characteristics requires modifying the network's activation calculation. Standard ex-situ training assumes a perfectly linear relationship between inputs ($x_i$) and weights ($w_{ij}$):
\begin{align}
y_j = f\left( \sum_{i=1}^{M} x_i\, w_{ij} \right) \label{eq:6.23}
\end{align}

\noindent Because non-Ohmic behaviour is device-specific, a nonlinear function $g$ replaces this linear product to capture the hardware characteristics \cite{joksas2022nonideality}:
\begin{align}
y_j = f\left( \sum_{i=1}^{M} g\left( x_i, w_{ij} \right) \right) \label{eq:6.24}
\end{align}

\noindent Three distinct quantities recur through this mapping and are kept notationally separate: the abstract, signed synaptic weight $w_{ij}$ used by the network (lowercase $w$); the physical, non-negative device conductances $G_+, G_-$ that encode it as a differential pair; and, where the two halves are trained independently, the non-negative weight pair $W_{ij}^\pm$ (uppercase $W$). Function $g$ must also reflect this mapping from mathematical weights to physical conductances. Scaling factors $k_V$ and $k_I$ map network inputs and outputs to physical voltages and currents, while $k_G$ maps weights to the hardware's conductance range $[G_{min}, G_{max}]$:
\begin{align}
V    &= k_V\, x \label{eq:6.25} \\
y    &= \frac{I}{k_I} = \frac{I}{k_V k_G} \label{eq:6.26} \\
k_G  &= \frac{G_{max} - G_{min}}{\max |\mathbf{W}|} = \frac{G_{on} - G_{off}}{\max |\mathbf{W}|} \label{eq:6.27}
\end{align}

\noindent Because conductances are non-negative, signed weights are encoded by the differential pair $G_+/G_-$ of Section~\ref{sec:neuro-hardware}, with the weight read as the difference of the two bitline currents. The question specific to the training mapping here is how the conductance budget $[G_{off}, G_{on}]$ is allocated to that pair. An intuitive scheme assigns the two devices symmetrically around the average conductance ($G_{avg}$):
\begin{align}
G_{\pm}  &= G_{avg} \pm \frac{k_G w}{2} \label{eq:6.28} \\
G_{avg}  &= \frac{G_{off} + G_{on}}{2} \label{eq:6.29}
\end{align}

\noindent Because infinite pairs yield the same difference \cite{kim20214k}, this ambiguity can minimise static power by keeping at least one device in its high-resistance OFF state ($G_{off}$):
\begin{align}
G_+ &= G_{off} + \max\left( 0, k_G w \right) \label{eq:6.30} \\
G_- &= G_{off} - \min\left( 0, k_G w \right) \label{eq:6.31}
\end{align}

\noindent An alternative 'double weights' approach trains two distinct sets of non-negative weights, $W^+$ and $W^-$, independently \cite{kendall2020training}. The activation function computes their difference naturally:
\begin{align}
y_j = f\left( \sum_{i=1}^{M} \left[ g\left( x_i, W_{ij}^+ \right) - g\left( x_i, W_{ij}^- \right) \right] \right) \label{eq:6.32}
\end{align}

\noindent These non-negative weights map linearly to physical conductances $G_+$ and $G_-$, avoiding mapping gaps:
\begin{align}
G_{\pm} = k_G W_{\pm} + G_{off} \label{eq:6.33}
\end{align}

\noindent The double-weight formulation exposes physical conductances directly to the training algorithm. If non-idealities like programming deviations \cite{kim2016voltage} dominate certain conductance ranges, gradient descent naturally selects optimal pairs to minimise these effects and maximise robustness.\\

\noindent With the mapping schemes established, the method is validated by classification on the \abbrev{MNIST} handwritten-digit benchmark. Network computation relies entirely on propagating individual spike events \cite{liu2014memristor}, with input intensity translated into precise firing times so that stronger stimuli elicit earlier spikes \cite{serrano2013stdp}. Validating this event-driven classification first requires calibrating the device non-idealities that affect inference.

\subsection[Device Model, Calibration, and Validation]{Device Model, Calibration, and Validation}

\noindent This simulation employs experimental data from a $SiO_x$ device, simplified device breakdown models, and literature findings on programming variability. I-V measurements characterise deviations from Ohmic behaviour in memristive devices:
\begin{align}
\gamma \equiv \frac{I(2 V_{ref})}{I(V_{ref})} \label{eq:6.34}
\end{align}

\noindent This approach examines two points on an I-V curve \cite{lentz2013current} to gauge conductance linearity (\ref{eq:6.34}). A value of 1 indicates Ohmic behaviour, and any deviation indicates I-V nonlinearity. Although $\gamma$ gives a snapshot of non-Ohmic behaviour at specific voltages, relying on it alone hampers accurate modelling \cite{sung2018effect}. Extrapolating the whole continuous I-V relationship from two arbitrary points imposes a rigid form. This form cannot capture highly non-linear conduction mechanisms, such as Poole-Frenkel emission or trap-assisted tunnelling, across the full operational voltage range. This empirical parameter therefore lacks the predictive physical grounding needed to simulate continuous memristive dynamics accurately during training and inference.
\begin{align}
I = V_{ref}\, G \left( \frac{V}{V_{ref}} \right)^{\log_2 \gamma} \label{eq:6.35}
\end{align}

\noindent Assuming the equality in (\ref{eq:6.35}) holds for all $V$, rather than just $V_{ref}$, constructs an entire I-V curve from a given nonlinearity parameter $\gamma$. In this equation, $G$ represents the conductance parameter.\\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
\centering    
\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Chapter6/Figs/siox_iv_regions.png}
\caption[I-V sweeps of a SiOx device are presented for two regions]{I-V sweeps of a SiOx device are presented for two regions: a) low-resistance region with average resistance ranging from 284.6 $\Omega$ to 1003 $\Omega$, and b) high-resistance region with average resistance ranging from 366.2 k$\Omega$ to 1.295 M$\Omega$. Only the voltage range from 0.0 V to 0.5 V was considered for all curves. The nonlinearity parameter was calculated by dividing the current at 0.5 V by the current at 0.25 V \cite{joksas2022nonideality}.}
\label{fig:6h}
\end{figure}

\noindent Nonlinearity parameters $\gamma$ were extracted from $SiO_x$ \abbrev{RRAM} experimental data. Incremental positive sweeps gradually reset the device from a low-resistance to a high-resistance state, yielding many states for I-V nonlinearity analysis. Low-resistance states display greater linearity and minimal variability, whereas high-resistance states exhibit greater, less predictable nonlinearity.\\

\noindent $V_{ref}$ in (\ref{eq:6.34}) was set to 0.25V, half the minimum switching voltage. Devices operated within a conductance range between $G_{off} = 1/R_{off}$ and $G_{on} = 1/R_{on}$, and states were selected so the $G_{on}/G_{off}$ ratio for high-resistance devices stayed slightly larger. This guarantees that any higher error rate in the high-resistance group stems from nonlinearity and its variability, not a limited dynamic range.\\

\noindent A faithful model must account for uncertainty in each device's nonlinearity. High-resistance states are generally more nonlinear, but the precise degree at any specific state remains hard to determine, as the I-V curves in Figure \ref{fig:6h} show. Each device's parameter $\gamma$ was therefore drawn from a truncated normal distribution, with values below 2 truncated to ensure realistic behaviour and numerical stability. The mean $m_\gamma$ and standard deviation $s_\gamma$ characterised the underlying normal distribution before truncation. \\

\noindent Simulating nonidealities like I-V nonlinearity by conventional noise injection, which merely disturbs conductance values, is insufficient. Such injection adds random, static perturbations to synaptic weights, implicitly assuming the underlying conduction stays Ohmic (current strictly proportional to voltage). True I-V nonlinearity, however, makes a memristor's effective conductance change with the instantaneous applied voltage. Static weight perturbations cannot capture this voltage-dependent modulation. A custom forward propagation function must therefore be defined to reflect the dynamic, nonlinear relationship between input voltages and output currents.
\begin{align}
g(x, w_\pm) = \frac{\left( k_G w_\pm + G_{off} \right) \left( k_V x \right)^{\log_2 \gamma_\pm}}{k_V} \label{eq:6.36}
\end{align}

\noindent Equation (\ref{eq:6.36}) incorporates this I-V nonlinearity into the function $g$ proposed in Equation (\ref{eq:6.32}) by combining Equations (\ref{eq:6.24}), (\ref{eq:6.33}), and (\ref{eq:6.35}). Here $g$ returns the network-scaled output $y = I/(k_V k_G)$ of Equation~(\ref{eq:6.26}), not the raw device current: substituting $V = k_V x$ and the conductance map into Equation~(\ref{eq:6.35}) gives a numerator factor $k_V x$ and an overall $1/k_G$, with the constant $V_{ref}^{\log_2\gamma}$ base-normalisation absorbed into the prefactor. $k_V$ was set to $2V_{ref}$ according to Equation (\ref{eq:6.34}) and the resulting function was implemented using Torch.\\

\noindent Training simulations require an I-V model with built-in stochasticity. A lookup table of experimental I-V measurements would let training adapt to one specific instance of nonlinear behaviour, limiting generalisation to other devices. The Poole-Frenkel conduction model \cite{joksas2022nonideality} was selected instead to describe non-Ohmic memristor behaviour. It provides a plausible physical mechanism for $SiO_x$ devices and fits the experimental I-V curves well. Its simple analytical form readily incorporates stochasticity by treating its two fitting parameters, the pre-exponential factor $c$ and the effective oxide thickness-permittivity product $d\varepsilon$, as uncertain variables.
\begin{align}
I = c V \exp\left( \frac{2e}{k_B T} \sqrt{\frac{e V}{4\pi d \varepsilon}} \right) \label{eq:6.37}
\end{align}

\noindent Equation (\ref{eq:6.37}) defines the Poole-Frenkel model, where $I$ is current, $V$ is voltage, $c$ is a conductance-like constant, $T$ is temperature ($20^\circ C$), $d$ is effective oxide thickness, and $\epsilon$ is permittivity. The parameter $c$ and the product $d\varepsilon$ were fitted from experimental data.\\

\noindent The variability of $c$ and $d\varepsilon$ was modelled by predicting their values from observable variables, with deviations from these trends quantifying parameter uncertainty. Memristive behaviour varies markedly across resistance states \cite{mehonic2015structural}, often linking to the fundamental conductance quantum $G_0 = 2e^2/h$ \cite{yi2016quantized}, the minimum conductance of a single atomic-scale transport channel ($e$ is elementary charge, $h$ is Planck's constant). When programmed to high-resistance states near or below $G_0$, a memristor's conductive filament becomes atomically thin or discontinuous, and the device shifts from continuous, bulk-like conduction to a quantum point-contact regime. Carrier transport then relies heavily on tunnelling across discrete atomic gaps, generating the highly variable, non-linear I-V characteristics of high-resistance states.\\

\noindent The behaviour of the fitted parameters $c$ and $d\varepsilon$ further validates the Poole-Frenkel model for $SiO_x$ devices. The parameter $c$ is a baseline conductance multiplier \cite{joksas2022memristive}. Fitting it reveals a clear distinction, with low-resistance states showing stable conductance and high-resistance states erratic deviations. The $d\varepsilon$ term sets I-V nonlinearity directly. In Equation (\ref{eq:6.37}) it sits in the denominator of the exponentiated square root, so a smaller $d\varepsilon$ drives a steeper, more non-linear current response. Low Resistance State (\abbrev{LRS}) devices accordingly yield much larger $d\varepsilon$ values, reflecting smoother, Ohmic-like conduction than the highly non-linear high-resistance states. \\

\noindent Artificially large $d\varepsilon$ values in Equation (\ref{eq:6.37}) approximate Ohmic behaviour in Low Resistance States algebraically but lack physical meaning, since the Poole-Frenkel mechanism applies strictly to less conductive states near and below $G_0$. Even within physically valid higher-resistance states, $d\varepsilon$ shows no predictable trend. The extracted values are lower, reflecting inherently steep, non-linear I-V profiles, but their distribution plateaus and becomes erratic, mirroring the highly variable curves in Figure \ref{fig:6h}. These deviations limit the model in deterministic simulations. The same uncertainty, however, provides the stochastic behaviour needed to test rigorously the ex-situ training algorithm's resilience to unknown hardware non-idealities.\\

\noindent Because physical characterisation yielded only a finite set of parameter pairs, these empirical points were used to build a generative multivariate distribution. This distribution synthetically produced the many unique, physically realistic synaptic parameters needed to populate and test a large-scale network. Two resistance regions were defined for evaluation. The low-resistance range interpolated parameters between the lowest resistance state and five times that resistance. The high-resistance range interpolated between the highest resistance state and one-fifth of that resistance, keeping a consistent dynamic range.\\

\noindent The statistical model determined each device's output current by first interpolating initial values for $c$ and $d\varepsilon$ from resistance $R$ fits, then perturbing them with a multivariate normal distribution defined by the fit residuals' covariance matrix, and finally computing the current $I$ from Equation (\ref{eq:6.37}).\\

\noindent Selecting the output function $g$ for ex-situ training with non-linear nonidealities depends on both the mapping scheme and the intrinsic device nonlinearity. Combining the linear input-output mapping (\ref{eq:6.24}), the double-weight mapping (\ref{eq:6.33}), and the current-voltage relationship characterised by $c$ and $d\varepsilon$ (\ref{eq:6.37}) yields $g$:
\begin{align}
g\left( x, W_\pm \right) &= \frac{c(W_\pm)\, (k_V x)}{k_V k_G} \exp\!\left( \dfrac{2e}{k_B T} \sqrt{\dfrac{e k_V x}{4\pi\, d\varepsilon(W_\pm)}} \right) \label{eq:6.38} \\
\begin{bmatrix} \ln c \\ \ln (d\varepsilon) \end{bmatrix} &= -\mathbf{m}\, \ln\left( k_G W_\pm + G_{off} \right) + \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{E} \label{eq:6.39}
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $\mathbf{m}$ represents slopes, $\mathbf{b}$ represents intercepts, and the error $\mathbf{E} \sim \mathcal{N}_2(0, \Sigma)$ is drawn from a normal distribution with zero mean and the residual covariance matrix $\Sigma$. The explicit notation $c(W_\pm)$ and $d\varepsilon(W_\pm)$ in Equation (\ref{eq:6.38}) records that the two PF parameters are not free at training time but are sampled per synapse from the bivariate regression of Equation (\ref{eq:6.39}) conditioned on the synapse's target weight $W_\pm$, so the forward map composes Equations (\ref{eq:6.39}) and (\ref{eq:6.38}) in that order.

\noindent With the device model calibrated, the resulting hardware-aware training is validated by classification. A two-layer network illustrates the \abbrev{SNN} structure (Figure \ref{fig:6i}). Multilayer SNNs operate as fully connected feedforward networks, with all neurons between adjacent layers connected, and both input and output neurons communicating via spike trains.\\

\noindent A two-layer memristive SNN (\abbrev{MSNN}) was designed to examine how non-linear device properties affect classification of the \abbrev{MNIST} handwritten dataset (converted to spike trains). The network takes 28x28 image inputs, trains on 60,000 samples and tests on 10,000, and learns representative features by updating synaptic weights. To evaluate non-ideality-aware training on more complex tasks, memristive convolutional spiking neural networks (\abbrevdef{MCSNN}) were trained, with the convolutional layers assumed digital and the fully connected layers using memristive crossbar arrays subject to high I-V nonlinearity.\\

\noindent The fully connected \abbrev{MSNN} model (Figure \ref{fig:6i}) has 100 hidden neurons with Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (\abbrev{LIF}) activation, followed by a 10-neuron output layer. The \abbrev{MCSNN} architecture stacks a convolutional layer (12 filters, $5 \times 5$ kernel, \abbrev{LIF} activation), a $2 \times 2$ pooling layer, a second convolutional layer (64 filters, $5 \times 5$ kernel, \abbrev{LIF} activation), and a second $2 \times 2$ pooling layer, with a final memristive fully connected layer feeding 10 \abbrev{LIF} output neurons. The Torch cross-entropy loss function \cite{eshraghian2023training} computes output loss and applies the softmax automatically.\\

\noindent Local rules like \abbrev{STDP} facilitate unsupervised feature extraction but do not provide the global credit-assignment signal required by deep classification networks, so the experiments in this chapter use Backpropagation Through Time (\abbrev{BPTT}) on randomly initialised weights as the sole training mechanism. The optimiser is Adam, and the loss is the cross-entropy between the temporally averaged output spike rate and the digit label, computed over the full simulation window of each example. \abbrev{BPTT} unrolls the recurrent computation graph in time and propagates the error signal backward through both layers and time steps to assign weight contributions.\\

\noindent The non-differentiability of the spike Heaviside, $s(t) = H(V(t) - V_{th})$, is handled with a surrogate-gradient trick: the forward pass uses true discrete spiking, and the backward pass replaces the Heaviside derivative by a smooth surrogate
\begin{align}
    \sigma(V) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\beta(V - V_{th})}} \label{eq:6.40}
\end{align}
\noindent whose derivative $d\sigma/dV$ approximates the missing gradient. The steepness $\beta$ controls how closely the surrogate tracks the Heaviside in the limit. This is distinct from stochastic gradient descent (\abbrevdef{SGD}), which refers to the optimiser update rule; the surrogate is a property of the backward pass, not of the optimiser. Together, the surrogate-gradient backward pass and the Adam update enable end-to-end gradient-based training of the multi-layer memristive \abbrev{SNN} while preserving discrete spike dynamics in forward inference.\\

\noindent All synapses were simulated with empirical $SiO_x$ memristor models, folding non-idealities like I-V nonlinearity and variability (Equations \ref{eq:6.36}--\ref{eq:6.39}) directly into the forward and backward passes, so the algorithm learns to compensate for the targeted hardware imperfections during software training. Deployment then programs these optimised weights onto the physical memristor array.\\

\noindent A rough order-of-magnitude projection illustrates the energy case, with the caveat that these are estimates rather than measured figures, since the network here is simulated and no on-chip power was recorded. Take a representative device switching energy of order $1$--$10$\,pJ per programming pulse and the network's measured spike count ($\sim$12{,}000 spikes per image). A per-image inference energy of the order of $10^{2}$\,nJ follows. This would place an asynchronous memristive \abbrev{SNN} roughly an order of magnitude below a conventional \abbrevdef{CPU}/\abbrev{GPU} deployment of comparable scale, consistent with the order-of-magnitude advantages reported for fabricated compute-in-memory systems \cite{yao2020fully}. The spike-integration window sets the inference latency in the present encoding. Time-to-first-spike encoding or early stopping would shorten it, and massively parallel crossbar execution would compress it further. These figures are indicative of the regime, not a validated power budget, which would require an on-chip measurement.\\

\noindent Modelling all non-idealities accurately for ex-situ training remains hard, as real fabrication variations often exceed simplified statistical models. Nonlinearity here was drawn from a truncated normal distribution, but exact means and standard deviations vary across batches, and unmodelled factors such as stuck-at faults, read noise, and retention loss degrade performance unless the hardware architecture mitigates them.\\

\noindent These challenges demand tighter hardware-algorithm co-design. The non-ideality-aware training above compensates for the device's smooth I-V nonlinearity, but not for the most disruptive failure mode of a large array: the stuck-at faults and device-to-device spread that drive individual neurons into runaway firing. The next subsection adds a biologically inspired firing-rate regulariser that targets exactly this mode, and finds that its benefit depends on the network architecture.

\subsection[Homeostatic Regularisation and its Architectural Ceiling]{Homeostatic Regularisation and its Architectural Ceiling}
\label{sec:6p2p3homeo}

\noindent Memristive devices frequently stick at either $G_{off}$ or $G_{on}$ \cite{joksas2020committee, xia2017stuck}. The fault model used here fixes a stuck realisation once, at programming time, and holds it for the rest of training, as a physically programmed array would. A fixed fraction of synapses, set by the failure rate, is drawn as stuck, while the rest retain their programmed conductance subject to device-to-device variability. A stuck device's conductance is sampled, on the non-negative support $G \geq 0$, from a reflected kernel density estimate (\abbrevdef{KDE}) of the measured conductance distribution \cite{turlach1993bandwidth, jones1993simple}:
\begin{align}
    p(G) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ \mathcal{K}_h(G - G_i) + \mathcal{K}_h(G + G_i) \right] \label{eq:6.41}
\end{align}
\noindent where $\mathcal{K}_h$ is a Gaussian kernel with bandwidth $h$ chosen by Scott's rule \cite{scott2015multivariate}, and the second term reflects each sample at the origin to remove the boundary bias of standard \abbrev{KDE} on a half-bounded variable.\\

\noindent Inaccurate programming also introduces device-to-device (D2D) variability. Resistance deviations follow a lognormal distribution, more pronounced in the high-resistance state (\abbrev{HRS}) than the low-resistance state (\abbrev{LRS}):
\begin{align}
    G_{observed} = G_{target} \exp\left( \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_g^2) \right) \label{eq:6.42}
\end{align}
\noindent The lognormal width $\sigma_g$ is set to a representative spread consistent with the device-to-device programming variability reported for comparable memristors \cite{joksas2022nonideality}, and is held at this nominal value for the baseline experiments below. The multiplicative form of Equation~(\ref{eq:6.42}) preserves positive-definite conductances.\\

\noindent The regulariser is a homeostatic gain control that adjusts synaptic conductance from the presynaptic neuron's firing rate alone, in the spirit of rate-dependent neuromorphic gain control \cite{kim2021spiking, stoffel2024spiking} but here grounded directly in the device: it is the steady state of the Chapter~\ref{ch:model} fill--emission trap balance under mean-rate drive (Equation~\ref{eq:5.15}), with the depression threshold $r_{th}=1/(\kappa\tau_{leak})$ set by the same measured retention $\tau_{leak}$ that fixes the eligibility trace of Chapter~\ref{ch:learning}. The steady-state conductance $G_{steady}$ decreases monotonically (sigmoidally) with the input firing rate $r_{in}$. It stays high at low rates but falls once the rate exceeds a threshold, inducing synaptic depression that suppresses overactive or faulty neurons. Its effect is analogous to dropout, in that it attenuates the contribution of the most active units during training. The mechanism, however, is deterministic divisive gain rather than the stochastic unit-zeroing of conventional dropout. Requiring neither bipolar presynaptic pulses nor access to postsynaptic variables, the rule simplifies circuit design:
\begin{align}
    G_{steady} = \frac{G_{max}}{1 + \left( \dfrac{r_{in}}{r_{th}} \right)^{n_H}} \label{eq:6.43}
\end{align}
\noindent where $G_{max}$ is the maximum conductance and $r_{th}$ the rate at which depression begins. The transition sharpness is not a free hyperparameter: the single screening state of the fill--emission balance (Equation~\ref{eq:5.15}) fixes the Hill exponent $n_H=1$ exactly, the value used here. The gate is therefore parameter-free in its exponent, $G_{steady} = G_{max}/(1 + r_{in}/r_{th})$, and its threshold $r_{th}=1/(\kappa\tau_{leak})$ is set by the measured retention; both come from the device rather than from tuning. (Invoking additional independent screening states would raise $n_H$ above unity and sharpen the turn-on, but a single state suffices to suppress the spike-rate runaway, so $n_H=1$ is retained throughout.) The synapse's output current is the product of $G_{steady}$ and the input voltage,
\begin{align}
    I_{out} = G_{steady}\, V_{in}. \label{eq:6.44}
\end{align}

\noindent A single causal chain explains why this addresses spike-rate runaway. A stuck-low-resistance synapse forces the forward map of Equation~(\ref{eq:6.38}) to its saturated maximum for any input $x$, adding a large weight-independent component to the bitline current of every postsynaptic neuron it drives. That component pushes the \abbrev{LIF} activation above threshold on every time step, so the neuron fires at near-saturation rate and its downstream synapses see a high $r_{in}$. Equation~(\ref{eq:6.43}) attenuates exactly those synapses as $r_{in}^{-n_H}$, breaking runaway at the first downstream layer rather than letting it propagate. The rule's locality also means no postsynaptic feedback path is required.\\

\noindent The mechanism is thus specific to the dense, static regime, where sustained high firing marks a fault rather than a feature. Its action is local: unlike compensation strategies that add hardware redundancy or per-device calibration overhead, the homeostatic rule integrates directly into the synaptic update. That the same suppression reverses on a temporal network is not a shortcoming of the rule but a signpost, that once the firing dynamics carry the computation the transient is better used than suppressed. The next section takes that opposite regime.

\section[The Transient as a Temporal-Integration Primitive]{The Transient as a Temporal-Integration Primitive}
\label{sec:6p5}

\noindent The homeostatic rule of the previous section suppressed the transient, and reached a ceiling once the firing dynamics carried the computation. This section takes the opposite strategy and uses the transient directly. When the network streams a signal in time, the same relaxation becomes the leak of the leaky integrate-and-fire membrane, and its measured retention $\tau_{leak}$ sets a working-memory hold-time fixed by fabrication rather than tuned. The reading is demonstrated first as a scaling relation between retention and bridgeable delay, then on recognised neuromorphic and biosignal benchmarks. Figure~\ref{fig:6liabilityasset} summarises this architectural split.

\subsection[The Device-Grounded LIF Membrane]{The Device-Grounded LIF Membrane}
\label{sec:6p5p2}

\noindent When the network streams a time-varying signal so that its neurons integrate it as it unfolds, the device relaxation becomes a computational primitive. The \abbrev{LIF} membrane of Equation~(\ref{eq:6.1}) is an exponentially leaky accumulator: writing its unit-step relaxation in multiplicative form, $V_m(t) = \beta\, V_m(t-1) + \sum_i w_{ij}\, x_i(t-1)$ with decay factor $\beta = \exp(-\Delta t/\tau_m)$ and the hard reset supplied by the firing rule of Equation~(\ref{eq:6.2}), makes explicit that the membrane retains a fraction $\beta$ of its state each step. Setting its time constant to the device's measured trap-discharge time makes the neuron's temporal memory the device's own relaxation,
\begin{align}
    \beta_{dev} = \exp\!\left( -\frac{\Delta t}{\tau_{leak}} \right), \label{eq:6.45}
\end{align}
\noindent where $\tau_{leak}$ is the field-free trap-discharge time resolved on the \abbrev{ITO}-contacted device, of order a few seconds (Chapter~\ref{ch:model}) \cite{vu2026ito}. The memory \emph{timescale} is then device-measured, while the integrator itself remains the standard leaky accumulator; the grounding is by calibration of the time constant, not reduction of the mechanism. The relevant constant is the discharge time, a decay, not the fill-rise time, which is a driven charging rate and the wrong kind of constant for a membrane leak. The synapses carry the same Poole--Frenkel map of Equation~(\ref{eq:6.38}); only the membrane is changed. A time-series input of length $T$ is streamed slice by slice (Figure~\ref{fig:6t}). Retention is probed by appending $D$ blank steps after the stimulus and reading the class from the output spikes accumulated over the final $R$ steps alone, after the gap,
\begin{align}
    \hat{y} = \arg\max_c \sum_{t=T+D-R+1}^{T+D} s_c^{out}(t), \label{eq:6.46readout}
\end{align}
\noindent so the decision depends on evidence the membrane must hold across $D$ rather than on the stimulus still being present.\\

\subsection[Retention as a Fabrication-Set Hold-Time]{Retention as a Fabrication-Set Hold-Time}
\label{sec:6p5p3}

\noindent The retention sets how long a decision survives the delay. The maximum bridgeable delay $D_{max}(\tau_{leak})$, the largest $D$ at which mean accuracy stays above a per-task criterion ($45\%$ dense, $50\%$ convolutional; chance $10\%$), is measured over 20 seeds. Because these tasks carry no intrinsic physical clock, $\tau_{leak}$ and $D$ share time-step units and $D_{max}/\tau_{leak}$ is a pure ratio; the swept range spans the measured device band up to longer retentions reachable by trap-density and trap-depth engineering of the oxide. On the convolutional network with delayed \abbrevdef{N-MNIST} \cite{orchard2015nmnist}, the hold-time scales linearly with retention,
\begin{align}
    D_{max}(\tau_{leak}) \approx s_D\, \tau_{leak}, \qquad s_D = 6.1,\ R^2 = 0.93. \label{eq:6.47dmax}
\end{align}
\noindent A long-retention membrane ($\tau_{leak}=30$) holds the decision across the whole delay range ($89.9\%$ at no delay, $51.7\%$ at 160 steps), while a short one in the measured band ($\tau_{leak}=1.3$) falls to chance by a 30-step delay and the no-memory control falls at the first blank step (Figure~\ref{fig:6tr}). The decision at zero delay is comparable across membranes; retention buys delay tolerance, not baseline accuracy. The slope $s_D$ is a task-specific quantity, not a universal constant: what is invariant is the linear proportionality to a measured $\tau_{leak}$, and the same relation recurs with a different slope in the reward-modulated learning of Chapter~\ref{ch:learning}. The dense network streaming sequential \abbrev{MNIST} row by row shows the same dependence, monotonic but sublinear ($R^2=0.65$), a fixed startup cost suppressing $D_{max}$ at short retention as the membrane must first hold the streamed digit before any delay begins.\\

\noindent The delay above is appended after the stimulus. On the store--recall benchmark, the canonical spiking working-memory task \cite{bellec2018lsnn}, the delay is intrinsic: a value is presented, a variable number of blank steps follow, and the network must reproduce it on a recall step. Here the informative comparison is not to a shorter membrane but to the mechanism the reference network uses, the adaptive leaky integrate-and-fire (\abbrevdef{ALIF}) neuron \cite{bellec2018lsnn}, whose slow timescale comes from an engineered second per-neuron state: an adaptation variable $b_j$ raised by each spike and decaying with its own long constant $\tau_a$, which lifts the firing threshold,
\begin{align}
    b_j(t) = \rho\, b_j(t-1) + s_j(t-1), \qquad \vartheta_j(t) = V_{th} + \gamma\, b_j(t), \qquad \rho = \exp\!\left( -\frac{\Delta t}{\tau_a} \right), \label{eq:6.48alif}
\end{align}
\noindent so that memory is carried by $b_j$ over $\sim\tau_a$, with $\gamma$ here the threshold-adaptation coupling (following \cite{bellec2018lsnn}), distinct from the I--V nonlinearity ratio of Equation~(\ref{eq:6.34}). The device-grounded neuron instead carries the same slow timescale in the membrane leak itself, with no extra state. The question is whether the device's membrane leak alone reaches the same memory. It does, and already within the measured retention band (Figure~\ref{fig:6sr}). A retention at the top of that band ($\tau_{leak}=8$, within the field-free few-seconds regime) holds the value across the delay range ($94\%$ recall at $D=60$; chance $50\%$) with no added per-neuron state. The equally-parameterised \abbrev{ALIF}, whose adaptation timescale is matched to the device ($\tau_a=8$), instead decays to chance by the longest delay ($51.7\%$ at $D=60$): a same-timescale slow variable does not by itself hold the value, and matching the device's hold requires widening it to $\tau_a=20$. The fast-membrane control and a held-bias-floor retention ($\tau_{leak}=1.3$) fall to chance once the delay exceeds a few steps. Longer retentions ($\tau_{leak}=20$, a fabrication target) hold with no further gain, so the hold-time is set by the measured device rather than by extrapolation. The result holds through the full device stack: replacing the ideal synapse with the measured Poole--Frenkel map, then adding the $30\%$ stuck-off fault prior, leaves the measured-band membrane holding the value ($\tau_{leak}=8$ reaching $87\%$ at $D=60$ under faults), where under the real synapse it matches or exceeds the \abbrev{ALIF} at long delay. The working memory is therefore a property of the device dynamics, not of an idealised synapse, and reaches the reference neuron's hold at the matched timescale where its added variable alone does not.\\

\noindent These are capability results rather than further instances of the hold-time relation. On a signal with an intrinsic physical timescale, a Dynamic Vision Sensor (\abbrevdef{DVS}) gesture spanning about a second at roughly $83$\,ms per frame, the measured-band retentions all correspond to membranes that barely decay over the stimulus, so a retention sweep cannot separate them. The hold-time relation of Equation~(\ref{eq:6.47dmax}) is therefore established on the \abbrev{MNIST}-derived tasks, whose abstract time steps admit the full range of decay factors, while \abbrev{SHD} and \abbrev{DVS128} Gesture show the network operating on recognised biosignal and event-camera data. Two of the four are biological signals, so the device-grounded temporal network's reach is not confined to synthetic tasks.\\

\noindent The learned representation is itself class-structured (Figure~\ref{fig:6sep}). A linear probe on the second convolutional layer's spike features separates the ten \abbrev{N-MNIST} classes at $87\%$ (\abbrevdef{PCA}, held-out) and $80\%$ (\abbrevdef{UMAP}, held-out), well above the $10\%$ chance level, confirming that the network organises its features by class rather than relying on the output layer alone.\\

\section[Summary]{Summary}

This chapter implemented spiking neural networks (SNNs) on silicon-oxide memristive devices, organised around a single principle: the network architecture decides whether the device transient should be suppressed or used. Empirical Poole-Frenkel nonlinearity and device-to-device variability, extracted from metal-insulator-metal (\abbrev{MIM}) $SiO_x$ memristors \cite{Barmpatsalos2021-yn}, were injected directly into the forward and backward passes of a fully connected (\abbrev{MSNN}) and a convolutional (\abbrev{MCSNN}) network trained on \abbrev{MNIST} by backpropagation through time. This nonideality-aware training retained roughly 80\% accuracy despite the injected nonlinearity, against an ideal-device ceiling near 90\%, extending the group's earlier non-ideality compensation in artificial networks \cite{mehonic2019simulation, joksas2020committee, joksas2022nonideality} to event-driven spiking architectures.\\

\noindent In the static classifier the transient is a variability to be suppressed. A homeostatic gain regulariser, reducing synaptic conductance in response to high presynaptic firing, recovers a clear generalisation benefit on fully connected networks ($+2.6$ to $+4.4$\,pp over 20 seeds) but reverses on a temporal-convolutional network ($-8.5$\,pp), where high firing carries the signal rather than marking a fault. That reversal is the signpost to the opposite regime. Streaming a signal in time, the same relaxation becomes the leak of the leaky integrate-and-fire membrane: setting the membrane time constant to the measured trap-discharge time $\tau_{leak}$ \cite{vu2026ito} makes the neuron's memory the device's own. Its retention then sets a working-memory hold-time fixed by fabrication, scaling linearly with $\tau_{leak}$ ($D_{max}\approx6.1\,\tau_{leak}$, $R^2=0.93$), and on the store--recall benchmark the device membrane reaches the working memory of an engineered adaptive neuron with no added per-neuron state, at a matched timescale where that added state alone decays to chance, even through synapses carrying the measured fault prior. The device-grounded temporal network clears recognised neuromorphic and biosignal benchmarks, reaching $93.4\%$ on \abbrev{N-MNIST} and learning cochlea-model speech and event-camera gestures.\\

\noindent Together these results establish a hardware-aware framework that folds experimental memristor data directly into \abbrev{SNN} training and shows the same material property serving, by architecture, as either a variability to suppress or a temporal-integration primitive whose timescale is set at the fabrication line. The measured $\tau_{leak}$ used here as a classifier membrane reappears in Chapter~\ref{ch:learning} as the eligibility trace of reward-modulated learning, in line with the broader roadmap for emerging neuromorphic technologies \cite{mehonic2024review}.
