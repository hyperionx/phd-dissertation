\chapter{Biorealistic Computing}

\section[Spiking Deep Networks]{Spiking Deep Networks}

This section explores biorealistic computing, focusing on implementing Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) with memristive devices. It aims to emulate the brain's energy-efficient processing using electronic circuits while addressing the practical challenges of real-world device imperfections.\\

\noindent Unlike conventional artificial neural networks that rely on continuous, high-precision activations and often ignore underlying neuroscience principles \cite{pfeiffer2018deep, tavanaei2019deep}, neuromorphic architectures communicate via discrete voltage spikes. These systems use electronic devices to faithfully mimic biological cells, with neurons acting as active decision-making units and synapses storing information \cite{mead1989analog}. By encoding data through both spike rate and precise timing, SNNs naturally support biologically plausible learning, drawing far more direct inspiration from nature than traditional machine learning frameworks.

\subsection[From Conventional ANNs to Brain-Inspired Analogies]{From Conventional ANNs to Brain-Inspired Analogies}

Before exploring brain-inspired computing, we must first understand how conventional Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) work. ANNs organize artificial neurons into distinct layers: an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer \cite{stone2019artificial}. In standard designs, like the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), information flows sequentially from one layer to the next.\\

\noindent At its core, each layer performs a mathematical operation called matrix-vector multiplication. The network computes a layer's output by multiplying the input by a set of synaptic weights, adding a bias, and applying a non-linear activation function \cite{pal2010handwritten}. Traditional computers struggle with these massive calculations because they must constantly shuttle large blocks of data between separate memory and processing units. This separation creates the "von Neumann bottleneck," which drastically increases both energy consumption and processing time \cite{zbontar2016stereo}.\\

\noindent The non-linear activation function gives the network the flexibility to model complex real-world data \cite{pomerat2019neural}. Modern deep learning often uses the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function \cite{zhang2018efficient}. ReLU prevents signals from fading out as they pass through many layers, allowing developers to train much deeper networks successfully. To learn, ANNs undergo supervised training to minimize errors \cite{kline2005revisiting}. The backpropagation algorithm acts as the engine for this learning \cite{rumelhart1986learning}. It calculates exactly how much each weight contributed to the final error by working backward from the output layer to the input layer.\\

\noindent Using these calculations, optimization algorithms like Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) tweak the weights to reduce the error. By processing small batches of data over many cycles, SGD helps the network learn general features and accurately classify new data. While conventional ANNs are powerful, their massive energy requirements and memory bottlenecks have pushed researchers to look for more efficient, biologically inspired computing methods.

The human brain processes information through vast networks of neurons. A biological neuron has three main parts: dendrites that receive signals, a soma (cell body) that integrates them, and an axon that transmits them. Neurons connect at tiny junctions called synapses, where the transmitting axon meets the receiving dendrite. \\

\noindent Synapses use both chemical and electrical signals to communicate. When a transmitting neuron fires an electrical spike (an action potential), it releases chemical messengers into the synapse. These chemicals create a temporary voltage in the receiving dendrite. As these voltage changes travel to the soma, they alter its electrical balance. If the total voltage crosses a certain threshold, the soma fires its own spike down its axon. The strength of this connection—how easily the signal transfers—represents the synaptic weight. The brain dynamically adjusts this weight as it learns. Figure \ref{fig:6a} illustrates this chemical and electrical signaling network. \\

\noindent Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) replicate this efficient, event-driven biological process in artificial intelligence. Unlike conventional ANNs that use continuous numbers to communicate, SNNs use discrete voltage spikes. To build this brain-like structure in hardware, engineers combine standard computer chips (CMOS devices) with specialized components called memristors. 

\noindent Memristors change their electrical resistance based on the history of the current that has passed through them. This memory-like behavior makes them perfect artificial synapses; they can adjust their conductance to represent changing synaptic weights \cite{chang2011short}. Figure \ref{fig:6b} shows this hybrid architecture, where CMOS circuits act as the neurons and dense grids of memristors serve as the synapses \cite{saighi2015plasticity}.

The schematic illustrates a feed-forward neural network comprising three layers of CMOS neurons interconnected by two fully connected memristive synaptic crossbars \cite{saighi2015plasticity}.}
    \label{fig:6b}
\end{figure}

\noindent In these circuits, CMOS neurons mimic biological behavior \cite{prezioso2016self}. The total current reaching a receiving neuron equals the sum of the currents passing through its connected memristors. If this accumulated current crosses a set threshold, the CMOS neuron fires a spike. This hardware setup enables networks to learn much like a biological brain. For example, if a transmitting neuron fires right before a receiving neuron, the overlapping pulses increase the memristor's conductance, strengthening the artificial synapse. If the firing order reverses, the conductance decreases. This mechanism allows the hardware to adapt and learn continuously without relying on external memory.

\subsection[The Spiking Computation Paradigm and Memristive Integration]{The Spiking Computation Paradigm and Memristive Integration}

Deep neural networks (DNNs) have transformed artificial intelligence, mastering tasks from image recognition \cite{krizhevsky2012imagenet} to natural language processing. However, these networks rely on "rate-based" neurons. They communicate using continuous numbers (like decimals) that represent an average firing rate, ignoring the precise timing of individual impulses. Translating these continuous models into discrete, event-driven spiking networks presents a major research challenge.\\

\noindent Researchers transition to spiking neurons for two main reasons. First, running large networks on spiking hardware saves massive amounts of energy, enabling real-time processing on mobile and robotic platforms. Second, spiking networks allow engineers to incorporate more brain-like features into machine learning models.\\

\noindent Understanding how the brain learns remains difficult due to complex neuronal behaviors, like shifting firing thresholds and variable spike timing. While artificial spiking networks do not perfectly replicate biology, they face identical challenges: efficiently processing sparse, noisy, and uncoordinated events. Solving these challenges helps developers build more biologically realistic computing systems.\\

\noindent Spiking networks transmit information using discrete spikes, introducing a critical dimension of time missing in standard DNNs. A spiking neuron's behavior evolves; it fires at certain times and stays quiet at others. You must observe it over time to understand its activity. In contrast, standard DNNs calculate an instantaneous, static output the moment they receive an input.\\

\noindent Furthermore, artificial spiking networks treat all spikes as identical events. While biological spikes vary in shape, artificial models simplify them into uniform pulses. As a result, a spike conveys information solely through when it happens. Networks can encode this timing in two ways: rate coding (counting spikes within a set time window) or timing coding (tracking the exact moment each spike occurs).\\

\noindent Spiking networks also exhibit inherent variability. A standard ANN neuron given a constant input produces a constant output. A spiking neuron, however, outputs a sequence of discrete spikes. When passed through a synapse, this sequence creates an oscillating, dynamic signal. Thus, even with constant input, a spiking network constantly fluctuates. Standard DNNs often use an activation function like ReLU to represent a firing rate. SNNs approach this differently. To classify data, an SNN might identify the output neuron that fires most frequently or the one that spikes first.\\

\noindent At the microscopic level, synapses connect neurons. Excitatory synapses encourage the receiving neuron to fire, while inhibitory synapses suppress firing \cite{kandel2000principles}. The synaptic weight dictates the strength of this push or pull. While traditional ANNs freeze these weights after training, SNNs adjust them dynamically over time, enabling continuous, on-the-fly learning \cite{tavanaei2019deep}.\\

\noindent Figure \ref{fig:6c} illustrates how a spiking neuron operates over time. A neuron receives incoming spikes, each modified by a synaptic weight. The neuron accumulates these weighted inputs as electrical charge. If the accumulated charge crosses a specific threshold, the neuron fires its own spike, resets its internal voltage, and enters a brief "refractory period" where it cannot fire again \cite{wu2017enabling}. Mathematically, Equation \ref{eq:6.1} models this by adding weighted inputs to the previous membrane potential and subtracting a continuous leak. 
\begin{equation}
    V_m(t) = V_m(t-1) + \sum_{i} w_{i,j} \times x_{i,j}(t-1) - \lambda
    \label{eq:6.1}
\end{equation}

\noindent Equation \ref{eq:6.2} defines the firing rule: the neuron fires if the voltage exceeds the threshold and the refractory period has passed. While the Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) model simplifies biological complexity \cite{lapique1907researches}, it is highly efficient for hardware implementation compared to complex biological models \cite{hodgkin1952quantitative, izhikevich2003simple}.
\begin{equation}
    x(t) = 
    \begin{cases} 
    1 & \text{if } V_m(t) > V_{th} \text{ and } t - t_{spike} > T_R \\ 
    0 & \text{otherwise} 
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:6.2}
\end{equation}

\noindent A major challenge lies in converting continuous real-world data into discrete spikes. Rate-coding converts analog values into firing frequencies (Equation \ref{eq:6.3}) \cite{gerstner1997neural}. 
\begin{equation}
    X(t) = 
    \begin{cases} 
    1 & \text{if } x < u \\ 
    0 & \text{otherwise} 
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:6.3}
\end{equation}

\noindent However, because it averages data over time, rate-coding destroys the precise timing of individual spikes—a critical feature in biological processing \cite{chase2006spike}. Alternatively, temporal coding schemes like Time-to-First-Spike (TTFS) or Inter-Spike Interval (ISI) encode information directly into the exact timing of the spikes, preserving this rich temporal structure \cite{gerstner2014neuronal}.

\noindent Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) adapt traditional deep learning by enforcing biological constraints, such as discrete communication and local learning rules. These constraints fundamentally change how networks compute, drastically improving speed and efficiency \cite{tavanaei2019deep, wang2020supervised}. Neuromorphic hardware—circuits built to mimic the nervous system—significantly reduces energy consumption \cite{wunderlich2019demonstrating}. Instead of using power-hungry, high-precision numbers, these systems encode information in the precise timing and frequency of voltage spikes \cite{zhou2020towards}.\\

\noindent While the backpropagation algorithm successfully trains traditional deep networks \cite{werbos1990backpropagation}, it fails in SNNs because discrete spikes lack the smooth mathematical derivatives required for gradient calculations. Researchers solve this using "surrogate gradients." They use actual spikes during forward processing but swap in a smooth mathematical approximation during the backward learning phase \cite{neftci2019surrogate}. Specialized software packages now support these SNNs \cite{hazan2018bindsnet}, leveraging GPUs to accelerate training significantly \cite{pehle2021norse}.\\

\noindent At the physical level, memristors change their conductance based on the movement of charged atoms. This allows them to naturally implement biological learning rules like Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (STDP) \cite{serrano2013stdp, lin2020adaptive}. Engineers also use memristors to build "neuristors"—artificial neurons that fire sudden voltage spikes \cite{lim2015reliability, bao2019dual, del2020caloritronics, hao2020monolayer, kang2021build, zhou2022fully}. Furthermore, simple resistors can emulate how biological cells gradually leak charge over time \cite{loeffler2021modularity, hochstetter2021avalanches, zhu2021mnist, zhu2020memristor}.\\

\noindent Architecturally, memristor arrays (RRAM) excel at "in-memory computing." They can perform massive matrix multiplications instantly and in parallel \cite{li2018analog, eshraghian20213}. Generally, developers train these networks offline and then program the fixed weights into the memristors for deployment \cite{zhang2020brain}. However, moving analog signals across standard computer chips requires expensive Analog-to-Digital Converters (ADCs). Spike-based systems bypass this problem entirely; because spikes are simple binary pulses, they eliminate the need for power-hungry ADCs \cite{eshraghian2022memristor, cai2019fully}. A few advanced designs successfully integrate both memristive neurons and memristive synapses to perform fully data-driven operations \cite{wang2018fully, tang2020fully}.\\       

\noindent Allowing hardware to operate naturally using its own physics, rather than a rigid digital clock, introduces challenges. The system becomes susceptible to chaotic ionic dynamics. Relying strictly on local learning rules (like STDP) restricts these fully memristive networks to solving simple, low-resolution pattern recognition tasks.\\

\noindent Researchers have explored numerous brain-inspired architectures \cite{kang2021build}. For example, one team built an 8×8 grid combining memristive neurons and synapses, successfully training it to recognize simple pixel patterns using STDP \cite{wang2018fully}. Another approach used half-wave rectification between analog crossbars to transmit signals without digital conversion, achieving over 93\% accuracy on handwriting recognition tasks \cite{kiani2021fully}.\\

\noindent Some teams pre-train standard networks and convert them to spiking models \cite{wang2018handwritten, wijesinghe2018all}, but these often struggle with complex, real-world data. To overcome the limitations of local learning, developers can train memristive SNNs using surrogate gradient descent. This method unrolls the network's behavior over time, allowing the algorithm to optimize the memristors based on the continuous dynamics of the artificial neurons \cite{zhou2022memristive, neftci2019surrogate}. However, successfully mapping these mathematically optimized models onto physical crossbars requires accurately modeling and overcoming the inherent imperfections of the devices themselves, necessitating rigorous nonideality simulations.\\


\section[Nonidealities Simulation]{Nonidealities Simulation}

\noindent Neuromorphic modeling computationally simulates artificial systems that emulate biological neural networks to predict their physical hardware behavior. Coined in the late 1980s \cite{mead1990neuromorphic}, "neuromorphic" describes hardware organized structurally like the brain. A foundational concept is parallel distributed processing. These systems arrange computations at the neural level to emphasize rapid inter-unit communication, contrasting with conventional graphics processing units (GPUs) optimized for independent parallel computations with minimal communication.

\subsection[Local Plasticity and Neuromorphic Learning Rules]{Local Plasticity and Neuromorphic Learning Rules}

\noindent Synapses undergo structural and functional changes via synaptic plasticity. In neural systems, spike timing determines synaptic strength, forming the basis for learning and memory. Two principal forms of synaptic plasticity exist: long-term plasticity (LTP) \cite{bear1994synaptic} and short-term plasticity (STP) \cite{zucker2002short}. Synapses can strengthen or weaken, retaining memory over extended periods (Long-Term Facilitation (LTF) or Long-Term Depression (LTD)). More transient changes are Short-Term Facilitation (STF) or Short-Term Depression (STD). While standard deep neural network (DNN) training mimics LTP by optimizing a unified weight matrix through error backpropagation, actual neurobiological learning rules differ significantly.\\

\noindent The Hebbian rule \cite{hebb2002organization}, famously summarized as "neurons that fire together, wire together" \cite{shatz1992developing}, relies on simultaneous neuron activity \cite{gerstner2014neuronal}. It defines a localized rate model:
\begin{align}
\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = F\left( w_{ij},M,v^{prev}_j,v^{post}_i \right) \label{eq:6.4} 
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $w_{ij}$ is the synaptic weight, $M$ represents a neuromodulator (e.g., dopamine gating synaptic plasticity), $v^{prev}_j$ is the presynaptic firing rate, and $v^{post}_i$ is the postsynaptic firing rate. A Taylor expansion of Equation \ref{eq:6.4} with respect to the rate yields:
\begin{align}
\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = a_0({w_{ij},M}) + a_1({w_{ij},M})^{prev}v^{prev}_j + a_1({w_{ij},M})^{post}v^{post}_i + a_2({w_{ij},M})^{corr}v^{prev}_jv^{post}_i + ... \label{eq:6.5} 
\end{align}

\noindent The zeroth-order term $a_0$ represents background weight decay absent any spikes. The first-order coefficients $a^{prev}_1$ and $a^{post}_1$ scale the contributions of isolated presynaptic and postsynaptic activity, respectively. The second-order coefficient $a^{corr}_2$ dictates the true Hebbian correlation derived from simultaneous firing. The ellipsis represents higher-order terms. All coefficients depend on the current synaptic weight $w_{ij}$ and neuromodulator $M$. Depending on these variables, the Hebbian rule induces either LTF or LTD, functioning as a flexible framework rather than a rigid rule.\\

\noindent Spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) adjusts synaptic weights based on the precise time interval between pre- and postsynaptic spikes. Equation \ref{eq:6.6} defines the total weight change from neuron $j$ to neuron $i$:
\begin{align}
\Delta w_{ij} = \sum_{n}^{}\sum_{f}^{} W\left( t_i^n - t_j^f \right)\label{eq:6.6} 
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $t_i^n$ denotes the $n$-th postsynaptic spike time, and $t_j^f$ denotes the $f$-th presynaptic spike time. $W(x)$ defines the STDP learning window. The prevalent pair-based STDP variant dictates:
\begin{align}
W_+ (x) &= \left\{ A_+(w)e^{-\left| \Delta t \right| / \tau_+} \right\}  \quad \forall  t_{post} \in t_{prev} < t_{post} \label{eq:6.7} \\
W_- (x) &= \left\{ A_-(w)e^{-\left| \Delta t \right| / \tau_-} \right\}  \quad \forall  t_{prev} \in t_{post} < t_{prev} \label{eq:6.8}
\end{align}

\noindent where $\Delta t = t_{post} - t_{prev}$. The amplitude parameters $A_+(w) > 0$ and $A_-(w) < 0$ determine the magnitude of LTF and LTD, respectively, and often depend on the current weight. \\

\noindent By expressing the presynaptic and postsynaptic spike trains as $S_j = \sum_{f} \delta( t - t_j^f )$ and $S_i = \sum_{n} \delta( t - t_i^n )$, respectively, the pair-based STDP rule reformulates as:
\begin{align}
\frac{dx_j}{dt} &= \sum_{f}^{}\delta\left( t - t_j^f \right) - \frac{x_j}{\tau_+} = S_j(t) - - \frac{x_j}{\tau_+} \label{eq:6.9} \\
\frac{dy_i}{dt} &= \sum_{n}^{}\delta\left( t - t_i^n \right) - \frac{y_j}{\tau_-} = S_i(t) - \frac{y_j}{\tau_-} \label{eq:6.10} 
\end{align}

\noindent The variables $x_j$ and $y_i$ act as biochemical traces left by pre- and postsynaptic spikes, corresponding to the exponential decay terms $e^{-|\Delta t| / \tau_+}$ and $e^{-|\Delta t| / \tau_-}$, yielding:
\begin{align}
\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = A_+ w_{ij}x_i(t)S_i(t) + A_-w_{ij}y_i(t)S_j(t) \label{eq:6.11} 
\end{align}

\noindent The right-hand terms denote the pre-before-post and post-before-pre effects, respectively. For independent Poisson inputs, STDP aligns directly with rate models \cite{gerstner2014neuronal}:
\begin{align}
\frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = \int_{0}^{+\infty } W(-s)\epsilon(s)ds\cdot v_{j}^{prev} + \int_{-\infty }^{+\infty } W(s)ds \cdot v_j^{prev}v_i^{post} \label{eq:6.12}
\end{align}

\noindent The first term integrates the causal "pre-before-post" learning window, where $W$ is the weighting function and $\epsilon(s)$ defines the postsynaptic potential time course for $s > 0$. Comparing Equations \ref{eq:6.5} and \ref{eq:6.12} shows that STDP defines the $a_1^{prev}$ and $a_2^{corr}$ coefficients, nullifying the rest. Non-volatile memristors natively emulate LTP synapses by retaining updated memristance states. Arranged in an $m \times n$ crossbar array, these devices perform physical analog vector-matrix multiplication. Input row voltages scale by each memristor's conductance (Ohm's law), and the resulting currents accumulate along the columns (Kirchhoff's current law).\\

\noindent Gradient backpropagation—a global optimization algorithm updating weights via backward error propagation—dominates DNNs, while STDP dominates SNNs. Memristive crossbars support both training and inference paradigms \cite{hasan2017chip, ankit2019puma}.\\

\noindent Backpropagation requires complex external digital circuitry to calculate gradients and sequence update pulses, introducing substantial overhead. Conversely, STDP naturally maps to memristor physics \cite{maranhao2021low, yakopcic2013generalized}. Figure \ref{fig:6e} illustrates a memristor connecting two neurons. Pre- and postsynaptic spikes generate a precise voltage difference across the device, updating its memristance strictly based on the spike timing interval.\\

\noindent While most research focuses on non-volatile memristors for LTP, volatile memristors effectively emulate short-term plasticity (STP), which governs critical functions like motion detection, speech recognition, and working memory \cite{ghanbari2017estimating}. Unlike LTP, STP depends heavily on presynaptic spiking activity. Defining $P_{release}$ as the fraction of neurotransmitter released allows us to model both STF and STD directly through its dynamics:
\begin{align}
\frac{dP_{release}}{dt} = \frac{P_{release} - P_0}{\tau_F} + f_F(1 - P_{release})\sum_{f}\delta\left( t- t^f \right) \label{eq:6.13}
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $t^f$ is the presynaptic spike time, $P_0$ is the resting value, $\tau_F$ governs the recovery timescale, and $f_F$ controls facilitation. Similarly, STD is defined as:
\begin{align}
\frac{dP_{release}}{dt} = \frac{P_{release} - P_0}{\tau_D} + f_D(1 - P_{release})\sum_{f}\delta\left( t- t^f \right) \label{eq:6.14}
\end{align}

\noindent Volatile memristors naturally emulate STF and STD by retaining conductance changes only briefly. Paired-Pulse Facilitation (PPF) and Paired-Pulse Depression (PPD) metrics measure these effects. The index $\frac{A_2}{A_1}$ computes PPF/PPD strength, where $A_1$ and $A_2$ are absolute amplitudes of the excitatory (EPSC) or inhibitory postsynaptic currents (IPSC) from successive spikes. For instance, a single-layered molybdenum disulfide ($MoS_2$) memristor leverages Joule heating to achieve this volatile conductance modulation \cite{sun2018synaptic}.\\

\noindent Alternatively, the supervised spike-rate-dependent plasticity (SRDP) rule trains SNNs with high hardware compatibility. Supervised SRDP overcomes the accuracy limitations of unsupervised methods by incorporating global error signals \cite{pfeiffer2018deep}:
\begin{align}
\Delta_{ij} &= \begin{cases}
+1 & \text{ if } f_{r_j} < f_{t_j} \text{ \& } f_{s_i} > \text{ threshold }\\ 
-1 & \text{ if } f_{r_j} < f_{t_j} \text{ \& } f_{s_i} < \text{ threshold } \\
-2 & \text{ if } f_{r_j} > f_{t_j} \text{ \& } f_{s_i} > \text{ threshold }
\end{cases} \label{eq:6.15}
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $f_s$, $f_r$, and $f_t$ represent the output frequencies of the sensory, relay, and teaching neurons, respectively. The discrete update command $\Delta_{ij}$ dictates the voltage pulses applied to the synapses connecting the $i$-th sensory and $j$-th relay neurons.\\

\noindent The rule evaluates the relay neuron's output against the teaching frequency. If the relay neuron fires too slowly ($f_{r_j} < f_{t_j}$), the network strengthens pathways from highly active sensory neurons ($f_{s_i} > \text{threshold}$) via $\Delta_{ij} = +1$. It weakens pathways from insufficiently active neurons ($f_{s_i} < \text{threshold}$) via $\Delta_{ij} = -1$. If the relay neuron fires too fast ($f_{r_j} > f_{t_j}$), it has incorrectly learned alternative features; the network aggressively erases these features by decreasing the weight ($\Delta_{ij} = -2$).\\

\noindent Because physical memristors strictly possess positive conductance, hardware implementations use differential pairs to encode signed weights. A $\Delta_{ij} = +1$ command pulses the 'negative' memristor to increase the effective weight, while a $-1$ or $-2$ command pulses the 'positive' memristor to decrease it. A global loss function evaluates overall training progress by measuring the normalized discrepancy between actual and target frequencies. For a single training sample $k$, the error $l_k$ across all $j$ relay neurons is:
\begin{align}
l_k &= \frac{\sum_{j}\left( f_{r,j} - f_{t,j} \right)^2}{\sum_{j}\left( f_{t,j} \right)^2} \label{eq:6.16}\\
Loss &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}l_k \label{eq:6.17}
\end{align}

\noindent Here, $f_{r,j}$ and $f_{t,j}$ represent the actual and target output frequencies for the $j$-th relay neuron. The system computes the individual error $l_k$ and averages it across all $N$ training samples to determine the total $Loss$. The algorithm continually applies local SRDP updates and terminates once the $Loss$ falls below a set threshold.

\subsection[Memristive Array Architectures and Continuous-Time Dynamics]{Memristive Array Architectures and Continuous-Time Dynamics}

\noindent Nanoscale non-volatile memory (NVM) devices and STDP mechanisms enable highly parallel, brain-inspired computing \cite{zamarreno2011spike, querlioz2012bioinspired}. Resistive random-access memory (RRAM) synapses offer particularly low state-transition energy and a compact physical footprint \cite{yu2011electronic}. A multi-layer Spiking Deep Convolutional Neural Network (SDCNN) encodes real-valued inputs into spatiotemporal spike patterns \cite{kheradpisheh2018stdp}. Subsequent convolutional layers extract features using STDP-based unsupervised learning. Figure \ref{fig:6f} illustrates an RRAM crossbar array connecting two neuronal layers. Spiking neurons implement competitive learning via a winner-take-all (WTA) shared bus mechanism, where the first neuron to spike inhibits all others in the layer \cite{markram2012spike}. STDP and WTA learning rules combine to update synapses locally based on pre- and post-synaptic spike interactions, adapting weights natively in hardware.\\

\noindent This dense 2D array architecture mimics standard memory layouts and forms a repeatable motif that scales to deeper SNNs. Through-silicon-vias (TSVs) extend this scaling to 3D integrated circuits. Because TSVs provide massive direct vertical connectivity between stacked layers, this high-density integration avoids the routing and encoding overhead of asynchronous address-event representation (AER) protocols \cite{jo2009programmable}.\\

\noindent While analog-like weights optimize STDP learning \cite{gaba2013stochastic}, most practical small RRAM devices \cite{soni2011stochastic} exhibit abrupt, digital-like switching between resistance states \cite{yu2012switching}. This limits stable synaptic resolution to a single bit \cite{suri2013bio}. Device switching probabilities also depend heavily on applied voltage and pulse duration \cite{li2014stochastic}. To circumvent binary resolution, developers utilize compound memristive synapses consisting of multiple parallel bistable devices \cite{bill2014compound}. By probabilistically switching a fraction of these components, the aggregated conductance emulates a continuous, analog weight on a macroscopic scale.\\

\noindent Theoretical studies confirm that STDP enables unsupervised local learning by implementing Bayesian expectation-maximization \cite{nessler2013bayesian} and hidden Markov models \cite{kappel2014stdp}. Nonlinear STDP windows ensure computational stability and efficiency \cite{sprekeler2007slowness, toyoizumi2004spike}. Nanoscale memristors like phase-change memory (PCM) \cite{kang2015emulation}, RRAM \cite{mandal2014novel}, and spin-torque-transfer RAM (STT-RAM) \cite{sengupta2015spin} naturally exhibit STDP-like switching characteristics \cite{krzysteczko2012memristive, li2013ultrafast}. They provide sub-pico-Joule switching energy, full CMOS compatibility, and a dense $4F^2$ footprint (where $F$ is the feature size) \cite{chen2016review}.\\

\noindent RRAMs directly implement STDP through overlapping pairwise spikes. A presynaptic spike followed by a postsynaptic spike generates a net potential $V_{net}$ exceeding the positive threshold $V_{th}^+$, thereby increasing conductance. Conversely, an earlier postsynaptic spike creates a potential crossing the negative threshold $V_{th}^-$, decreasing conductance. While analog resistance optimizes STDP, experimental RRAMs suffer from stochastic filament formation. This causes abrupt transitions between the high resistance state (HRS) and low resistance state (LRS) and introduces significant variations in switching threshold voltages \cite{prakash2016multilevel}.\\

\noindent This intrinsic stochasticity severely limits stable synaptic resolution. To scale MSNN complexity, researchers apply gradient descent directly to the nonlinear state evolution of memristive components \cite{zhou2022memristive}. The memristive integrate-and-fire (MIF) neuron model achieves distinct depolarization, hyperpolarization, and repolarization phases using minimal circuit elements. Because STDP lacks a global credit assignment mechanism, applying it to deep networks is difficult. MSNNs solve this by interconnecting dynamic MIF neurons with memristive synapses and training them via gradient descent \cite{neftci2019surrogate}. The analog spiking characteristics of MIF neurons circumvent non-differentiable activations, enabling efficient gradient-based optimization.\\

\noindent To implement a fully memristive SNN physically, the MIF neuron's voltage response must drive the memristive synapses. This maps perfectly to a crossbar array:
\begin{align}
\begin{matrix}
& \textbf{G} & \\
& \begin{bmatrix}
    w_{1,1} & w_{1,2} & \cdots & w_{1, m} \\
    w_{2,1} & w_{2,2} & \cdots & w_{2, m} \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\
    w_{n, 1} & w_{n, 2} & \cdots & w_{n, m}
\end{bmatrix} &\\
\end{matrix}
\begin{matrix}
  & \textbf{V} & \\
 & \begin{bmatrix}
V_{1} \\
V_{2} \\
\vdots \\
V_{n}
\end{bmatrix} &\\
\end{matrix}
\begin{matrix}
 & = & \\
\\
\\
&=&  \\
\\
\end{matrix}
\begin{matrix}
  & \textbf{I} & \\
 & \begin{bmatrix}
I_{1} \\
I_{2} \\
\vdots \\
I_{m}
\end{bmatrix} &\\
\end{matrix}
\label{eq:6.18}
\end{align}


\noindent Here, \textbf{\textit{I}} is the output current vector, \textbf{\textit{V}} is the input voltage vector, and \textbf{\textit{G}} is the conductance matrix.
\begin{align}
I_n = \sum_{i=1}^{m} v_i \cdot w_{n,i} \label{eq:6.19}
\end{align}

\noindent The output current vector $\textbf{I}$ accumulates along the bitlines and drives the next MIF neuron layer. Buffers or scaling factors effectively mitigate resistive loading attenuation \cite{wang2020high, wang2022low}.
\begin{align}
\tau_{syn} \frac{dI}{dt} &= a - I \label{eq:6.20} \\
\tau_{syn}\frac{da}{dt} &= W_i \cdot\sum_{n}\delta\left( t - t_i^n \right) \label{eq:6.21}
\end{align}

\noindent Neural coding models often idealize spike trains as sums of time-shifted Dirac delta pulses, $\sum_{n}\delta\left( t - t_i^n \right)$, where $\delta$ is the Dirac delta function and $t_i^n$ denotes the $n$-th spike of the $i$-th neuron. To align with physical systems, this discrete spike train modulates a continuous alpha input current $I$ via Equations \ref{eq:6.20} and \ref{eq:6.21}. Here, $a$ represents an internal state variable (e.g., filament length), $\tau_{syn}$ dictates the alpha current shape, and $W_i$ is the synaptic weight. This continuous alpha waveform accurately approximates biological graded potentials in the sensory periphery, effectively translating discrete spikes into analog physical dynamics \cite{eshraghian2018formulation}.
\begin{align}
I^{t+1} &=  I^t + \frac{a^t - I^t}{\tau_{syn}} \label{eq:6.22} \\
a^{t+1} &= a^t - \frac{a^t}{\tau_{syn}} + \sum_{n}\delta\left( t - t_i^n \right)\label{eq:6.23}
\end{align}


\noindent To train this network via gradient descent, the continuous dynamics are discretized (Equations \ref{eq:6.22} and \ref{eq:6.23}). This unfolds the memristive state in a computational graph similar to a recurrent neural network (RNN). Standard SPICE simulators use complex solvers like backward Euler, but ensuring compatibility with backpropagation through time (BPTT) requires the forward Euler method to compute the next time step explicitly. The unrolled MSNN allows gradient descent to optimize memristive synapses against MIF evolution. A loss function evaluates the output layer's continuous membrane potential $v$ at each time step. SNN training requires retaining intermediate states to guide the learning process accurately, making BPTT the optimal choice over memory-efficient alternatives like the continuous-time adjoint sensitivity method \cite{chen2018neural}.\\

\noindent During supervised learning, the algorithm maximizes the target neuron's spike frequency while suppressing incorrect outputs. Error backpropagation applies directly to the continuous membrane voltages \cite{neftci2019surrogate}. BPTT iteratively applies the chain rule backward through time and layers to calculate synaptic gradients \cite{eshraghian2023training}. Treating mathematical weights $w$ as physical conductances maps these updates directly to the RRAM crossbars, providing a highly biorealistic model for hardware-accelerated learning.

\subsection[Hardware-Aware Conductance Mapping]{Hardware-Aware Conductance Mapping}

\noindent Mathematical optimization of MSNNs typically assumes ideal components. Physically mapping weights onto memristor crossbars introduces challenges because physical memristors exhibit stuck-at faults, random telegraph noise (RTN), and highly nonlinear current-voltage (I-V) characteristics. To mitigate non-idealities, hardware modifications like compensation circuitry \cite{ambrogio2018equivalent, li2018analogue} or complex programming schemes \cite{xia2017stuck, chen2017accelerator, jain2019cxdnn, wang2020ssm, li2019build} introduce significant overhead \cite{amirsoleimani2020memory, cai2019fully}. Alternatively, hardware-agnostic algorithmic solutions, such as committee machines (CMs), neutralize noise by averaging outputs across parallel ensembles \cite{joksas2020committee}. Ex-situ training techniques also build resilience by modifying loss functions \cite{zhu2020statistical} or injecting noise \cite{he2019noise, joshi2020accurate}. Simple noise injection fails to model complex I-V nonlinearity adequately. To develop a robust ex-situ methodology, this research embeds empirical characteristics from a physical $SiO_x$ memristor directly into the training loop.\\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
\centering    
\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Chapter6/Figs/g.png}
\caption[Single sweeps of 53 resistance states of a $SiO_x$ device.]{Single sweeps of 53 resistance states of a $SiO_x$ device. Different states were obtained by incrementing maximum voltage by 0.05V. Every seventh state shares the same colour; this does not indicate any other relationship between such states \cite{Barmpatsalos2021-yn}.}
\label{fig:6g}
\end{figure}

\noindent The modeled $SiO_x$ device features a 35 nm amorphous silicon oxide layer between a 280 nm $Mo$ bottom electrode and a 115 nm $Au$ top electrode, with a 3 nm $Ti$ wetting layer. After electroforming and initial cyclic stress tests, positive voltage sweeps generated diverse resistance states spanning multiple orders of magnitude. Integrating these characteristics requires modifying network activation calculations. Standard ex-situ training assumes a perfectly linear relationship between inputs ($x_i$) and weights ($w_{ij}$):
\begin{align}
y_j = f\left( \sum_{i=1}^{M} x_i, w_{ij} \right) \label{eq:6.24}
\end{align}

\noindent Because non-Ohmic behavior occurs individually per device, a nonlinear function $g$ replaces this linear product to encapsulate specific hardware characteristics \cite{joksas2022nonideality}:
\begin{align}
y_j = f\left( \sum_{i=1}^{M} g \left( x_i, w_{ij} \right) \right) \label{eq:6.25}
\end{align}

\noindent Function $g$ must also reflect the mapping from mathematical weights to physical conductances. Scaling factors $k_V$ and $k_I$ map network inputs and outputs to physical voltages and currents, while $k_G$ maps weights to the hardware's available conductance range $[G_{min}, G_{max}]$:
\begin{align}
V &= k_V x \label{eq:6.26} \\
y &= \frac{I}{k_I} = \frac{I}{k_V k_G} \label{eq:6.27} \\
k_G &= \frac{G_{max} - G_{min}}{max |\mathbf{W}|} = \frac{G_{on} - G_{off}}{max |\mathbf{W}|}\label{eq:6.28}
\end{align}

\noindent Because conductances are non-negative, standard architectures use differential pairs to encode signed weights. A weight represents the difference between conductances $G_+$ and $G_-$ on positive and negative bitlines. An intuitive scheme assigns these symmetrically around the average conductance ($G_{avg}$):
\begin{align}
G\pm &= G_{avg} \pm \frac{k_G w}{2} \label{eq:6.29} \\
G_{avg} &= \frac{G_{off} + G_{on}}{2} \label{eq:6.30}
\end{align}

\noindent Because infinite pairs yield the same difference \cite{kim20214k}, this ambiguity can minimize static power by keeping at least one device in its high-resistance OFF state ($G_{off}$):
\begin{align}
G_+ &= G_{off} + max\left\{ 0, k_GW \right\} \label{eq:6.31} \\
G_- &= G_{off} - min\left\{ 0, k_GW \right\} \label{eq:6.32}
\end{align}

\noindent An alternative 'double weights' approach trains two distinct sets of non-negative weights, $W^+$ and $W^-$, independently \cite{kendall2020training}. The activation function computes their difference naturally:
\begin{align}
y_j = f\left( \sum_{i=1}^{M} g\left( x_i,W_{i,j}^+ \right) - g\left( x_i,W_{i,j}^- \right) \right) \label{eq:6.33}
\end{align}

\noindent These non-negative weights map linearly to physical conductances $G_+$ and $G_-$, avoiding mapping gaps:
\begin{align}
G_{\pm} = k_GW_{\pm} + G_{off} \label{eq:6.34}
\end{align}

\noindent Double-weight formulation exposes physical conductances directly to the training algorithm. If non-idealities like programming deviations \cite{kim2016voltage} dominate specific conductance ranges, gradient descent naturally selects optimal pairs to minimize these negative effects, maximizing network robustness.

\section{Inference and Classification}


In spiking neural networks (SNNs), neurons facilitate information exchange through spike emission. This phenomenon represents a neural network architecture predicated on individual spikes \cite{liu2014memristor}. The SNN architecture is thus structurally analogous to the human brain. Signals in SNNs can utilize both pulse coding and rate coding, permitting multiplexing of information as frequency and amplitude. In certain electronic SNN forms, spikes exhibit a waveform shape analogous to biological spikes. However, in general electronic systems, spikes are represented in a far more simplified manner, typically as a square or Gaussian digital pulse.\\

\noindent In SNNs, the presence and timing of individual spikes are considered the means of communication and neural computation. The fundamental biological principle asserts that greater input intensity leads to earlier spike transmission. Therefore, it is possible to design a network of spiking neurons with input neurons whose firing times are determined by an external stimulus \cite{serrano2013stdp}.

\subsection[Nonidealities Calibrations]{Nonidealities Calibrations}

\noindent In the simulation presented in this chapter, a combination of experimental data from a $SiO_x$ device, models based on simplified assumptions regarding breaking-down devices, and findings from the literature on programming variability were employed. I-V nonlinearities have been identified as a prevalent method for characterizing deviations from ohmic behavior in memristive devices.
\begin{align}
\gamma \equiv \frac{G\left( 2V_{ref} \right)}{G\left( V_{ref} \right)}  \label{eq:6.35}
\end{align}


\noindent This approach involves examining two points on an I-V curve \cite{lentz2013current}, as previously discussed (\ref{eq:6.32}). This equation defines conductance linearity, quantifying nonlinear I-V behavior. A conductance linearity of 1 indicates ohmic behavior; any deviation indicates I-V nonlinearity. While useful for describing non-ohmic behavior at different voltages, it is more challenging for modeling purposes \cite{sung2018effect}.
\begin{align}
I & = V_{ref}G\left ( \frac{V}{V_{ref}} \right )^{log_2 \gamma} \label{eq:6.36}
\end{align}

\noindent To construct an I-V curve from a given nonlinearity parameter $\gamma$, it is possible to assume that the equality in Equation (\ref{eq:6.32}) holds for all $V$, rather than just $V_{ref}$. This would result in a relationship between current and voltage, as shown in (\ref{eq:6.32}). In this equation, $G$ represents the conductance parameter, which has a specific meaning in the context of $V_{ref}$, where the device produces the expected ohmic amount of current, equal to $V_{ref} G$.\\

\begin{figure}[htbp!] 
\centering    
\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Chapter6/Figs/h.png}
\caption[I-V sweeps of a SiOx device are presented for two regions]{I-V sweeps of a SiOx device are presented for two regions: a) low-resistance region with average resistance ranging from 284.6 $\Omega$ to 1003 $\Omega$, and b) high-resistance region with average resistance ranging from 366.2 k$\Omega$ to 1.295 M$\Omega$. Only the voltage range from 0.0 V to 0.5 V was considered for all curves. The nonlinearity parameter was calculated by dividing the current at 0.5 V by the current at 0.25 V \cite{joksas2022nonideality}.}
\label{fig:6h}
\end{figure}

\noindent Nonlinearity parameters $\gamma$ were extracted from experimental data obtained from a $SiO_x$  RRAM device. $SiO_x$ devices are capable of resistance switching, characterized by a typical I-V switching curve. To achieve a wide range of resistance states and analyze I-V nonlinearity, incremental positive sweeps were employed to gradually reset the device from the low-resistance state (LRS) to the high-resistance state (HRS). The low-resistance discrete states display greater linearity and minimal variability. Conversely, high-resistance states are more nonlinear, and their nonlinearity is less predictable.\\

\noindent The value of $V_{ref}$ from (\ref{eq:6.35}), was set to 0.25V, which is equivalent to half of the minimum switching voltage. In each case, the devices could be set to any conductance between $G_{off} = 1/R_{off}$ and $G_{on} = 1/R_{on}$. The states for the two groups were selected in such a way that the $G_{on}/G_{off}$ ratio for high-resistance devices would be slightly larger. This ensures any higher error rate in this group can be attributed to nonlinearity and its variability, rather than limited dynamic range.\\

\noindent To guarantee more robust modeling, it was deemed necessary to account for uncertainty regarding the degree of nonlinearity experienced by each device. Although high-resistance states generally exhibit greater nonlinearity, the precise degree of nonlinearity at any given resistance state may be challenging to ascertain, as evidenced by the variability observed in the I-V curves depicted in the experimental data presented in Figure \ref{fig:6h}. Consequently, for each device in either group, the parameter $\gamma$ was drawn from a truncated normal distribution. This distribution was truncated for $\gamma$ values less than 2, ensuring realistic device behavior and numerical stability of simulations. The mean $m_\gamma$ and standard deviation $s_\gamma$, referring to the underlying normal distribution prior to truncation, characterized the distribution. \\

\noindent Simulating nonidealities such as I-V nonlinearity using conventional noise injection methods that merely disturb conductance values is not possible. Consequently, a forward propagation function must be defined to reflect the nonlinear relationship between inputs and outputs.
\begin{align}
g(x, w_\pm) = \frac{\left( k_Gw_\pm + G_{off}\right) \times \left( k_V x \right)^{log_2\gamma_\pm}}{k_V} \label{eq:6.37}
\end{align}

\noindent In the proposed training function, as outlined in Equation (\ref{eq:6.33}), the aforementioned I-V nonlinearity is incorporated into the function g by combining Equations (\ref{eq:6.25}), (\ref{eq:6.34}), (\ref{eq:6.36}), using $k_V = 2V_{ref}$ in accordance with the definition in Equation (\ref{eq:6.35}), resulting in the form presented in Equation (\ref{eq:6.37}). This function is then implemented using Torch.\\

\noindent To conduct simulations involving training, it is essential to utilize an I-V model that incorporates stochasticity. As a specific instance of nonlinear behavior may be acquired during training, it is unclear how beneficial such a model would be when applied to a different set of devices. It can be seen, therefore, that the previous approach may not be sufficient, since experimental I-V measurements were used as a lookup table for computing currents of devices in certain conductance states at certain voltages. There are, however, multiple physical models that could be employed for describing non-ohmic memristor behavior. It was decided that the Poole-Frenkel conduction model \cite{joksas2022nonideality} would be most appropriate, given its plausible underlying physical mechanism for $SiO_x$  devices and its excellent fit to experimental I-V curves. Its simple analytical form also allowed for incorporating stochasticity by considering uncertainty in certain parameters.
\begin{align}
I = cV exp\left( \frac{2e}{k_BT} \sqrt{\frac{eV}{4\pi d\epsilon}} \right) \label{eq:6.38}
\end{align}

\noindent The Poole-Frenkel model postulates that the current through a device can be described by (\ref{eq:6.38}), where $I$ is the current, $V$ is the voltage, $c$ is a constant (with units of conductance), $T$ is the temperature which is 20 degrees Celsius, $d$ is the effective oxide thickness, and $\epsilon$ is the permittivity. Consequently, the parameter $c$ and the product $d\epsilon$ can be fitted.\\


\noindent To model the variability of $c$ and $d\epsilon$, an attempt was made to predict their values based on observable variables. Deviations from any constructed trend would indicate uncertainty in these quantities' values. It has been demonstrated that memristive devices can behave differently in different resistance states \cite{mehonic2015structural}, often linked to the conductance quantum$G_0 = 2e^2/h$ \cite{yi2016quantized}.\\

\noindent Variables c and $d\epsilon$ reiterate observations regarding the $SiO_x$  device's I-V behavior, assisting in assessing the Poole-Frenkel model's appropriateness. It was demonstrated that c behaves analogously to conductance, specifically as the reciprocal of resistance \cite{joksas2022memristive}. The primary distinction between lower- and higher-resistance states is that discrepancies from the trend are markedly more pronounced in the latter. In (\ref{eq:6.35}), the product $d\epsilon$ indicates the degree of nonlinearity. Since this product appears in the denominator of an exponentiated square root, a smaller $d\epsilon$ value corresponds to a more nonlinear I-V curve. In LRS, this product is significantly larger. \\

\noindent Although this parameter algebraically (\ref{eq:6.35}) can approximate linear behavior (i.e., ohmic conduction), $d\epsilon$ values are only plausible for less conductive states proximate to and below $G_0$. For higher-resistance states, this is less discernible. The $d\epsilon$ values are considerably lower for all such states, yet the trend is not only plateaued but also more erratic. This unpredictability indicates the diverse range of colors observed in the curves presented in Figure \ref{fig:6h}. While significant deviations from the trend line may limit this approach's applicability in conventional modeling scenarios, the inherent uncertainty is precisely what is required to assess ex-situ training's potential in addressing unknown behaviors.\\


\noindent Individual data points were integrated into a statistical model to generate as many data points as required. To evaluate the proposed training method's efficacy, two distinct resistance regions were considered. The low-resistance range was constructed by interpolating model parameters between the lowest resistance state and five times that resistance. The high-resistance range was constructed by interpolating model parameters between the highest resistance state and one-fifth of that resistance, ensuring consistent dynamic range.\\

\noindent The statistical model was employed to ascertain a given device's output current as follows: initial values of $c$ and $d\epsilon$ were interpolated from relevant fits using the resistance parameter $R$. Thereafter, $c$ and $d\epsilon$ were disturbed via a multivariate normal distribution, accounting for both parameter sets. The covariance matrix was determined using fit residuals, and current I was determined using (\ref{eq:6.35}).\\


\noindent As previously stated, the selection of output function $g$ in ex-situ training with linearity-nonpreserving nonidealities is contingent upon both the mapping scheme and the intrinsic nonlinearity. The combination of a linear mapping between inputs and outputs (\ref{eq:6.25}), a double weights mapping (\ref{eq:6.26}), and the relationship between current and voltage characterized by $c$ and $d\epsilon$ (\ref{eq:6.35}) allows $g$ to be expressed as follows:
\begin{align}
g\left( x, W_\pm \right) &= \frac{c\cdot (k_V x) \cdot exp\left( \frac{2e}{kbT} \sqrt{\frac{ek_Vx}{4\pi d\epsilon}} \right)}{k_G} \label{eq:6.39} \\
\begin{bmatrix} ln(c) \\ ln(d\epsilon) \end{bmatrix} &= -ln\left( k_GW_\pm + G_{off} \right)\textbf{m} + \textbf{b} + \textbf{E} \label{eq:6.40}
\end{align}

\noindent Where \textbf{m} is the slopes, \textbf{b} is the intercepts, and the error is drawn from a normal distribution $\textbf{E} \sim \mathcal{N}_2\left( 0, \Sigma \right)$, with 0 mean and standard deviation being the residual covariance matrix.

In this section, a two-layer neural network is employed to illustrate the SNN structure. As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:6i}, multilayer SNNs are configured as fully connected feedforward networks, with all neurons situated between two adjacent layers exhibiting connectivity. Both input and output neurons are characterized by multiple spikes, or spike trains.\\

    \centering    
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Chapter6/Figs/i.png}
    \caption[Abstract SNN representation for MNIST pattern recognition with input, hidden memristive layers, and output layer.]{Abstract SNN representation for MNIST pattern recognition with input, hidden memristive
    layers, and output layer.}

\noindent A two-layer memrisitive SNN (MSNN) was then designed to examine the impact of non-linear device properties on the MNIST handwritten dataset converted to a spike train.  The network was fed from the 28x28 image and trained on 60,000 samples with 10,000 samples used for testing. The network learns representative features in the input samples through updating the synaptic weights. \\


\noindent To evaluate the performance of non-ideality-aware training on complex tasks, memrisitive convolutional spiking neural networks (MCSNN) were trained with the assumption that their convolutional layers would be implemented digitally. Their fully connected layers were trained using memristive crossbar arrays that suffer from high I-V nonlinearity. \\

\noindent The MSNN model in Figure \ref{fig:6i} is fully connected and consists of a layer with 100 hidden neurons and LIF activation function, followed by another layer before the output layer with 10 neurons. The MSCNN architecture includes a convolutional layer with 12 output filters, a 5 × 5 kernel size, and an LIF activation function. This is followed by a pooling layer with a 2 × 2 pool size. \\

\noindent The architecture then includes another convolutional layer with 64 output filters, a 5 × 5 kernel size, and an LIF activation function, followed by another pooling layer with a 2 × 2 pool size. Finally, the architecture includes a memristive fully connected layer with 10 output neurons and an LIF activation function. The cross entropy loss function from Torch \cite{eshraghian2023training} automatically generates a loss at the output and handles the softmax of the output layer. \\

\noindent Despite the fact that local rules such as STDP facilitate unsupervised learning and feature extraction, classification tasks frequently benefit from global optimisation. In order to facilitate this, the present study proposes the integration of surrogate gradient descent (SGD) into the training pipeline. The weight conductance values were initially generated using a uniform random distribution. Alternatively, this initial stage can involve unsupervised pre-training of the network's convolutional layers or the first few layers in a fully connected network using (STDP). The primary objective of this stage was to allow the network to learn robust, sparse, and meaningful feature representations directly from the raw input data, without explicit labels.\\

\noindent During this stage, input data, MNIST images converted into spike trains via rate encoding or latency encoding is fed to the network. The STDP rule can be applied locally at each synapse within the pre-trained layers. Crucially, no task-specific labels are provided. The network learns to extract salient features by developing receptive fields that are highly sensitive to recurring temporal patterns in the input spike trains. For image data, this often leads to neurons specializing in detecting edges, corners, or other primitive visual features, similar to those found in the early visual cortex.\\

\noindent Following the weight condunctance intialisation or unsupervised pre-training, the network undergoes a supervised fine-tuning stage. The objective here is to adapt the pre-trained feature extractors and train the higher-level (classification) layers of the SNN to perform the specific task (e.g., image classification). This stage necessitates a global optimization approach due to the complexity of the task and the need for high accuracy, hence the use of surrogate gradient descent (SGD) within a Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) framework.\\

\noindent Unlike STDP, this stage uses a global, task-specific loss function in particular the Cross Entropy Loss, that measures the discrepancy between the SNN's output and the true labels. This loss is computed over the entire network's output activity during the simulation time window. As discussed, BPTT is applied by unrolling the SNN's computation graph over time. This allows the error signal from the global loss function to be propagated backward through the network's layers and backward through time, identifying how each weight contributed to the overall error.\\ 

\noindent Since the spiking function $s(t) = H(V(t) - V_{th})$ is non-differentiable, it was replaced with a smooth surrogate during backpropagation:
\begin{align}
    \sigma(V) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\beta(V - V_{th})}} \label{eq:6.41}
\end{align}
with $\beta$ controlling steepness. During forward passes, real spiking behavior is retained, while gradients are computed using $\frac{d\sigma}{dV}$ during the backward pass.This hybrid approach allows the training of multi-layer spiking networks with memristive synapses using gradient-based optimization while preserving local spike-based dynamics.\\

\noindent All synapses were implemented using simulated models of silicon oxide-based memristive devices, parameterized by empirical I-V characteristics and transient responses measured in physical experiments. The detailed models of memristor non-idealities such as I-V nonlinearity or variability as per Equations \ref{eq:6.37} - \ref{eq:6.40}, are incorporated into the forward and backward passes of the simulation. \\

\noindent This means the training algorithm "sees" and learns to compensate for the imperfections of the target memristive hardware. The optimized floating-point weights obtained from this software-based training can then be programmed onto the physical memristor array for deployment. The memristors act as the physical embodiment of these fine-tuned synaptic weights.\\

\noindent Energy efficiency and latency were estimated using device-level switching energy and network-level spike activity. Switching energy per pulse estimated from transient current response is $~1-10 pJ$, average spike count per inference is $~12,000$ spikes per image, and the total inference energy is $< 200 nJ$ per image for the device with peripheral overhead. \\

\noindent Compared to conventional digital implementations on CPUs/GPUs, the memristive SNN achieves over an order-of-magnitude reduction in energy consumption, particularly when operated in an event-driven, asynchronous manner. Inference latency was dictated by the spike integration window (250 ms), though this can be reduced through time-to-first-spike encoding or early-stopping criteria. Hardware implementations may further compress this timescale through massively parallel crossbar execution.

\section[Summary]{Summary}

This chapter presented a detailed study on implementing spiking neural networks (SNNs) using silicon oxide-based memristive devices, focusing on understanding and accommodating their inherent non-idealities. SNNs, which mimic the energy-efficient signal processing of biological brains, offer a promising avenue for neuromorphic computing. However, their practical realization is often limited by the physical imperfections of memristive devices, such as nonlinear conductance behavior and significant variability in device performance.\\

\noindent The study began by leveraging silicon oxide memristors, fabricated in a metal-insulator-metal (MIM) structure, to extract their specific nonlinearity parameters. These parameters were then crucial inputs in the SNN simulation models. To evaluate the effect of these non-idealities on SNN performance, two network architectures were simulated: a fully connected memristive SNN (MSNN) and a memristive convolutional SNN (MCSNN). Both networks were trained on the MNIST dataset using rate coding and a leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron model, with training incorporating backpropagation through time (BPTT) to update synaptic weights, thereby mimicking biological spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP). \\

\noindent A key finding of this research is that SNNs, even when exposed to significant I-V nonlinearities, retained high classification accuracy. Ideal SNN models achieved over 91\% accuracy, while their memristive counterparts, trained with awareness of these imperfections, still reached over 85\%. This demonstrated a notable resilience to the inherent non-idealities. Furthermore, convolutional architectures consistently outperformed fully connected ones in both ideal and non-ideal settings, suggesting their inherent structure offers a degree of robustness against device imperfections. The study also modeled conductance variation due to device-to-device variability using a lognormal distribution, adding critical realism to the training simulations.\\

\noindent In conclusion, this work provides a robust simulation framework for integrating experimental memristor data into SNN training. By accounting for non-idealities directly during the training process, it establishes a foundational approach for the future physical implementation of neuromorphic systems using silicon oxide devices. While the achieved accuracy with non-ideal devices is promising, the persistent performance gap compared to ideal implementations underscores the complexity of fully mitigating all real-world device imperfections.\\

\noindent Future research should prioritize the development of more comprehensive non-ideality models, potentially incorporating dynamic behaviors such as current transients and regulatory mechanisms akin to biological homeostasis. Validating these advanced models against a wider range of experimental data from diverse memristor technologies will be crucial for building truly robust, high-performing, and biologically plausible neuromorphic computing systems.
